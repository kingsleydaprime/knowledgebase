# Reading Papers

Reading research papers well means reading the right parts, in the right order, for what you need, instead of every word from start to finish.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 6188ab4d-5013-4f11-a5b5-24ae5457b9e7 -->

Read [[research/01-what-research-is|What Research Is]] first, and have your `my-study` folder from its project steps: this lesson adds to it.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. use the three-pass method to decide quickly which papers to read, and how deeply;
2. answer the five C's about any paper after a 10-minute flyover;
3. read a paper critically: pin its exact claim and check the evidence supports it;
4. map a field by following citations backward and forward from a survey;
5. keep a reading log and a reference manager from day one.

## What reading papers is
<!-- bite: 41448c38-4261-45d6-b96c-7d6bbd431c75 -->

A research paper is written by experts for experts, packed into a few pages, and full of background it assumes you know. Reading one like a storybook, first word to last, is slow and painful, and most of any given paper you don't need.

So researchers read in **passes**. A quick look first to decide if a paper is worth more time, a fuller read for the ones that are, and a slow, careful study for the few that matter most to their own work. This lesson teaches that method, how to read critically, how to map a whole field, and how to keep notes so you don't lose what you read. Then you'll use it to find and read the first papers for your own study.

## Where we stopped
<!-- bite: c5ebe093-017c-49a6-b49a-ea27373bd92e -->

Your journal has a chosen study and a draft contribution: for the default study, "Before this study, nobody knew which free weather app forecasts Lagos best; now we know ___." You can't be sure that's new until you know what's already been published. This lesson is how you find out.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: 266992a4-664e-4b4a-9e8b-d5d28fb73178 -->

Imagine you have to explore a huge forest to find the few trees with the best fruit. Walking every path would take years. So first you fly over it in a plane and spot the patches that look promising. Then you walk through those patches. Only at the few best trees do you climb up and look at every branch.

Reading papers works the same way. You'll glance at a hundred, properly read ten, and study three in depth. That isn't laziness; it's how researchers cope with far more papers than anyone could read.

**Where the analogy stops working.** From a plane you can see which trees have fruit. With papers, the flyover tells you what a paper *claims*, not whether the claim is right. Checking that takes the closer reading.

## Why papers are hard on purpose
<!-- bite: beb36388-a097-4e45-9c0f-5ec95d05e1ad -->

Papers are dense by design. They have strict page limits, they're written for an expert audience, and they pack months or years of work into a few pages. They assume you know the background, they put the contribution up front, and they tuck the caveats away.

They're not meant to be read in order. Once you accept "I'll read the right parts, in the right order, for what I need", reading gets much faster.

## The three passes
<!-- bite: 93e58ac4-7eca-4f44-98fd-a4e30c0605f4 -->

Here's the whole method as a funnel. Each pass is slower and deeper than the one before, and fewer papers get through to it:

![The three-pass method as a funnel. Pass 1, the flyover, takes 5 to 10 minutes and reads only the title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusion and figures; you do it for about 100 papers. Pass 2, the walk-through, takes about an hour and reads the whole paper but skips the proofs; about 10 papers get it. Pass 3, the deep dive, takes several hours and rebuilds the paper yourself; only about 3 papers get it](images/rs-three-pass.svg)

The next three sections take one pass each.

## Pass 1: the flyover
<!-- bite: 1b1fab74-c8fd-4c94-98c4-043a999c38ef -->

**5 to 10 minutes. Question: should I read this at all?**

Read only the title, the abstract, the introduction, the section headings and the conclusion. Glance at the figures and skim the reference list: do you recognise the big names? Then you should be able to answer the **five C's**:

1. **Category**: what kind of paper is it? A new method, a measurement study, a survey?
2. **Context**: what other work does it build on?
3. **Correctness**: do its assumptions look reasonable at first sight?
4. **Contributions**: what does it claim to add, in one sentence?
5. **Clarity**: is it well written?

Then decide: read further, or stop. You'll stop at pass 1 for most papers, and that's the point. Being ruthless here is what lets you cover a whole field.

## Pass 2: the walk-through
<!-- bite: bfa702df-395b-4448-89a1-ec847bf280b9 -->

**About an hour. Question: what did they actually do?**

Read the whole paper, but skip the heavy machinery: the proofs and long derivations. Look hard at the figures and tables, because in a good paper they carry the story. Can you read the main result straight off a chart? Note the terms you don't know and the references worth chasing.

Afterwards you should be able to **explain the paper to a friend, with evidence**: its contribution, its method in broad strokes, and what the results show. If you can't, either the paper is badly written or you need background first. Pass 2 is the depth you need for most papers you'll cite.

## Pass 3: the deep dive
<!-- bite: af3c5436-3d50-4f35-8ee9-9d6d8a3fc489 -->

**Several hours. Question: could I have done this, and is it right?**

Only for the few papers you'll build on, reproduce or review. You rebuild the paper in your head, or for real: re-derive the results, follow every step, and question every choice. "Would I have done it this way? What didn't they test? Where would this break?"

This is how you find a paper's hidden limitations, and those gaps often become your own research question. It's slow, and you'll do it rarely, but it's where real understanding comes from.

## Reading critically
<!-- bite: 1e2aef9a-c168-4535-9273-47cc12dce645 -->

In passes 2 and 3, don't just take the paper in: question it. Five questions:

1. **What exactly is the claim?** Say it in one sentence. It's often narrower than the title suggests.
2. **Does the evidence support that claim**, or only something weaker? Over-claiming is the most common flaw.
3. **What didn't they compare against?** Missing comparisons and hand-picked datasets hide here.
4. **What limitations did they play down?** Every method has some. Honest papers state them; for the rest, you work them out.
5. **What would break it?** The conditions where it fails are often your opportunity.

This is the same skill as peer review: you're reviewing the paper for yourself.

## Mapping a whole field
<!-- bite: 3a0c9890-a32c-4c3a-a3ce-b8d3fc91cc43 -->

To get into a new area, don't read papers at random. Build a map:

![Mapping a field from one survey paper. The survey sits in the middle. Following its references backward leads to older papers on the left; the one that almost everything cites is a seminal paper, drawn bigger. Following "cited by" forward leads to newer papers on the right, which show where the frontier is now. Time runs from left to right](images/rs-citation-map.svg)

## Mapping a field: the four moves
<!-- bite: a2894235-9abe-4aec-b040-8e5637c22150 -->

Here's how to build that map, starting from one paper:

1. **Start from a recent survey**, or a well-cited recent paper. It gives you the vocabulary and the landmarks.
2. **Follow citations backward**: the papers it cites are the foundations. The one nearly everyone cites is a **seminal** paper you must know.
3. **Follow citations forward**: who cited this paper? Google Scholar's "Cited by" and Semantic Scholar show you what came after, and where the frontier is now.
4. **Notice the names**: the same few authors and groups keep appearing. They're the people driving the area.

A few hours of this gives you a picture of the ten papers that matter and how they connect, which is worth more than fifty read in random order.

## Notes and a reference manager
<!-- bite: 4e21cf25-fdf9-40b8-98aa-c4a1932d4dd0 -->

You will forget why a paper mattered. So keep a **one-paragraph note per paper**: its contribution, its method in a line, its key result, its limitations, and how it relates to your study.

Store the PDFs in a **reference manager**. **Zotero** is free and the usual choice. It keeps every paper's details, and later it writes your reference list for you.

**Where to find papers:** **Google Scholar** and **Semantic Scholar** (search, plus who-cited-whom); **arXiv** (free preprints, especially in computer science and physics); the proceedings of your field's main conferences and journals; and **Connected Papers**, which draws a citation map for you.

## Step 1: set up your library
<!-- bite: 22fc96fb-98c5-46d1-8819-104a69cb2208 -->

Install **Zotero** (zotero.org) and its browser connector. Make a collection called `my-study`. In your `my-study` folder, create `reading-log.md`; you'll keep one entry per paper there.

## Step 2: search and flyover ten papers
<!-- bite: 748f7e09-027d-4fc9-b7b3-af975be4e37d -->

Search Google Scholar for your topic. For the weather-app study, try searches such as `weather forecast verification Nigeria`, `accuracy of weather forecasts smartphone apps`, and `numerical weather prediction West Africa evaluation`.

Do **pass 1 on ten papers**, 5 to 10 minutes each, and save the promising ones to Zotero. For each, add a short entry to `reading-log.md`: the title, the year, the five C's in a line each, and your decision (stop, or read further).

## Step 3: walk through the best three
<!-- bite: 013eaf70-e77a-4f97-a7fd-66e5fe070286 -->

Pick the **three most useful** papers and do **pass 2** on each. Under each one's entry, write the one-paragraph note: contribution, method, key result, limitations, and how it relates to your study. For the weather study, look for how they measured accuracy, which places and seasons they covered, and whether anyone has compared the free apps people actually use.

## Step 4: update your contribution
<!-- bite: a1ef271d-b802-419a-86a7-3df0f2b58df9 -->

Go back to the draft contribution in your journal. After reading, is it still new? Is it narrower than you thought, or did a paper already answer it? Rewrite it. This is normal: reading almost always sharpens your question.

## Your project so far
<!-- bite: faabf320-342e-40b8-a28b-83e305233326 -->

```text
my-study/
├── journal.md       ← + today's entry: what you searched, and your rewritten contribution
└── reading-log.md   ← ten pass-1 entries (five C's + decision), three with a pass-2 paragraph
Zotero: a "my-study" collection with the papers you kept
```

**Done when** you have ten pass-1 entries with a decision each, three pass-2 notes you could explain to a friend, the papers saved in Zotero, and a rewritten contribution in your journal that you now believe is new (or a new question, if you found yours already answered).

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: f2267f50-32e0-46cc-9f0b-f64fceda7436 -->

1. The **three-pass method** reads a paper in up to three passes, each slower and deeper: a flyover, a walk-through and a deep dive.
2. The **five C's** are the questions a flyover answers: category, context, correctness, contributions and clarity.
3. A **survey** is a paper that organises a whole field, and the best place to start mapping one.
4. A **seminal paper** is one that founded or shaped an area, and that almost everyone in it cites.
5. Following citations **backward** means reading what a paper cites; **forward** means reading what later cites it.
6. A **reference manager**, such as Zotero, stores your papers and their details and builds your reference lists.

## Common pitfalls and traps
<!-- bite: 17253474-72a0-4286-b133-1c18fb444e90 -->

1. **Reading every paper start to finish.** Most papers only deserve a flyover. Decide first.
2. **Believing the title.** The real claim is usually narrower; find it in the results.
3. **Reading at random.** Start from a survey and follow citations, or you'll miss the papers that matter.
4. **No notes.** A paper you didn't summarise is a paper you'll have to read again.

   - [x] Do a 5-to-10-minute flyover of each and decide which deserve more
   - [ ] Do a deep dive on the most-cited one
   - [ ] Read only the conclusions

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A flyover of each. Pass 1 is a filter: it lets you cover all 40 in a few hours and spend real time only on the handful worth it.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: gap id="2b7f6a90" answers="contributions|contribution;clarity" -->
   Fill in the two missing C's.

   The five C's are category, context, correctness, ___ and ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Contributions and clarity: what the paper claims to add, and whether it's well written.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="c0f93e15" -->
   A paper is titled "Deep Learning Solves Weather Forecasting". Its results test one model on one city for one rainy season. What's the most accurate statement of its claim?

   - [ ] Deep learning solves weather forecasting.
   - [x] One deep-learning model was accurate for one city in one rainy season.
   - [ ] Deep learning is better than every other method.
   - [ ] Weather forecasting is now a solved problem.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The second. The evidence supports only that narrow claim; the title over-claims. Pinning the exact claim is the first critical-reading question.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="5a19d8e3" -->
   You've found a good recent survey on your topic. How do you find where the field is heading **now**?

   - [ ] Read the survey's reference list
   - [x] Look up who has cited the survey since it came out
   - [ ] Read the oldest paper it cites
   - [ ] Do a deep dive on the survey

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Follow citations forward: the papers that cite the survey came after it and show the current frontier. Its own references point backward, to the foundations.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="f6820b4a" -->
   Which paper deserves a pass-3 deep dive?

   - [ ] Every paper in your reading log
   - [ ] Any paper with an interesting title
   - [x] The paper whose method you'll copy and build on in your own study
   - [ ] The shortest paper you found

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The one you'll build on. A deep dive takes hours, so it's only for papers you'll reproduce, extend or review, where you need to know every step is right.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="3d5e7c81" -->
   Name three things you should write in your one-paragraph note on a paper, and say why the note matters.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Any three of: its contribution, its method in a line, its key result, its limitations, and how it relates to your work. It matters because you will forget why a paper mattered; a searchable set of your own summaries saves rereading, and later becomes your literature review.

   </details>

## Before moving on
<!-- bite: 76deb0d2-e183-480f-a604-529c786cce24 -->

You're done when you can flyover a paper in ten minutes and answer the five C's, explain a pass-2 paper to someone else, say a paper's exact claim and whether its evidence supports it, map a field from a survey, and your reading log and Zotero library exist for your study.

Recap: read in three passes (flyover, walk-through, deep dive), so you can cover many papers and go deep on few. Read critically: the exact claim, the evidence, the missing comparisons, the hidden limitations. Map a field from a survey, backward to the foundations and forward to the frontier. Note every paper from day one.
