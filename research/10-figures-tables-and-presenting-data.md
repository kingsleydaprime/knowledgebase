# Figures, Tables & Presenting Data

**[reference]** · **⏳ outline — deep note in progress** (part of the research-course build).

Readers look at your **figures before they read your words** — often a figure and the abstract are *all* they read. A great figure can carry the whole contribution; a bad one can sink a strong paper. This is how to make visuals that argue for you, honestly.

## Will cover
- **The "figure-first" reality** — design the key result as a figure a skimmer can understand in 10 seconds; the caption should stand alone.
- **Figures vs tables** — figures for trends/comparisons/relationships, tables for exact numbers and many-way comparisons; don't make readers do the trend-spotting a chart would do for them.
- **The anatomy of a good scientific figure** — clear axes + units, legible fonts, a self-contained caption, error bars/variance shown, no chartjunk.
- **Honest visualization** — truncated axes, misleading scales, cherry-picked ranges — the ways charts lie, and why it's an [[research/07-research-ethics-and-integrity|integrity]] issue, not just aesthetics.
- **Comparison discipline** — same axes/scale across compared plots, consistent colors for the same method throughout the paper.
- **Accessibility** — colorblind-safe palettes, readable in grayscale (many print your paper B&W), high-DPI/vector (PDF/SVG) not blurry PNGs.
- **Tooling** — matplotlib/seaborn, ggplot, TikZ/PGFPlots for LaTeX-native figures; the `dataviz` principles apply.
