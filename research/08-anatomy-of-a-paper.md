# The Anatomy of a Paper

A research paper is a short, structured argument for one contribution, laid out in standard sections so any reader knows where to find what they need.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 2b35a1d6-4571-4e6c-ad8b-c089cb97d61a -->

Read [[research/07-research-ethics-and-integrity|Research Ethics and Integrity]] first, and have your full study folder: `journal.md`, `lit-review.md`, `plan.md`, `results.md` and `credits.md`. This lesson opens part B of the subject, writing it up, and turns that folder into the first draft of a paper.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. explain why a paper is an argument, not a diary;
2. say what job each standard section does;
3. see the hourglass shape a paper follows;
4. write a paper's sections in a sensible order, not top to bottom;
5. lead with the point at every level, from paper to paragraph.

## What a paper is
<!-- bite: ac3b5d53-ece3-4e9d-b589-3d7d75a84054 -->

You've done the research. Now it has to reach other people, and for research that means a **paper**: usually 4 to 12 pages that argue for one contribution and give the evidence.

The surprise for most new writers is that a paper isn't a report of what you did, in the order you did it. It's a **case** you make to a sceptical expert, and it uses the same sections in the same order as almost every other paper, so the reader can find what they need fast. This lesson is about those two ideas: the argument, and the sections.

## Where we stopped
<!-- bite: 3302d3b8-c72d-4c5d-a913-9b37a51f0dd7 -->

Your study folder holds everything a paper needs: your contribution sentence in `journal.md`, the gap in `lit-review.md`, the method and ethics in `plan.md`, the honest claims in `results.md`, and the credits in `credits.md`. Now you'll set up `paper.md`, map each section to the file that feeds it, and draft the two sections that are easiest to write first.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: e180e4dd-01b9-468b-bc42-2a27143e90bf -->

A paper isn't a diary of *"here's everything I did all month, in order"*. It's like a lawyer speaking to a jury: *"Here's my claim. Here's why you should care. Here's my evidence. So believe me."*

The lawyer doesn't mention the dead ends, the tea breaks, or the week spent finding the right witness: only what builds the case. And trials follow a standard order (opening, evidence, closing) so everyone knows what's coming. Papers do too.

**Where the analogy stops working.** A lawyer argues one side no matter what. A researcher argues only for what the evidence supports, and says out loud where it's weak.

## An argument, not a diary
<!-- bite: ca9d249c-6344-42ff-8128-3048bfd93d20 -->

The biggest shift for a new writer: **you're not reporting your journey, you're convincing a sceptical expert of one contribution.** Three things follow.

1. **The order follows the argument, not the calendar.** You found the 7 am gauge problem during the pilot; in the paper it's simply part of the method.
2. **Cut what doesn't serve the argument.** The app you dropped on day two because it needed a login gets one line at most.
3. **One paper, one contribution.** If you have three separate ideas, you probably have three papers.

So start with a one-sentence **storyline**: *"This study shows that ___."* Every section then serves that sentence.

## The hourglass
<!-- bite: 8b83ee96-ef89-4166-a832-9ab03163bf71 -->

Most papers follow the same shape: wide at the start, narrow in the middle, wide again at the end.

![The hourglass shape of a paper. It starts wide, in the introduction, with the broad problem everyone cares about (people plan their day around free weather apps), narrows to the gap (nobody has checked them in Lagos) and to this paper's contribution. The narrow middle is the method and results: exactly what was done and found, three apps, 28 days, one part of Lagos. It widens again in the discussion and conclusion: what this means for people choosing an app, what it does not show, and what to study next](images/rs-hourglass.svg)

The introduction starts with a problem anyone can care about and narrows to exactly what this paper adds. The middle is precise and narrow: what was done and found, nothing more. The end widens again: what it means, where it stops, and what comes next.

## Title and abstract
<!-- bite: 83974c15-02b9-4b08-8c59-f989c3566578 -->

Each section has one job. The first two are the most read:

1. **Title.** Say what the paper is actually about, in words people would search for. Not clever, not vague. *"How accurate are free weather apps in Lagos? A four-week comparison with measured rain and temperature."*
2. **Abstract** (150 to 250 words). A mini-paper that stands alone, because many people read only this. Five moves, roughly a sentence each: **the problem, the gap, what you did, the key result, and why it matters.** No "in this paper we will discuss"; give the actual result.

## An abstract, worked
<!-- bite: 206c3a90-dd31-497f-89e0-cad6204d8d43 -->

Here's an abstract for the weather study, using the made-up example numbers from lessons 05 and 06. Each sentence does one of the five moves:

> **(Problem and gap)** Many people in Lagos decide whether to carry an umbrella, open a market stall or water a farm using free weather apps, but no published study has checked how often these apps are right there. **(What we did)** Over 28 rainy-season days in Yaba, Lagos, we recorded three free apps' next-day forecasts each evening and compared them with a home rain gauge and thermometer. **(Key result)** The best app was right about rain on 22 days (79%), against 17 (61%) for simply guessing that tomorrow will be like today, and was off by 0.7 °C on an average day; another did worse than always predicting rain. **(How sure)** The best app's lead over the guess was not statistically significant over four weeks (sign test, p ≈ 0.09). **(Why it matters)** Free apps differ widely in Lagos, and a longer study is needed to show whether even the best beats a simple guess.

Notice it reports the null result honestly, in one sentence, and still says something useful.

## The introduction
<!-- bite: e7571d6c-cf59-4629-8521-c9b0a7a238be -->

If the abstract earns a read, the introduction earns the rest of the paper. By the end of page one, the reader should know what you did and why it matters. It follows the top of the hourglass, in six moves:

1. **The problem**, in plain terms.
2. **Why it matters**: who cares, and what's at stake.
3. **The gap**: what's missing, your literature review in two or three sentences.
4. **What this paper does**, in a sentence or two.
5. **The contributions**, often as a numbered list: *"This paper contributes: (1) …, (2) …"*. Reviewers love it, because it tells them exactly what to judge.
6. **A roadmap**: one line on how the rest of the paper is laid out.

## Related work and method
<!-- bite: 3bcd0b7c-4ce6-480e-88e4-99b3a898816c -->

3. **Related work** shows you know the field and how your study differs. It's your literature review, tightened: organised by theme, ending in how this paper fills the gap. Many papers put it after the introduction; some put it near the end, so a wall of citations doesn't stall the reader before they know your idea.
4. **Method** says what you did, precisely enough that a careful stranger could repeat it. It's written in the past tense, in a clean logical order, not the messy order you discovered things in. It includes the ethics statement, and any change you made after the pilot.

## Results, discussion, conclusion
<!-- bite: 1a490f26-9ef1-4cda-b9e6-c6ffda222c6d -->

5. **Results** give the evidence: first the setup (baselines, metrics, how many days), then the findings, mostly in figures and tables. Answer the questions you posed in the introduction, in order. Report honestly, with the test and effect size.
6. **Discussion** says what the results mean: why they might have come out this way, and their **limitations** and threats to validity. Naming your own limits reads as strength, and it answers the reviewer before they ask.
7. **Conclusion** restates what you *showed*, not what you "discussed", and points to future work. Keep it short.
8. **Acknowledgements and references** close the paper: everyone who helped, and every source, checked.

## The order to write it in
<!-- bite: eaf601e8-b3de-4da6-baee-218ab8e1b922 -->

You read a paper top to bottom, but you don't write it that way. Start with the parts you know best, the middle of the hourglass, and finish with the parts that summarise everything else:

![The sections of a paper, top to bottom, each with the file in your study folder that feeds it and the order to write it in. Title: written last, ninth. Abstract: the whole paper shrunk, eighth. Introduction: from journal.md and the gap in lit-review.md, seventh. Related work: from lit-review.md, fourth. Method: from the Method and Ethics parts of plan.md, second. Results: from results.md and the analysis output, first. Discussion: from the boring explanations and threats in results.md, third. Conclusion: from the claims in results.md, sixth. Acknowledgements from credits.md and References from the checked lit-review.md, both fifth. You read a paper top to bottom but write it from the middle out](images/rs-paper-map.svg)

Results come first because they're the evidence the whole paper argues from. The abstract and title come last, because you can't shrink a paper you haven't written. Almost every section already has a file feeding it.

## Point first, everywhere
<!-- bite: 9ae615dc-e9c8-4d0c-88fc-6bfc5f9033c5 -->

At every level, **lead with the point, then support it.** The abstract gives the result up front. Each section's first lines say what it will show. Each paragraph's first sentence is its claim, and the rest backs it up.

A good test: read only the first sentence of every paragraph. If you still get the argument, the paper is well built. Busy readers skim exactly like that. The next lesson, [[research/09-scientific-writing-craft|Scientific Writing Craft]], works on this down to the sentence.

## Step 1: write your storyline
<!-- bite: 6e9d7629-52ad-404a-9f86-d9355744ccc0 -->

In `journal.md`, write one sentence: *"This study shows that ___."* Use your actual results, not what you hoped for. If your main result is a null result, say that: *"This study shows that over four weeks, no free app clearly beat a simple guess at Lagos rain."*

## Step 2: build the skeleton
<!-- bite: 8e484970-5c50-4522-82e5-e3d280720ca9 -->

Create `paper.md` with every section as a heading. Next to each, a comment says its job and the file that feeds it; keep the comments until the section is written:

```markdown
# Title                <!-- write last -->
## Abstract            <!-- the whole paper in 5 moves; write last -->
## 1. Introduction     <!-- problem, why it matters, gap, what we did, contributions, roadmap -->
## 2. Related work     <!-- lit-review.md, tightened, ending in the gap -->
## 3. Method           <!-- plan.md Method + Ethics, past tense, with pilot changes -->
## 4. Results          <!-- results.md: setup, table, tests, effect sizes -->
## 5. Discussion       <!-- what it means, limitations, threats to validity -->
## 6. Conclusion       <!-- what we showed, future work -->
## Acknowledgements    <!-- credits.md -->
## References          <!-- every checked source from lit-review.md -->
```

## Step 3: draft the results
<!-- bite: 365f22df-9227-4ff1-9135-557e3aaa9d87 -->

Fill **4. Results** from `results.md`. Start with the setup in two or three sentences (how many days, which baselines, which metrics). Then the table, then the test for each app, then the narrowest-claim sentences with their effect sizes. Answer your descriptive question first, then your comparative one.

Don't polish yet. A rough, complete draft is the goal; the next lesson is about making it read well.

## Step 4: draft the method
<!-- bite: 9a53339e-378d-47e8-8c21-787445457021 -->

Fill **3. Method** from `plan.md`, in the past tense: the apps and their versions, the location setting, the reading time and shuffled order, how the gauge and thermometer were made, placed and read, the rules for "said rain", "rained" and the error, the dates, and what changed after the pilot. End with a short ethics statement from your Ethics section.

Check it against one question: could a careful stranger repeat the study from this section alone?

## Step 5: outline the introduction
<!-- bite: 7c43f32b-bf23-4cc1-b2a5-4ec80f51845e -->

Under **1. Introduction**, write the six moves as six bullet points, one line each. Then write your **contributions** as a numbered list. For the weather study, for example:

1. the first published comparison of free weather apps' next-day forecasts with measured weather in Lagos;
2. a cheap method anyone can repeat with a tin, a thermometer and four weeks, with the data and script released;
3. evidence that the apps differ widely, with one doing worse than always predicting rain.

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: 99e64993-572e-41eb-9a49-0de04c2c30b3 -->

1. A **paper** is a short, structured argument for one contribution, with its evidence.
2. A **storyline** is the one sentence the whole paper argues: *"This study shows that ___."*
3. The **hourglass** is a paper's shape: broad problem, narrow method and results, broad meaning.
4. An **abstract** is a stand-alone summary in five moves: problem, gap, what you did, key result, why it matters.
5. A **contributions list** states exactly what the paper adds, usually numbered, in the introduction.
6. **Limitations** are the ways a study's result is narrower or weaker than it might seem; the discussion states them.

## Common pitfalls and traps
<!-- bite: 0521c590-012b-4efe-b5e3-c0b34b28a7e6 -->

1. **The diary.** Telling the story in the order it happened instead of the order of the argument.
2. **A vague abstract.** "Results are presented and discussed" instead of the actual result.
3. **Hidden contributions.** A reader who finishes page one without knowing what's new won't read page two.
4. **Starting with the title and abstract.** You can't summarise what you haven't written.
5. **Three ideas in one paper.** None gets argued properly.
6. **No limitations.** The reviewer will find them anyway, and trust you less.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 49107650-6f9e-4613-aa8e-97240ff46dfe -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="d6a2f0b8" -->
   Which sentence would you cut from the method section of a paper on drying cassava with a solar dryer?

   - [ ] "Each batch was 5 kg of cassava chips, 3 mm thick."
   - [ ] "Moisture was measured every two hours with a grain moisture meter."
   - [x] "At first we tried a black plastic cover, which melted on the second day, so we went to the market for glass."
   - [ ] "The dryer faced south at an angle of 15 degrees."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The melted cover is part of the journey, not the argument. The method says what was finally done; if the first cover's failure teaches something, it gets one line at most.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="a83c5e19" -->
   Which is the strongest abstract sentence for reporting the key result?

   - [ ] "The results of the experiments are presented and discussed."
   - [ ] "The new stove performed well in our tests."
   - [x] "The new stove boiled 2 litres of water in 9 minutes on average, against 14 for a traditional three-stone fire, using 40% less firewood."
   - [ ] "Stoves are important for millions of families."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The third gives the actual result, with numbers and a comparison. The first and second are empty; the fourth is background, not a result.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="f19e6b42" -->
   In what order does this lesson recommend writing a paper's sections?

   - [ ] Title, abstract, introduction, then the rest in order
   - [x] Results and method first; introduction, abstract and title last
   - [ ] Conclusion first, then work backward
   - [ ] Related work first, then everything else in order

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Results and method first, because you know them best and they're the evidence; the introduction, abstract and title last, because they summarise a paper that must already exist.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: gap id="7c4b0e95" answers="gap;result" -->
   The five moves of an abstract are: the problem, the ___, what you did, the key ___, and why it matters.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The gap and the key result.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="2b6f8d03" -->
   A student's paper reports three separate ideas: a new phone-battery saver, a survey of how students charge phones, and a cheaper charging cable. What's the best advice?

   - [ ] Put all three in one paper to make it look bigger
   - [x] Split them: one paper, one contribution
   - [ ] Drop the survey, because surveys aren't research
   - [ ] Put the three ideas in the conclusion

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   One paper, one contribution. Three ideas in one paper means none of them gets a full argument and full evidence.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="c0e7a548" -->
   A study measured how much a ₦5,000 water filter reduces bacteria in well water in Ogun State, and found an 85% reduction across 30 wells. Write its storyline sentence and two numbered contributions.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For example. Storyline: "This study shows that a ₦5,000 filter cut bacteria in Ogun State well water by 85% on average across 30 wells." Contributions: (1) the first field test of this low-cost filter on real well water in Ogun State, across 30 wells; (2) a simple testing method, with its data released, that health workers could repeat in other areas.

   </details>

## Before moving on
<!-- bite: 06c0272a-c304-4ab6-9eef-a03d073449e3 -->

You're done when you can explain why a paper is an argument, give the job of each section, sketch the hourglass, say the order to write in, and test a draft by reading first sentences; and your `paper.md` has its skeleton, drafted method and results, and an introduction outline with contributions.

Recap: a paper argues for one contribution, in the order of the argument, not the order of events. It's an hourglass: broad problem, narrow method and results, broad meaning. Each section has one job. Write from the middle out, results first, title last, and lead with the point at every level.
