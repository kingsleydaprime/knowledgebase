# The Anatomy of a Paper

**[reference]** · **⏳ outline — deep note in progress** (part of the research-course build). Opens Part B (writing). Built around Simon Peyton Jones' "How to Write a Great Research Paper."

The standard sections of a paper, what each one must *do*, **and the argument that runs underneath them** — because a paper isn't a lab report, it's a **persuasive case** for one claim.

## Will cover
- **The paper is an argument, not a chronology** — you're not narrating "what I did all summer"; you're making the reader believe *one contribution*. Structure serves the argument.
- **Section by section, and its job:**
  - **Title & Abstract** — the abstract is a mini-paper (problem → gap → what you did → key result); most people read *only* this.
  - **Introduction** — the most important section: the problem, why it matters, the gap, your contribution stated explicitly (often a bulleted "our contributions"), and a roadmap. SPJ's rule: *state your contributions in the intro, concretely.*
  - **Related Work** — situate, don't list ([[research/03-finding-a-problem-and-literature-review|lit-review]] discipline); often placed later so it doesn't block the reader early.
  - **Method / Approach** — what you did, precisely enough to reproduce.
  - **Experiments / Results** — the evidence; setup, then findings ([[research/10-figures-tables-and-presenting-data|figures/tables]] carry it).
  - **Discussion** — what it means, limitations (stating them builds trust), threats to validity.
  - **Conclusion & Future Work** — recap the contribution; open questions (a gift to future-you and others).
- **Write in a non-linear order** — often method/results first, intro and abstract *last*.
- **The "reverse pyramid"** — lead with the point everywhere (abstract, each section, each paragraph).
