# Presenting & Defending

**[reference]** · **⏳ outline — deep note in progress** (part of the research-course build). Closes the course.

A paper is written; now you have to **stand up and tell people about it** — a talk, a poster, or a thesis defense. Different skill from writing: writing is for careful solo readers; speaking is for a live audience who can't rewind.

## Will cover
- **Talk ≠ paper** — a talk conveys the *one* idea and makes people want to read the paper; it is *not* the paper read aloud. Ruthless selection; motivation-heavy.
- **Structure of a research talk** — hook/motivation → problem → your idea (the core, most time here) → key results → takeaway; the "tell them what you'll tell them" arc.
- **Slide craft** — one idea per slide, visuals over text, big fonts, no wall-of-bullets, [[research/10-figures-tables-and-presenting-data|figures]] that read from the back row.
- **Delivery** — pace, signposting, handling nerves, timing/practice, reading the room.
- **Posters** — the different medium: a poster is a *conversation starter*, not a wall of text; the 30-second pitch you repeat all session.
- **Q&A** — the part people fear: listen fully, answer honestly ("I don't know" is fine), don't get defensive, treat hard questions as gifts.
- **The thesis defense** — the high-stakes version: knowing your work cold, defending choices, engaging critique gracefully; what committees actually probe.
- **Beyond the talk** — the elevator pitch, explaining your work to non-experts, science communication.
