# Scientific Writing Craft

Scientific writing craft is the skill of turning a correct but hard-to-read draft into one a reader follows the first time.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: b7319762-4e34-430d-a3fb-bfcdab79c2d2 -->

Read [[research/08-anatomy-of-a-paper|The Anatomy of a Paper]] first, and have your `paper.md` with its skeleton, rough Method and Results, and an introduction outline with contributions. This lesson finishes the draft and then revises it until it reads clearly.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. write for a reader's time, not to sound clever;
2. build paragraphs that open with their point;
3. make claims precise, with one name for each thing;
4. write tight sentences: active voice, strong verbs, no filler;
5. make sentences connect, old information before new;
6. revise in passes: reverse outline, filler hunt, read aloud, cut, outside reader.

## What writing craft is
<!-- bite: f26db6e6-ca59-4cb3-b0e3-0e97b05c9825 -->

Your draft is probably correct and hard to read. That's normal: every first draft is. Craft is what you do next, sentence by sentence, so the reader gets your idea with the least effort.

Good scientific writing isn't fancy. It's the opposite: **a hard idea made easy to follow.** If a reader has to read a sentence twice, the writer failed, not the reader. This lesson gives you the habits that fix that, then you'll use them on your own paper.

## Where we stopped
<!-- bite: afaf1809-efab-45d7-b600-994a3ba0ec16 -->

`paper.md` has its skeleton, a rough Method and Results, and a six-bullet introduction outline with numbered contributions. Now you'll write the remaining sections (discussion, conclusion, introduction, related work, then the abstract and title) and revise the whole paper.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: 7c8831bf-0e80-44d3-8b11-98fdb13c60b8 -->

Think of the best teacher you ever had. They didn't use big words to sound clever. They explained hard things so plainly that you barely noticed you were learning.

Good scientific writing works like that teacher. Your reader is busy and has many other papers to read. If they have to read a sentence twice, that's your mistake, not theirs. So the whole craft is one rule: **be understood with the least effort from the reader.**

**Where the analogy stops working.** A teacher sees your face and explains again when you look lost. A paper can't. It has to be clear the first time, which is why writers revise so much.

## Clarity over cleverness
<!-- bite: e74cd0c1-1c72-4c0a-9def-c64f558a90f9 -->

A paper exists to move an idea from your head into the reader's, not to show your vocabulary. Big words, long sentences and dense jargon *feel* scholarly, and every one is a place the reader can stumble.

Write for a smart reader who isn't an expert in your exact topic and is reading quickly. For the weather study, imagine a sharp engineer who has never heard of a hit rate. When in doubt, choose the plainer word and the shorter sentence: "use", not "utilise"; "because", not "owing to the fact that".

## Point first, in every paragraph
<!-- bite: 1d3d7626-e301-4c22-81b9-8834f8ef59db -->

The last lesson said: lead with the point at every level. In a paragraph, that means two rules.

1. **One idea per paragraph**, and the **first sentence states it**. This is the **topic sentence**. The rest of the paragraph supports that one idea.
2. If a paragraph has two ideas, split it. If you can't write its topic sentence, you don't yet know what the paragraph is about.

A reader who skims only the first sentence of each paragraph should still follow your argument. You'll test exactly that in the revision steps.

## Precision
<!-- bite: e551b354-7130-4ac9-af96-4121e4dd8e96 -->

Vague claims are weak, and worse, nobody can check them.

1. **Concrete beats vague.** "Right on 22 of 28 days", not "performed well". "Off by 0.7 °C on an average day", not "fairly accurate".
2. **One name for each thing.** Define a term once, then use it every time. If you call app B "the application", then "the tool", then "the program", a reader wonders if you mean three things.
3. **One meaning for each symbol.** Introduce it before you use it, and don't reuse it for something else.

## Tight sentences
<!-- bite: a5024a55-61fc-42b6-b76e-b6d96ebd50e2 -->

1. **Active voice and strong verbs.** "We measured the rain each morning", not "the rain was measured each morning". "The app predicts", not "the app is able to make a prediction of". Some fields still prefer the passive in the method; check papers in your venue, but default to active.
2. **Cut filler.** "In order to" becomes "to". "Due to the fact that" becomes "because". Delete "it is worth noting that", "very", "quite" and "somewhat": they add words and remove confidence.
3. **Short sentences for hard ideas.** When the idea is complex, keep the grammar simple.
4. **Parallel lists.** Items in a list share a form: "we recorded, scored and tested", not "we recorded, scored and the testing".

Before, 59 words: it opens with filler (it is worth noting that, due to the fact that), hides who did what in the passive, calls app B three different names (the application, the tool, the program), and buries the actual result, 22 of 28 days, at the very end without the baseline's score. ## Flow: old before new
<!-- bite: eb5ed76c-4958-445f-a316-69fc5bf5bf09 -->

Clear sentences can still read badly if they don't connect. The strongest fix is to **start each sentence with something the reader already knows, and end it with something new**:

![Old information before new. Four sentences about the rain gauge, each starting with what the previous one ended on. Sentence one ends with a straight-sided tin; sentence two starts with the tin and ends with roofs and trees; sentence three starts with roofs and trees and ends with catching less rain; sentence four starts with less rain. Arrows link each new idea at the end of a sentence to the start of the next, which is why the paragraph reads smoothly](images/rs-old-new.svg)

Each sentence picks up where the last one left off, so the reader never has to jump. Two more habits help:

1. **Name the link.** Use words that say how two sentences relate: "however" (a contrast), "so" (a result), "for example" (an instance).
2. **Forecast and recap** at the start and end of sections, so readers always know where they are.

## Mistakes reviewers groan at
<!-- bite: 0f4c54a1-0798-4c5e-8f3e-e14a2f957657 -->

1. **A buried contribution**: the reader can't find what's new.
2. **Undefined jargon and acronyms**: define each on first use.
3. **Passive fog**: endless "it was found that", hiding who did what.
4. **Throat-clearing openings**: "Since the dawn of time, humans have watched the sky…". Get to the point.
5. **A table with no words**: results dumped without saying what they show.
6. **Three names for one thing.**
7. **Overclaiming**: writing claims the evidence can't support.

## Writing is rewriting
<!-- bite: 7454f558-d14a-45c9-9983-61dac7d743ca -->

Nobody writes a good first draft. **A first draft's only job is to exist**; the writing gets good in revision. Revise in passes, each looking for one thing:

1. **Structure first.** Make a **reverse outline**: list the first sentence of every paragraph. If the list isn't a clear argument, fix the order before touching any sentence.
2. **Filler and fog.** Hunt down the filler phrases and passive "it was found that"s.
3. **Read it aloud.** Your ear catches clumsy sentences your eye slides over.
4. **Cut about 10%.** Almost every draft is too long, and shorter is clearer.
5. **An outside reader.** Someone not in your head shows you exactly where the writing fails.

## A reverse outline, worked
<!-- bite: 883c2b0a-a3c6-4e9b-8c48-24c4ddf788e6 -->

Here's a reverse outline of a draft discussion for the weather paper:

![A reverse outline of a discussion section. Each paragraph is reduced to its first sentence, listed in order: we measured three apps for 28 days; the best app beat a simple guess but not by more than luck; the gauge was read at 7 am so some rain landed on the wrong day; rain is very important in Lagos for many reasons; a longer study would settle the question. Four of them form an argument, but the fourth is general background that does not move the argument on, so it is marked to cut or rewrite](images/rs-reverse-outline.svg)

Four first sentences make an argument. Paragraph 4 doesn't: "rain is very important" is background that belongs in the introduction, if anywhere. The fix is structural (move or cut the paragraph), and no amount of polishing its sentences would have found it.

## Writing in a second language
<!-- bite: bf5c5e6e-9906-472e-afe9-841cc3a770ab -->

Most of the world's researchers write papers in a second language, and clear scientific English is a skill you learn, not a talent you're born with. Short, simple sentences are clearer *and* harder to get wrong.

Keep a file of well-built sentences from good papers in your area, and borrow their patterns (not their words). Grammar tools and AI editing can help with polish, if you disclose them as [[research/07-research-ethics-and-integrity|the ethics lesson]] says, but you own every claim. A clear, simple paper beats a fancy, tangled one every time.

## Step 1: write the discussion and conclusion
<!-- bite: 80187053-f84f-4f9f-a01a-6d29eb9cbda2 -->

Fill **5. Discussion** from `results.md`: what the results mean, why they might have come out this way (labelled as a guess), and a **Limitations** paragraph with your threats to validity and the boring explanations you checked. Then fill **6. Conclusion** in three to five sentences: what you showed, and the most useful next study.

## Step 2: write the introduction and related work
<!-- bite: 5334ac92-5911-439b-a4af-67003b875cb4 -->

Turn your six-bullet outline into **1. Introduction**: one short paragraph per move, with the numbered contributions list kept as a list. Then tighten `lit-review.md` into **2. Related work**, ending with the gap your study fills. Check that the introduction promises only what Results delivers.

## Step 3: write the abstract and title
<!-- bite: 66b79ca8-8078-4375-80ee-e3106ce209e4 -->

Now the paper exists, shrink it. Write the **abstract** in five moves (problem and gap, what you did, key result with numbers, how sure, why it matters), under 250 words. Then write a **title** that says what the paper is about in words people would search for.

## Step 4: revise in passes
<!-- bite: 4df9e7eb-bc86-4a7e-b687-32b60baf7bc8 -->

Do each pass separately:

1. **Reverse outline.** List the first sentence of every paragraph in a scratch file. Fix the structure until the list is an argument.
2. **Filler hunt.** In a terminal in your study folder, list the usual suspects with their line numbers, then fix each one:

   ```bash
   grep -n -i -E "in order to|due to the fact|it is worth noting|it should be noted|was found|very|quite|somewhat" paper.md
   ```

3. **One name each.** List the names you use for each app, each metric and each baseline. Keep one per thing.
4. **Read it aloud**, and fix every sentence you stumble on.
5. **Cut 10%.** Count words with `wc -w paper.md` before and after, and keep cutting until you've lost a tenth.

## Step 5: get one outside reader
<!-- bite: 06098ec7-1181-4d1e-ab61-a942e082606e -->

Give the paper to one person outside your field, and ask them to mark every place they had to read twice or got lost. Don't explain anything while they read: the paper has to work without you. Fix each mark, then commit.

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: 02c9f20a-9255-420b-9abd-4cb9608309fd -->

1. A **topic sentence** is a paragraph's first sentence, stating its one idea.
2. **Active voice** puts the doer first ("we measured the rain"); **passive voice** hides or delays it ("the rain was measured").
3. **Filler** is words that add length but no meaning, like "it is worth noting that".
4. **Old before new** means starting a sentence with something the reader already knows and ending it with something new.
5. A **reverse outline** is the list of a draft's topic sentences, used to check its structure.
6. **Revising in passes** means going through a draft several times, looking for one kind of problem each time.

## Common pitfalls and traps
<!-- bite: 014f6a71-3c09-480d-97c0-76e74c2f0cae -->

1. **Polishing too early.** Perfecting sentence one before the draft exists.
2. **Fixing sentences when the structure is broken.** Reverse-outline first.
3. **Sounding clever.** Long words and long sentences lose readers.
4. **Synonyms for variety.** In a paper, a new word suggests a new thing.
5. **Explaining instead of fixing.** If a reader got lost, change the text; you won't be there to explain.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: f386fe3d-c1eb-438e-b8bb-f0ad5acc6111 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="b4e19c70" -->
   Which is the best revision of: "In order to assess the durability of the bricks, a series of tests was carried out by the team"?

   - [ ] "In order to assess durability, tests were carried out on the bricks by the team."
   - [x] "To test how durable the bricks were, we dropped each one from 1 m, ten times."
   - [ ] "A series of tests of brick durability was conducted."
   - [ ] "It is worth noting that the team tested the bricks' durability."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The second: active voice, no "in order to", and it says exactly what was done. The others keep the filler or the passive, and none says what the test was.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="f702a8d3" -->
   A paper calls the same device "the sensor", "the detector" and "the probe" in one section. Why is that a problem?

   - [ ] It makes the paper too long
   - [x] Readers may think the paper is describing three different devices
   - [ ] Only "sensor" is a scientific word
   - [ ] It breaks the active-voice rule

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   In scientific writing, a new word suggests a new thing. Pick one name, define it, and use it every time.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="2e6c94b1" -->
   Which pair of sentences follows "old before new"?

   - [ ] "Cassava was dried in a solar dryer. Moisture falls fastest at midday."
   - [x] "We dried the cassava in a solar dryer. The dryer reached 55 °C by midday."
   - [ ] "The cassava was dried. Readings were taken. The dryer was hot."
   - [ ] "Midday is hottest. We used cassava."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The second: the second sentence starts with "the dryer", which the first sentence just introduced, then adds something new.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="c38d0f56" -->
   A reverse outline of a methods section gives: "We chose 20 farms." / "Each farm got both seed types." / "Maize is Nigeria's most grown cereal." / "We measured the yield at harvest." What should the writer do?

   - [ ] Polish the third paragraph's sentences
   - [x] Move or cut the third paragraph: it's background, not method
   - [ ] Add more paragraphs about maize
   - [ ] Nothing, as all four are true

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The third first sentence doesn't move the method on. It's background that belongs in the introduction, if anywhere. That's a structural fix, found only by looking at the outline.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: gap id="91a5e2f7" answers="10" -->
   A first full draft is 4,000 words. The lesson suggests cutting about ___ % on a revision pass, which would leave about 3,600 words.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   About 10%: 4,000 − 400 = 3,600 words.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="e5b07d42" -->
   Rewrite this sentence to be clear and concrete: "It was observed that the new irrigation system resulted in a somewhat significant improvement in the yield of tomatoes on the farm."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For example: "With the new drip irrigation, the farm's tomato yield rose from 8 to 11 tonnes per hectare." It names the system, gives the numbers, uses the active voice, and drops "it was observed that" and "somewhat significant" (which is vague, and misuses "significant").

   </details>

## Before moving on
<!-- bite: a2697cd6-a64d-4b4b-b862-7b30c711d18a -->

You're done when you can write a paragraph that opens with its point, make a claim precise, cut filler, chain sentences old before new, and revise in passes; and your `paper.md` is a complete, revised draft that one outside reader has read.

Recap: write to be understood with the least effort, not to impress. One idea per paragraph, point first. Be concrete, and give each thing one name. Use active voice and strong verbs, and cut the filler. Start each sentence with what the reader knows. Then revise in passes: structure first with a reverse outline, then filler, then read aloud, then cut, then an outside reader.
