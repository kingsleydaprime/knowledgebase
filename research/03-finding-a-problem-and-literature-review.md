# Finding a Problem and the Literature Review

Finding a problem means choosing a question worth a study, and the literature review is the argument that shows nobody has answered it yet.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 8d36e0f8-7df6-4124-8767-5c3e8fb85641 -->

Read [[research/02-reading-papers|Reading Papers]] first, and have your `reading-log.md` from its steps: this lesson turns those notes into your literature review.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. say what makes a research question good: novel, significant and feasible;
2. find candidate questions in other people's papers;
3. write a literature review as an argument, organised by theme, that ends in a gap;
4. scope a question with the FINER checklist and the "so what?" test;
5. keep your reading current with alerts.

## What finding a problem is
<!-- bite: c7a63a8b-0afb-4f55-b53e-65e344ef97fd -->

Everyone thinks the hard part of research is *answering* the question. It isn't: it's **choosing a good question in the first place**. A well-chosen question is half the paper; a badly chosen one wastes months, however well you do the work.

And the **literature review** isn't a summary of everything written on your topic. It's an **argument**: here's what is already known, and here's the specific gap my study fills. It's how you prove your question is new. This lesson covers both, and then you'll write the first version of your own review.

## Where we stopped
<!-- bite: 01bf9ea3-94ef-4cdb-b9c7-bbec91c21015 -->

Your reading log has ten flyovers and three proper reads, and your journal has a contribution you rewrote after reading. Now you'll check that it's a good question, and write down, as an argument, why it's new.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: d3d31065-77de-45e2-a05d-6707606e3458 -->

A good research question is like a good place to dig for treasure. It has to be three things at once. **Nobody has dug there yet**, or you'll just find what someone else already found. **There's probably treasure**, or you'll dig a lot for nothing. And **your shovel can reach it**: dig toward the centre of the Earth with a plastic spade and you'll never get there.

The literature review is your map of where people have already dug.

**Where the analogy stops working.** On a treasure map, a dug spot is obvious. In research, other people's digging is spread across papers that use different words for the same thing, so making the map takes real searching.

## Seeing a good question
<!-- bite: c74bbcd7-1844-422d-8a74-45c0af5787a3 -->

Those three things are **novel**, **significant** and **feasible**. A good question sits where all three overlap:

![A good research question as three overlapping circles: novel (nobody has answered it), significant (the answer matters to someone) and feasible (you can do it with your time, skills and equipment). Only the small middle, where all three overlap, is a good question; outside it, a question is already answered, or nobody cares, or it is out of reach](images/rs-good-question.svg)

Most bad projects fail one of the three before any work is done. Good questions are scarce, and experienced researchers are valuable mostly because they've learned a taste for them. That taste comes from reading a lot, and from knowing where questions hide.

## Where good questions come from
<!-- bite: 02b7afab-eb59-468d-9df0-250f59a4b6e3 -->

Questions rarely arrive like lightning. You dig them out of other people's work:

1. **"Limitations" and "Future work" sections.** Authors list what their paper doesn't solve. They've handed you open problems.
2. **"Does it still hold if…?"** Take a result and change one thing: a different place, harsher conditions, a bigger scale, the real world instead of a lab. Testing a result under new conditions is real, publishable research.
3. **Borrowing across fields.** Use a method from one field on a problem in another.
4. **Where papers disagree.** Two papers claim opposite things. Why? Settling it is a good contribution.
5. **Where a method breaks.** In a deep read you spot a hidden assumption. What happens when it's broken?
6. **Your own itch.** A problem you hit while building something: "I needed X, nothing did it well, so I built one and measured it."

The weather study comes from number 2: weather forecasts have been checked in many places, but do the free apps people in Lagos actually use hold up there?

## What a literature review is for
<!-- bite: f2a75ace-2ebc-4c21-9506-c21568454f5c -->

A literature review does two jobs: it shows **what's already known**, and it reveals the **gap** your study fills. Here's the difference between a weak one and a strong one, using the same four papers:

![Two ways to write a literature review. Left, a list in date order: Smith 2015 did this, then Okafor 2018, then Lee 2020, then Bello 2023, each described on its own; it shows reading but no understanding, and no gap. Right, the same papers grouped by theme into two boxes, what the groups show together, and an arrow from the themes to the gap: what nobody has done yet, which is what your study does](images/rs-lit-review-shapes.svg)

The list on the left proves you read the papers. The themes on the right prove you understood them, and they lead somewhere: to the gap.

## Writing it well
<!-- bite: 788a40a3-e787-4120-9847-1ab3d54e1b2d -->

Four rules:

1. **Search systematically.** Start from a survey and the seminal papers, and follow citations backward and forward, as in the last lesson. Try several wordings: fields use different words for the same idea.
2. **Organise by theme, not by date.** Group papers by approach or sub-problem ("studies that measured X", "studies that compared Y"), not "Smith did this, then Okafor did that".
3. **Synthesise, don't list.** Say what each group shows *together*, where they agree and where they don't, citing several papers at once.
4. **Name the gap out loud.** End with the sentence the whole review builds toward: *"However, no published work has ___; this study does."*

A **gap** is something the field would benefit from knowing but doesn't yet. Not every gap is worth filling; the good ones are gaps whose answer matters.

## Scoping the question
<!-- bite: 6e4e2d2e-fccd-4637-bd93-37e7eef4730b -->

Once you have a candidate question, size it. The classic checklist is **FINER**:

1. **Feasible**: can you actually do it with your time, skills, data and equipment? Students overestimate this one most.
2. **Interesting**: will anyone care about the answer?
3. **Novel**: not already answered. The literature review checks this.
4. **Ethical**: can it be done responsibly? See [[research/07-research-ethics-and-integrity|Research Ethics and Integrity]].
5. **Relevant**: does it matter to someone, or move the field forward?

## Not too broad, not too narrow
<!-- bite: 57da87f9-e007-4c82-af08-37b2da78b527 -->

Then two blunt tests. First, size. Too broad is a career, not a study; too narrow is trivial. Here's the weather study on that scale:

![Scoping a research question on a scale from too broad to too narrow, using the weather study. Too broad: make weather forecasting accurate, which is a whole career. Just right: how accurate are three free weather apps for next-day rain and temperature in Lagos over four weeks, which one person can answer convincingly. Too narrow: was one app right about rain on one Tuesday, which nobody would care about](images/rs-scope-scale.svg)

Second, the **"so what?" test**: imagine you've finished and have a clean answer. Say it out loud. Does anyone care? For the weather study: "app B is right about tomorrow's rain 20% more often than app A in Lagos" matters to anyone deciding whether to carry an umbrella, plan a market day or water a farm.

## Staying current
<!-- bite: 2efb9a59-1b2c-4b48-9d62-2604b799edc4 -->

The frontier moves, so keep up cheaply. Set a **Google Scholar alert** for your topic, and another for when a key paper gets cited. Follow the main researchers and groups in your area. Fifteen minutes a week keeps your map current, and often turns up your next question.

## Step 1: mine your three papers for questions
<!-- bite: b196877a-aee0-4ae8-910c-52e65e5cb42f -->

Open the three papers you read properly in the last lesson. Find their "Limitations" and "Future work" sections. In your journal, copy every open problem they name, and next to each one write which of the six sources it is.

## Step 2: run FINER on your question
<!-- bite: 1db57bf9-bc33-4be4-95c6-0fe222c07ca4 -->

Write your question as one sentence. Under it, write one line for each letter of FINER. Be honest about **feasible**: list what you need (for the weather study: two or three apps, a phone, a reliable record of what the weather actually did, such as a rain gauge or a nearby weather station's data, and about four weeks) and whether you have it. If a letter fails, change the question until it passes.

## Step 3: write your literature review
<!-- bite: b905b29b-80de-4feb-9819-bc43ac13caf3 -->

Create `lit-review.md`. Write **one page**:

1. Two or three themed paragraphs, each grouping several papers. For the weather study, likely themes are how forecast accuracy is usually measured; what studies of forecasts over West Africa have found; and what's known about the accuracy of consumer apps and public forecasts elsewhere.
2. A final paragraph that names the gap: *"However, no published work has ___; this study does."*

Cite each paper by author and year. The proper reference style comes in a later lesson.

## Step 4: set your alerts
<!-- bite: 32a9dd66-5fc0-4feb-aa1e-94652072a1d4 -->

Set at least one Google Scholar alert for your topic's key search words.

## Your project so far
<!-- bite: 1b6d8375-1868-4230-8d57-3715bdd47563 -->

```text
my-study/
├── journal.md       ← + open problems from your papers; your question with a FINER line each
├── reading-log.md
└── lit-review.md    ← one page: 2–3 themes, then the gap sentence
Google Scholar: at least one alert
```

**Done when** your question passes all five FINER letters and the "so what?" test, and `lit-review.md` has two or three themed paragraphs (not a list by date) ending in a gap sentence that your study fills.

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: 301940d2-6931-4e14-8cd7-fb78abed2b52 -->

1. A research question is **novel** when nobody has answered it, **significant** when the answer matters, and **feasible** when you can actually do it.
2. A **literature review** is an argument that shows what's already known and reveals the gap a study fills.
3. A **gap** is something the field would benefit from knowing but doesn't yet.
4. **FINER** is a checklist for a question: feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant.
5. The **"so what?" test** asks whether anyone would care about the answer.

## Common pitfalls and traps
<!-- bite: b057db3e-dfde-4782-aa8b-c504ef3cd956 -->

1. **Reinventing the wheel.** Skip the literature review and you may build something that already exists. It's the most demoralising failure, and the easiest to prevent.
2. **"New to me" isn't "new".** Only the literature can tell you a question is novel.
3. **Too broad.** You'll never finish, and nothing gets a convincing answer.
4. **Not feasible.** You need data, equipment or time you don't have. Check before you commit.
5. **A list by date.** A review that only summarises paper after paper shows no understanding, and no gap.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 0f080bbf-d850-4c5e-baf2-71540da4fccb -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="4f9a2c61" -->
   A student plans to "build a perfect self-driving car for Lagos traffic" as a one-semester project. Which part of a good question does this fail most clearly?

   - [ ] Novel
   - [ ] Significant
   - [x] Feasible
   - [ ] Ethical

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Feasible: it's a career's work, not a semester's. Scoping it down, for example "how accurately can a cheap camera detect okadas at junctions in daylight?", could make it feasible.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="b8e0d317" -->
   Which part of a paper is the richest place to find open problems?

   - [ ] The abstract
   - [x] The "Limitations" and "Future work" sections
   - [ ] The reference list
   - [ ] The acknowledgements

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Limitations and future work: the authors list exactly what their paper doesn't solve.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="6c13f5a9" -->
   Which sentence is the strongest ending for a literature review?

   - [ ] "Many papers have studied weather forecasts."
   - [ ] "Finally, Bello (2023) measured rainfall in Ibadan."
   - [x] "However, no published work has compared the free weather apps people use in Lagos against measured weather; this study does."
   - [ ] "Weather is very important for everyone."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The third: it names the specific gap and says this study fills it. That's the sentence the whole review builds toward.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: gap id="1e7b84d2" answers="feasible;novel;ethical" -->
   Fill in the missing letters of FINER.

   F is ___, I is interesting, N is ___, E is ___, and R is relevant.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Feasible, novel, ethical.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="9a4d26f0" -->
   A literature review describes eight papers in the order they were published, one paragraph each. What's the main problem?

   - [ ] It has too many papers
   - [x] It lists instead of synthesising, so it shows no themes and no gap
   - [ ] Papers should be described newest first
   - [ ] It should only use three papers

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   It lists instead of synthesising. Grouping the papers by theme and ending with the gap turns it into an argument.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="d52e9b80" -->
   Take the topic "I'm interested in solar power" and turn it into one sharp research question that one student could answer in a few weeks. Say why it passes the "so what?" test.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For example: "How much does a week of Harmattan dust reduce the output of a 100 W solar panel in Kano, and how much does daily cleaning recover?" It's specific (one panel type, one place, one season, one measure), feasible with a panel, a meter and a few weeks, and it passes "so what?" because households and installers deciding how often to clean panels would use the answer.

   </details>

## Before moving on
<!-- bite: 22a1d7af-ba3d-46fe-8505-84d36bc38b0b -->

You're done when you can say whether a question is novel, significant and feasible, find candidate questions in papers, write a literature review as themes ending in a gap, and scope a question with FINER and "so what?", and your own `lit-review.md` and FINER check exist.

Recap: the scarce resource in research is a good question: novel, significant and feasible. Questions hide in limitations and future-work sections, in testing results under new conditions, in borrowing across fields, in disagreements, and in your own problems. The literature review is an argument organised by theme that ends in the gap you'll fill. Scope with FINER and "so what?".
