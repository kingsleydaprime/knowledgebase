# Production 2: Diminishing Returns and Returns to Scale

The law of variable proportions says that adding more of one input to fixed inputs eventually adds less and less output.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 7e99bbcb-1632-43d3-9aa3-ed2c33b5a9e0 -->

You should know the production function and the short and long run from [[engineering-economics/31-production-1|Production 1]], and average and marginal cost from [[engineering-economics/03-cost-concepts-2|Cost Concepts 2]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. calculate total, average and marginal product from a table, and use the relationships between them;
2. state the law of variable proportions, its assumptions and its three stages, and say why firms work in stage 2;
3. tell constant, increasing and decreasing returns to scale apart, and use the marginal and average cost relationships.

## What diminishing returns are
<!-- bite: 3d316d0b-e5d3-417f-9bff-e2603dfb5e0a -->

Picture a small kitchen with one stove. One cook makes some meals. A second cook helps a lot: one chops while the other fries. A third still helps. But by the sixth cook, they're bumping into each other around the one stove, and the seventh adds almost nothing. Put in an eighth and they get in each other's way so badly that fewer meals come out.

That's the law of variable proportions. The stove is the fixed input, the cooks are the variable input.

## Total, average and marginal product
<!-- bite: 5708bb09-6997-4b10-9f49-f3880018cdbd -->

Three numbers describe what's happening:

1. **Total product (TP):** This is the total output from all the units of the variable input.
2. **Average product (AP):** This is total product divided by the number of units of the variable input: output per cook.
3. **Marginal product (MP):** This is the extra output from one more unit of the variable input: TP(n) − TP(n − 1).

## A worked table
<!-- bite: 9f9add5e-e403-4044-97f8-b325d1e8e647 -->

The kitchen, with one stove and L cooks (this table was added for this lesson):

| Cooks (L) | TP (meals) | MP | AP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 9 | 9 | 9 |
| 2 | 32 | 23 | 16 |
| 3 | 63 | 31 | 21 |
| 4 | 96 | 33 | 24 |
| 5 | 125 | 29 | 25 |
| 6 | 144 | 19 | 24 |
| 7 | 147 | 3 | 21 |
| 8 | 128 | −19 | 16 |

MP rises to 33, then falls. AP rises to its peak of 25 at 5 cooks, then falls. TP peaks at 7 cooks, and the 8th cook makes it fall.

## The law of variable proportions
<!-- bite: 34fa81c1-5893-4339-9f94-e53ee4e58b91 -->

This law looks at output when **one input varies and the others are fixed**. In simple words: as you keep adding equal amounts of one input to fixed inputs, after some point the extra output from each addition gets smaller.

**Assumptions:** technology is unchanged, at least one input is fixed, and the inputs can be combined in different proportions.

## The three stages
<!-- bite: 76571ef2-d8d2-4965-b1bb-1a9be7832987 -->

| Stage | Name | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Increasing returns | Total product rises at an increasing rate. Average product rises throughout. The fixed input is too large compared to the variable input. |
| 2 | Diminishing returns | Total product still rises but at a decreasing rate. Marginal and average products both fall but stay positive. It ends where marginal product is zero and total product is at its maximum. |
| 3 | Negative returns | Total product falls and marginal product is negative. There is too much of the variable input. |

In the kitchen: stage 1 runs up to 5 cooks (AP still rising), stage 2 from 5 to 7 cooks (MP falling but positive), and stage 3 is the 8th cook (MP negative).

## Why firms work in stage 2
<!-- bite: 7ece58e3-8da2-454c-908d-27fe8443199a -->

A rational producer never operates in stage 1 (the fixed input is not being used well) or stage 3 (adding more input reduces output). So production happens in **stage 2**.

This links to cost: in stage 2 each extra cook adds fewer meals, so the marginal cost of each extra meal rises. And rising marginal cost is why the supply curve slopes upward (lesson 33).

## Product relationships to remember
<!-- bite: ea4697e8-c9ab-4e19-b06e-47184d7d2c20 -->

These appear in the multiple-choice questions:

- **MP equals zero when TP is at its maximum.**
- MP is greater than AP while AP is rising, and **MP cuts AP at the maximum of AP**.
- MP is highest at the point of inflexion of the TP curve (where TP stops getting steeper).

Check them on the kitchen table: TP is highest at 7 cooks, where MP is nearly zero (3). MP is above AP up to 5 cooks, and AP peaks at 5.

## Cost relationships to remember
<!-- bite: 427c83c8-7e06-49ce-ab67-7824d6cd826a -->

The same pattern holds for cost:

- TC = TFC + TVC, so TFC = TC − TVC. Average fixed cost = TFC ÷ Q. Average variable cost = the cost per unit that varies with output.
- When **marginal cost is below average cost**, average cost is falling. **MC cuts AC at the minimum point of AC.**

It's like your CGPA. If this semester's GPA (the marginal) is below your CGPA (the average), your CGPA falls. If it's above, your CGPA rises. The marginal always pulls the average towards it.

## Returns to scale
<!-- bite: d8f15c13-992b-4924-b751-7d0c84f26625 -->

The law above changes **one** input. **Returns to scale** asks what happens when **all** inputs increase in the same proportion. It's a long-run idea.

- **Constant returns:** output rises in the same proportion. Doubling all inputs doubles output.
- **Increasing returns:** output rises by more than the proportion. Doubling inputs more than doubles output.
- **Decreasing returns:** output rises by less than the proportion. Doubling inputs gives less than double output.

On an isoquant map, constant returns means equal gaps between successive isoquants along a straight line from the origin. (Your handout explains the constant case in detail and states the other two briefly.)

Increasing returns to scale is the production side of the economies of scale you met in capacity factoring (lesson 20). Production sets cost, and cost sets what producers will supply. Supply is next.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 8cfe7353-a4a4-4c79-901d-2f2382b3e915 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: exact id="b67854ab" answer="22" -->
   A workshop's total product is 40 units with 3 workers and 62 units with 4 workers. What is the marginal product of the 4th worker?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   MP = TP(4) − TP(3) = 62 − 40 = 22 units.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: exact id="950e5b98" answer="15.5" -->
   A workshop's total product is 62 units with 4 workers. What is the average product?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   AP = TP ÷ workers = 62 ÷ 4 = 15.5 units per worker.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="57a3ce7e" -->
   A firm's total product is at its maximum. What is the marginal product at that point?

   - [ ] At its maximum
   - [ ] Equal to average product
   - [x] Zero
   - [ ] Negative

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Zero. TP stops rising when the extra unit adds nothing, so MP = 0 at the top of TP. After that MP is negative (stage 3).

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="c8fcd477" -->
   A firm's marginal cost is below its average cost. What is happening to average cost?

   - [x] It is falling
   - [ ] It is rising
   - [ ] It is at its minimum
   - [ ] It is constant

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   It is falling. The marginal pulls the average towards it, and MC cuts AC at AC's minimum.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="642a4ffd" -->
   A factory doubles all its inputs (workers, machines, floor space and materials) and its output rises from 1,000 to 2,600 units. What does it show?

   - [ ] Constant returns to scale
   - [x] Increasing returns to scale
   - [ ] Decreasing returns to scale
   - [ ] Diminishing returns to a variable input

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Increasing returns to scale: doubling all inputs more than doubled output. (Diminishing returns is about changing one input only.)

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="a4a0a764" -->
   Why does a rational producer operate in stage 2 of the law of variable proportions?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   In stage 1 the average product is still rising, so the fixed input is not being used well and adding more of the variable input raises output per unit; the producer should keep adding. In stage 3 the marginal product is negative, so adding more of the variable input actually reduces total output. So the producer works in stage 2, where total product still rises but at a decreasing rate, and both marginal and average product are positive and falling.

   </details>
