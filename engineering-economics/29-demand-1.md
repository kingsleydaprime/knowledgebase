# Demand 1: Demand and the Law of Demand

The demand for a commodity is the quantity people will buy at a given price, per unit of time.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: e2867430-e80c-4588-bc06-fb4dffa8cd89 -->

You should know the cost concepts from [[engineering-economics/02-cost-concepts-1|Cost Concepts 1]]. Parts 2 to 7 are not needed for part 8.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. tell microeconomics from macroeconomics, and name the central problems of an economy;
2. define demand, build individual and market demand schedules, and draw a demand curve;
3. state the law of demand, its three causes, its assumptions and its exceptions, and tell a movement along the curve from a shift.

## What demand is
<!-- bite: 96ef6a16-19f0-4625-a0e9-856345531af1 -->

The earlier lessons treat cost and benefit as given. This part explains where they come from: what buyers want (demand), how producers turn inputs into output (production), and how the two meet in a market. It also ties back to cost, because production decides cost.

The **demand** for a commodity is the quantity people will buy **at a given price, per unit of time**. Two things follow: demand always refers to a price (saying demand for mangoes is 100 kg means nothing without a price), and it always refers to a time period (a day, a week, a month).

## Desire is not demand
<!-- bite: 1e1ad192-3a55-44a3-8b0c-43a8c4a12acb -->

A desire becomes demand only when you have the means and are willing to spend. Wanting a fan is a desire. Having the money and being ready to pay for it is demand.

So demand has three parts: the **desire** for the good, the **resources** (ability to pay) and the **willingness** to pay.

## Micro and macro
<!-- bite: a96e5771-ef1d-488a-a9b6-a0d312552c54 -->

Demand belongs to one half of economics.

- **Microeconomics** studies single decision makers: households and firms, how prices of goods are set, and how resources are shared between different goods.
- **Macroeconomics** studies the whole economy (aggregate demand, output, inflation): households, firms and government together.

The central problems of any economy are **what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce**. They exist because wants are unlimited, resources are scarce and scarce resources have alternative uses. (Scarcity again, from lesson 01.) Maximising private profit is not one of the central problems.

## The demand schedule
<!-- bite: e32f6e87-d7bb-42fc-8a71-ad0cc1967641 -->

A **demand schedule** is a table of the quantities demanded at different prices.

- **Individual** demand schedule: one person's demand. Example: 1 kg at ₦5, 2 kg at ₦4, 3 kg at ₦3, 4 kg at ₦2, 5 kg at ₦1.
- **Market** demand schedule: add up all buyers at each price. With three buyers A, B and C the market totals are 6, 9, 12, 15 and 18 kg at ₦5, 4, 3, 2 and 1.

**Why the demand schedule matters:** it shows how demand changes when price changes, it is the base for elasticity, it explains the law of demand, and together with the supply schedule it decides the market price. Businesses use it to forecast the rise in sales from a price cut.

## The demand curve
<!-- bite: 02885281-5c29-42f4-aa44-0ac5ade35865 -->

Plotting price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis gives a downward-sloping **demand curve**. The market curve is the sum of individual curves.

![Market demand curve from the lecture's schedule: price in naira on the vertical axis from 0 to 6 and quantity in kilograms on the horizontal axis from 0 to 20, with points at 6 kg for 5 naira, 9 kg for 4 naira, 12 kg for 3 naira, 15 kg for 2 naira and 18 kg for 1 naira, joined by a straight line sloping down to the right](images/ee-demand-curve.svg)

The curve slopes down: the lower the price, the more people buy. That's the law of demand.

## The law of demand
<!-- bite: d19190d7-9941-4cd7-a27c-43fc15e4cb6a -->

**Other things being equal, when the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded rises, and when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls.**

Price and quantity move in opposite directions. There is no fixed proportion: a 10% fall in price does not necessarily give a 10% rise in demand. (How big the response is, is elasticity, in lesson 30.)

## Why it happens: three causes
<!-- bite: e374130e-2b2b-472b-ac5d-8eacb8cba785 -->

1. **Diminishing marginal utility:** each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so a buyer will only take more at a lower price. **Marginal utility (MU)** is the extra satisfaction from one more unit.
2. **Income effect:** when the price falls, your real income rises, so you can buy more.
3. **Substitution effect:** when the price of a good falls, it becomes cheaper than substitutes, so people switch to it.

Your first cold bottle of zobo on a hot afternoon is heaven. The second is nice. By the fourth you don't want it even free. That falling satisfaction is diminishing marginal utility.

## Assumptions and exceptions
<!-- bite: 43c6b0e6-a70c-4275-af93-57f0e3244344 -->

**Assumptions (the "other things equal"):** the buyer's income is unchanged, tastes are unchanged, prices of substitutes and complements are unchanged, no new close substitute appears, and the good has no prestige value.

**Exceptions (where the law seems to fail):**

- Changes in habit, custom or income.
- Necessities of life such as flour: demand hardly changes with price.
- Fear of a future shortage or a future price rise makes people buy more now at today's price.
- Articles of distinction (prestige goods): a higher price raises demand.
- **Giffen goods:** very basic inferior goods, named after Sir Robert Giffen, who observed low-paid workers buying more bread after its price rose, because they could no longer afford meat. Less is bought when the price falls and more when it rises.
- Ignorance: a buyer who does not know the old price may buy more after a rise.

## Movement along the curve, or a shift
<!-- bite: e46d8fee-9e55-40a5-9d92-f7d334bba164 -->

This is a favourite exam trap.

- **Movement along the curve** (a change in *quantity demanded*) is caused only by a change in the good's own price.
- **A shift of the curve** (a change in *demand*) is caused by anything else: income, taste, the price of related goods (substitutes and complements), expected future prices or the number of buyers.

A change in the *supply* of the good does not shift the demand curve.

If garri gets cheaper, you move down the garri demand curve. If your salary rises, the whole garri demand curve shifts. Demand tells us how buyers react to price. Lesson 30 measures how strongly.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 02f11364-3cb4-42dc-aafb-ae3f00ebc7a9 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="997521d6" -->
   Which of these is a study in microeconomics?

   - [ ] Nigeria's national inflation rate
   - [x] How a single bakery sets the price of its bread
   - [ ] Total output of the whole economy
   - [ ] Government's overall budget deficit

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   How a single bakery sets its price. Microeconomics studies single decision makers (households and firms) and how prices of goods are set.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: exact id="c8ef0f15" answer="21" -->
   At a price of ₦300, buyer A wants 4 loaves, buyer B wants 7 and buyer C wants 10. They are the only buyers. What is the market quantity demanded at ₦300?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The market schedule adds all buyers at each price: 4 + 7 + 10 = 21 loaves.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="06b6aa0e" -->
   The price of rice falls and people buy more rice. On a diagram, what happens?

   - [x] A movement down along the rice demand curve
   - [ ] The rice demand curve shifts to the right
   - [ ] The rice demand curve shifts to the left

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A movement along the curve: a change in the good's own price changes the quantity demanded, not demand.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="2ef1eec6" -->
   Workers' incomes rise and they buy more meat at every price. On a diagram, what happens?

   - [ ] A movement along the meat demand curve
   - [x] The meat demand curve shifts to the right
   - [ ] The meat demand curve shifts to the left

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The curve shifts right. A change in anything other than the good's own price (here, income) shifts the curve.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="f2c30903" -->
   Low-paid workers buy more of a very basic food when its price rises, because they can no longer afford better food. What is this food called?

   - [ ] A prestige good
   - [x] A Giffen good
   - [ ] A substitute
   - [ ] A complement

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A Giffen good, a very basic inferior good: more is bought when its price rises. It is named after Sir Robert Giffen's observation about bread.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: gap id="55d176dc" answers="diminishing marginal utility;income;substitution" -->
   Fill in the gaps with the three causes of the law of demand: ___, the ___ effect and the ___ effect.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Diminishing marginal utility, the income effect and the substitution effect.

   </details>
