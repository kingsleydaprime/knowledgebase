# Introduction to Engineering Economics

Engineering economics is the use of economic ideas and tools to judge engineering options and help you decide between them.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: e69c25b8-ec0c-4338-910f-43d41ef68681 -->

Nothing beyond everyday experience of money: paying for things, saving, borrowing.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. say what engineering economics is, in plain words and in the lecture's exam wording;
2. list what it covers and how it differs from traditional economics;
3. describe the engineer's role in decision making and the seven steps of an engineering economic decision.

## What engineering economics is
<!-- bite: 8b66ded0-4d67-4330-b3ab-f1658deb7379 -->

An engineering solution is only good if it also makes financial sense. A bridge that stands but costs three times what the state can pay is not a good solution. So this course teaches you how to put numbers on that.

**Definition (your lecture version):** the application of economic concepts, principles and analytical tools to evaluate engineering options and guide decision making.

Another wording in your notes: the application of economic techniques to the evaluation of design and engineering alternatives. Its job is to check whether a project is appropriate, estimate its value, and justify it from an engineering point of view.

## It all starts with scarcity
<!-- bite: e22e369b-74f9-4c8b-a8ca-810e4b08f034 -->

The starting idea behind all economics is **scarcity**: resources are limited, so you must choose between alternatives. Economics is the study of that decision making, and engineering economics applies it to projects.

Think of it like this: you have ₦500,000. You can buy a new generator or fix the old one and put the rest into solar panels. You can't do everything, so you have to choose, and you want to choose with numbers, not vibes.

A one-sentence version for an exam: engineering economics is the application of economic concepts, principles and analytical tools to evaluate engineering alternatives and guide decision making, so that a project is both technically sound and financially justified.

## Scope
<!-- bite: fa508443-0165-432a-861e-fdafe4abaf76 -->

These are the jobs engineering economics does. Each one gets its own lessons later in the course.

- Project evaluation: will the project bring benefits that justify the investment?
- Comparing alternatives: choosing the best among competing options.
- Cost estimation.
- Risk assessment.
- Profit (profitability) analysis.
- Resource allocation and optimization.

## Engineering economics vs traditional economics
<!-- bite: 64f2333a-15a0-40b9-8c3d-f210af283df1 -->

Engineering economics borrows from general economic theory, but it is used differently.

| Point | Engineering economics | Traditional economics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Project specific, technical and financial decisions | Broad issues (macro) |
| Time horizon | Long term, a project life of about 3 to 30 years | Short-term market behaviour |
| Method | Quantitative: explicit numbers, cost projections | Qualitative reasoning and theoretical models |
| Use | Practical, applied engineering | Theoretical and policy oriented |

The row to remember is the time horizon. Because projects run for years, money spent and earned at different times has to be compared fairly. That is the whole of the time value of money, which starts in lesson 04.

## Role of engineers in decision making
<!-- bite: 3fcc2cc3-5a15-40b8-a3d9-c8feb4f49717 -->

Engineers are decision makers, not just designers. They:

1. Carry out feasibility studies (technical and economic) to compare alternative designs and investments.
2. Select materials and processes that cut cost and environmental impact.
3. Design for life cycle cost: initial, maintenance, operating and disposal cost.
4. Advise management on capital investment, for example buying new equipment or upgrading old.
5. Make replacement decisions by analysing maintenance, repair cost and replacement strategies for ageing assets.

## Steps in engineering economic decision making
<!-- bite: b5140e24-9688-4732-9f60-de245c1a63dd -->

These steps follow one another, and each one feeds the next.

1. Identify the problem or investment need. Define the alternatives, constraints and objectives clearly.
2. Develop feasible alternatives. Every problem has several possible solutions.
3. Estimate the cash flows: initial cost, operating cost, maintenance cost, salvage value, and so on.
4. Compare the options using economic criteria: present worth, annual worth, internal rate of return, benefit-cost ratio.
5. Consider risk and uncertainty, using sensitivity analysis or probability analysis.
6. Select the best option, the one that meets the technical and economic goals.
7. Implement and monitor performance, so the project delivers the expected result.

The whole course follows these steps. Cost concepts and cost estimation are step 3, the comparison methods are step 4, and risk and sensitivity analysis are step 5.

## Applications
<!-- bite: 2673a1c3-1953-4374-a304-417aebd98b8f -->

- Facility planning: plant location, capacity and layout.
- Make-or-buy decisions: build or outsource.
- Equipment replacement: the best time to replace machines.
- Energy efficiency projects: judging solar, wind or other energy-saving technology.
- Infrastructure and production planning: pipelines, bridges, roads, rail.

Engineering economics gives engineers the tools to make decisions that are technically sound and financially justified. It is the backbone of project evaluation, capital budgeting and optimization.

Every one of those decisions is a comparison of costs and benefits. So before anything else, you need to know the kinds of cost, and that's the next lesson.
