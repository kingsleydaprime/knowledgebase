# Demand 2: Elasticity of Demand

Elasticity of demand measures how strongly the quantity demanded responds to a change in price, income or the price of another good.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 0f8809a4-cb35-4cc8-bbe2-234a99405bd6 -->

You should know the law of demand from [[engineering-economics/29-demand-1|Demand 1]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. calculate the price elasticity of demand and classify it from perfectly elastic to perfectly inelastic;
2. list the factors that decide elasticity;
3. calculate income and cross elasticity, and say what their signs mean.

## What elasticity is
<!-- bite: 2bb6d333-9705-431e-a52d-0729b13a18c1 -->

The law of demand only gives the **direction** of change. Elasticity measures the **size** of the response.

If the price of salt doubles, you'll still buy about the same amount: you need it and it's cheap anyway. If the price of a particular brand of TV doubles, people will buy far fewer, or buy another brand. Both obey the law of demand, but the responses are very different. Elasticity puts a number on that difference.

## Price elasticity of demand
<!-- bite: c4b7ec21-5060-4344-9226-929c3335825c -->

The **price elasticity of demand ($e_p$)** is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price:

$$e_p = \frac{\Delta q / q}{\Delta p / p} = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta p} \times \frac{p}{q}$$

Mathematically $e_p$ is negative, because quantity and price move in opposite directions. For convenience we use the absolute value, so $e_p$ = 2 means demand is more responsive than $e_p$ = 1.

**Example:** the price of a bag of cement rises from ₦10,000 to ₦12,000 (a 20% rise), and sales fall from 100 bags to 70 (a 30% fall). $e_p = 30\% \div 20\% = 1.5$. Demand is elastic. (This example was added for this lesson.)

## The five types of price elasticity
<!-- bite: 995af424-a6ae-4972-82b6-624ab56d1e52 -->

| Type | $e_p$ | Meaning | Curve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfectly elastic | infinity | Any price rise loses all sales | Horizontal |
| Relatively elastic | greater than 1 | Quantity changes proportionally more than price | Flat |
| Unit elastic | 1 | Quantity changes by the same proportion as price | Rectangular hyperbola |
| Relatively inelastic | less than 1 | Quantity changes proportionally less than price | Steep |
| Perfectly inelastic | 0 | Quantity does not change at all | Vertical |

Relatively inelastic goods are necessities such as salt. Relatively elastic goods are luxuries and durable goods such as cars.

## Elasticity and total revenue
<!-- bite: b5bdb679-dc6d-4200-80e1-19eeab577aea -->

If demand is unitary elastic, a 25% rise in price gives a 25% fall in quantity, and total revenue does not change. Spending stays the same whatever the price.

The same logic tells a seller what a price change will do (general knowledge):

- **Elastic demand:** raising the price loses so many sales that revenue falls. Cutting the price raises revenue.
- **Inelastic demand:** raising the price loses few sales, so revenue rises.

This is why the market facet of a project study (lesson 36) looks at the elasticity of demand.

## Factors that decide elasticity
<!-- bite: afabb484-d7a8-4611-a7c9-a0ff913c03fd -->

1. **Nature of the product:** necessities (rice, salt) are inelastic, luxuries (TVs) are elastic.
2. **Extent of usage:** goods with many uses (steel, aluminium, wood) are more elastic.
3. **Availability of substitutes:** many substitutes mean elastic demand.
4. **Income level:** high earners are less affected by price changes.
5. **Proportion of income spent:** cheap items such as matches and salt are inelastic.
6. **Urgency of demand:** an urgent need with no alternative is inelastic.
7. **Durability of the product.**

## Other elasticities
<!-- bite: 429e208a-1fdd-40e4-b60c-7c7874fa9598 -->

The handout only names these; the formulas are general knowledge.

- **Income elasticity** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income. Positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods. Example: income rises from ₦25,000 to ₦50,000 (+100%), and shirts bought rise from 50 to 100 (+100%), so the income elasticity is 1.
- **Cross elasticity** = % change in quantity demanded of one good ÷ % change in the price of a related good. Positive for substitutes, negative for complements.

Why the signs? If tea gets dearer and people buy more coffee, both changes are positive: substitutes. If petrol gets dearer and people buy fewer cars, one is up and one is down: complements.

## Two more elasticities
<!-- bite: 5d5d7c69-840d-4771-af99-f69ae89e79d9 -->

Two more show up in the practice booklet.

- **Elasticity of substitution:** the responsiveness of the ratio of two goods or inputs to a change in their relative price.
- **Price elasticity of supply** = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. Above 1 is elastic. It comes back in lesson 33.

Demand is the buyer's side. Lessons 31 and 32 turn to the producer: how inputs become output, and why that sets cost.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: f5d547ac-255e-4e9e-ab25-c1a226440e31 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: exact id="399ef904" answer="1.5" -->
   The price of a phone rises from ₦100,000 to ₦110,000, and monthly sales fall from 2,000 to 1,700. What is the price elasticity of demand (as an absolute value)?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   % change in price = 10%. % change in quantity = −300/2,000 = −15%. $e_p = 15 \div 10 = 1.5$. Demand is relatively elastic.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="323658d7" -->
   A good's price elasticity of demand is 0.3. How is its demand described?

   - [ ] Relatively elastic
   - [ ] Unit elastic
   - [x] Relatively inelastic
   - [ ] Perfectly inelastic

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Relatively inelastic: less than 1 but more than 0. Quantity changes proportionally less than price, as for a necessity.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: exact id="df9fde29" answer="0.4" -->
   A household's income rises from ₦40,000 to ₦60,000 a month, and the bread it buys rises from 10 loaves to 12 loaves a month. What is its income elasticity of demand for bread?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   % change in income = 20,000/40,000 = 50%. % change in quantity = 2/10 = 20%. Income elasticity = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.4. Positive, so bread is a normal good here.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="72693228" -->
   The price of good B rises 10% and the quantity demanded of good A rises 15%. What is the cross elasticity, and how are A and B related?

   - [x] +1.5, substitutes
   - [ ] −1.5, complements
   - [ ] +1.5, complements
   - [ ] −1.5, substitutes

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Cross elasticity = +15% ÷ +10% = +1.5. Positive means substitutes: people switch from B to A when B gets dearer.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="9d4152bf" -->
   Demand for a good is unitary elastic. Its price rises by 25%. What happens to total revenue?

   - [ ] It rises by 25%
   - [ ] It falls by 25%
   - [x] It stays the same
   - [ ] It doubles

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   It stays the same. Quantity falls by the same 25%, so spending is unchanged.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: mcq id="8c070064" -->
   Which product is likely to have the most elastic demand?

   - [ ] Table salt
   - [ ] Matches
   - [x] One brand of television, with many rival brands on sale
   - [ ] Insulin for a diabetic patient

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The TV brand: a luxury with many close substitutes. Salt and matches take a tiny share of income, and insulin is an urgent need with no alternative, so those are inelastic.

   </details>
