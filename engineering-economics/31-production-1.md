# Production 1: The Production Function and Isoquants

Production is turning inputs into output, and the production function is the relationship between the inputs a firm uses and the output it gets.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 76bf20df-2eea-431a-add5-6fad7270db84 -->

You should know demand from [[engineering-economics/29-demand-1|Demand 1]] and the cost concepts from [[engineering-economics/02-cost-concepts-1|Cost Concepts 1]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. define production and the production function, and explain why production links back to cost;
2. tell the short run from the long run;
3. read an isoquant, calculate the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS), and state the three properties of isoquants.

## What production is
<!-- bite: 98ac5c6b-dc43-4f55-b375-b441d7957fbe -->

Production is turning inputs into output. It includes services such as transport and banking, not only making physical goods.

The **production function** is the relationship between inputs and output: put in this much labour, machinery and material, get out this much product. We study it by holding some inputs fixed and varying others.

Its importance: it links cost to the amount of output. Costs govern the supply of a product, and supply with demand decides price. That is why this topic connects back to the cost concepts in lesson 02.

## Short run and long run
<!-- bite: 90bcb350-0112-49e3-ab61-ffe14460772e -->

How many inputs can change depends on how much time you have.

- **Short run:** at least one input (such as the building or land) is fixed. A bakery can hire more bakers this week, but it can't build a bigger oven room by Friday.
- **Long run:** all inputs can change. Given a year, the bakery can build that room.

Lesson 32 uses both: the law of variable proportions is a short-run idea (one input changes), and returns to scale is a long-run idea (all inputs change).

## Isoquants (equal-product curves)
<!-- bite: d9d3afbb-8c86-4c84-b415-3866f666f8b9 -->

An **isoquant** shows all the combinations of two inputs that produce the **same level of output**. It is like an indifference curve, except that output can be measured exactly.

**Example** for 20 units of output:

| Combination | Units of X | Units of Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1 | 12 |
| B | 2 | 8 |
| C | 3 | 5 |
| D | 4 | 3 |
| E | 5 | 2 |

Think of X as machines and Y as workers. You can make 20 units with 1 machine and 12 workers, or 5 machines and 2 workers, or anything between.

## Drawing the isoquant
<!-- bite: 651641ac-0b0b-4bba-af12-c417e9b42fa8 -->

Plotting the five combinations gives a curve that slopes down and bends in towards the origin.

![Isoquant for 20 units of output with input X on the horizontal axis and input Y on the vertical axis: points A (1, 12), B (2, 8), C (3, 5), D (4, 3) and E (5, 2) joined by a curve that slopes down and is convex to the origin, with the drops in Y of 4, 3, 2 and 1 marked between neighbouring points](images/ee-isoquant.svg)

Look at the drops in Y between neighbouring points: 4, then 3, then 2, then 1. Each extra unit of X replaces less Y than the one before. That shrinking swap has a name.

## Marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)
<!-- bite: ee993866-a246-48e4-b809-10544bb54914 -->

The **MRTS** is the rate at which one input can replace another at the margin while output stays the same. From the table above, moving from combination A to B swaps 4 units of Y for 1 unit of X, so MRTS = 4. Then it falls to 3, 2 and 1.

It is the amount of Y given up per unit of X gained, which also equals the ratio of the two inputs' marginal products:

$$MRTS_{XY} = -\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X} = \frac{MP_X}{MP_Y}$$

The MRTS equals the slope of the isoquant (ignoring the minus sign), and equals the ratio of the marginal products of the two inputs. It **diminishes** as you substitute more X for Y.

The slower it falls, the easier it is to substitute the inputs. If it stays constant, the inputs are perfect substitutes (the isoquant is a straight line).

## Properties of isoquants
<!-- bite: bf1629b4-37d5-481d-a963-a688dfd0c61c -->

1. **They slope downward from left to right.** To keep output the same, more of one input means less of the other.
2. **Two isoquants never cross.** Each one is a different output level. If they crossed, one combination of inputs would give two different outputs at once.
3. **They are convex to the origin,** because MRTS diminishes.

A curve further from the origin is a higher output, since it uses more of both inputs.

Isoquants change two inputs while output stays put. Next lesson does the opposite: change one input and watch what happens to output.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: da17058f-2e5b-4afc-a259-e3bb6e2e58b0 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="04020a92" -->
   A farm can hire extra labourers this season but cannot buy more land until next year. Which period is this, for the farm?

   - [x] The short run, because land is fixed
   - [ ] The long run, because labour can change
   - [ ] Neither, because farming is not production

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The short run: at least one input (land) is fixed. In the long run all inputs can change.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: exact id="ab5e584f" answer="3" -->
   An isoquant for 50 units of output passes through (2 machines, 15 workers) and (3 machines, 12 workers). What is the MRTS of machines for workers between these two points?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   MRTS = −ΔY/ΔX = −(12 − 15)/(3 − 2) = 3. One more machine replaces 3 workers while output stays at 50.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="35ed42ba" -->
   Why are isoquants convex to the origin?

   - [ ] Because output rises along them
   - [x] Because the MRTS diminishes
   - [ ] Because inputs are perfect substitutes
   - [ ] Because two isoquants never cross

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the MRTS diminishes: each extra unit of X replaces less Y than the one before, so the curve flattens out.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="0ea93ea5" -->
   Along an isoquant, the MRTS stays at exactly 2 everywhere. What does that tell you about the two inputs?

   - [ ] They cannot be substituted at all
   - [x] They are perfect substitutes, and the isoquant is a straight line
   - [ ] The firm is in the long run
   - [ ] Output is rising along the isoquant

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   They are perfect substitutes: one unit of X always replaces 2 of Y, so the isoquant is a straight line.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: gap id="ee1fb520" answers="marginal products|marginal product" -->
   Fill in the gap. The MRTS equals the slope of the isoquant, and it also equals the ratio of the ___ of the two inputs.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Marginal products: $\text{MRTS} = \text{MP}_X / \text{MP}_Y$.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="d1031a06" -->
   Why can two isoquants never cross?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Each isoquant shows one level of output. If two crossed, the crossing point would be one combination of inputs giving two different outputs at the same time, which is impossible with the same technology.

   </details>
