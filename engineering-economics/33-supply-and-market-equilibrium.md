# Supply and Market Equilibrium

Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell at a given price, and equilibrium is where it equals the quantity demanded.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 79e4f8ea-5503-42b4-9865-dd65786247ac -->

You should know demand and the movement-versus-shift idea from [[engineering-economics/29-demand-1|Demand 1]], and why marginal cost rises in stage 2 from [[engineering-economics/32-production-2|Production 2]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. state the law of supply and the determinants of supply, and tell a movement along the supply curve from a shift;
2. find the equilibrium price and quantity from demand and supply equations, and explain surpluses and shortages;
3. predict what happens to price and quantity when the curves shift, and explain consumer and producer surplus.

## What supply is
<!-- bite: 1a991be4-dc9e-413d-8caa-9311cf6f3de3 -->

> **Flag:** your files cover demand but have no section on supply, elasticity of supply or equilibrium price, although they are on the syllabus. This lesson is written from general knowledge, so check it against your lecturer's wording.

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to sell **at a given price per unit of time**. It mirrors demand: demand is the buyer's side of the market, supply is the seller's.

## The law of supply
<!-- bite: 9dc40bcd-3cb4-4af4-ba10-6ed5f82d4cc7 -->

**Law of supply:** other things equal, when the price rises, the quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes **upward**.

Why? Higher prices make production more profitable and let producers cover higher marginal cost. That links back to stage 2 of the law of variable proportions: each extra unit costs more to make, so producers only make it if the price is higher.

## What shifts supply
<!-- bite: 8984ee98-88bf-442f-8ac9-6521228c2b4a -->

**Determinants of supply** (a change in any of these shifts the whole curve): cost of inputs, technology, number of sellers, prices of related goods, taxes and subsidies, government rules, and expectations.

**Movement vs shift:** a change in the good's **own price** moves you along the curve (a change in quantity supplied). A change in any other factor **shifts** the curve (a change in supply). It's the same rule as for demand.

- Better technology or cheaper inputs: supply rises, the curve moves right.
- Higher cost of production: supply falls, the curve moves left.

**Price elasticity of supply** = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. It is positive. It is higher when producers can raise output easily (spare capacity, storable goods, a long time to adjust).

## Equilibrium price
<!-- bite: e70f3d2c-2dd3-4b74-8cf5-ed4d8bb86115 -->

The market is in **equilibrium** where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The price at that point is the **equilibrium price** and the quantity is the **equilibrium quantity**.

- If the price is **above** equilibrium, supply exceeds demand (a **surplus**), so sellers cut prices.
- If the price is **below** equilibrium, demand exceeds supply (a **shortage**), so prices rise.

Either way the price is pushed back towards equilibrium. That's why it's called equilibrium: once there, nothing pushes it away.

## Example: finding the equilibrium
<!-- bite: 4d2de16b-836c-45d2-a4d7-c97bc7ba163a -->

Demand Qd = 100 − 2P and supply Qs = 20 + 3P.

1. Set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = 20 + 3P.
2. So 80 = 5P and P = **16**.
3. Then Q = 100 − 2(16) = **68** (check: 20 + 3 × 16 = 68).

At a price of 20 instead: Qd = 100 − 40 = 60 and Qs = 20 + 60 = 80, a surplus of 20. Sellers can't sell 20 units, so they cut the price back towards 16. (The surplus working was added for this lesson.)

## When the curves shift
<!-- bite: fa798f2d-6432-425f-9595-54668836a4f9 -->

| Change | Price | Quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rises (right) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand falls (left) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply rises (right): lower input cost, better technology | Falls | Rises |
| Supply falls (left): higher cost of production | Rises | Falls |
| Supply rises more than demand rises | Falls | Rises |

If price and quantity both rise together, the most likely cause is an increase in demand. If both curves move at once, one of price or quantity is certain and the other depends on which shift is bigger. A tax on a good is meant to discourage its use.

This links to inflation in lesson 09: a demand-pull rise in prices is demand growing faster than supply.

## Consumer and producer surplus
<!-- bite: fff2a362-8c41-465a-b0cb-74411f985dd3 -->

- **Consumer surplus:** the gap between what buyers would be willing to pay and what they actually pay. It is the area below the demand curve and above the price.
- **Producer surplus:** the gap between the price sellers receive and the lowest price they would accept. It is the area above the supply curve and below the price.

If you'd happily pay ₦1,500 for a plate of rice and it costs ₦1,000, you've got ₦500 of consumer surplus.

If demand increases (the curve shifts right), the equilibrium price and quantity both rise, so both consumer surplus and producer surplus can rise.

Prices and quantities in markets set the revenues and costs of projects. The last lesson of part 8 looks at the market for money itself: banking.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: df5132cc-0d0a-4e7a-8882-8a73618393ac -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: gap id="a4a0a765" answers="20;60" -->
   In a market, demand is Qd = 120 − 3P and supply is Qs = 20 + 2P. Fill in the gaps. The equilibrium price is ___ and the equilibrium quantity is ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   120 − 3P = 20 + 2P, so 100 = 5P and P = 20. Q = 120 − 60 = 60 (check: 20 + 40 = 60).

   </details>

2. <!-- q: exact id="719109dc" answer="25" -->
   In a market, demand is Qd = 120 − 3P and supply is Qs = 20 + 2P. The price is held at 25. How big is the surplus?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Qd = 120 − 75 = 45 and Qs = 20 + 50 = 70. Surplus = 70 − 45 = 25 units.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="2e6e28e0" -->
   A new machine makes it much cheaper to produce cement. What happens to the cement market?

   - [ ] Price rises and quantity falls
   - [x] Price falls and quantity rises
   - [ ] Price and quantity both rise
   - [ ] Price and quantity both fall

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Better technology shifts supply to the right, so the equilibrium price falls and the quantity rises.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="8b578309" -->
   Over a year, both the price and the quantity sold of laptops rise. What is the most likely cause?

   - [x] An increase in demand
   - [ ] An increase in supply
   - [ ] A decrease in supply
   - [ ] A decrease in demand

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   An increase in demand. Demand shifting right raises both price and quantity.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="a19dc9b7" -->
   The price of tomatoes rises, and farmers bring more tomatoes to market. On a diagram, what happens?

   - [x] A movement up along the supply curve
   - [ ] The supply curve shifts right
   - [ ] The supply curve shifts left
   - [ ] The demand curve shifts right

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A movement along the supply curve: a change in the good's own price changes the quantity supplied, not supply.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: exact id="997521d7" answer="400" accept="₦400" -->
   A buyer is willing to pay up to ₦2,400 for a pair of shoes and buys them at the market price of ₦2,000. What is the buyer's consumer surplus, in naira?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   2,400 − 2,000 = ₦400, the gap between what the buyer would pay and what they actually pay.

   </details>
