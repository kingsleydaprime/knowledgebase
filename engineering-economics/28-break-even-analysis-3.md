# Break-Even Analysis 3: Several Products

Multi-product break-even treats a fixed mix of products (say 5 of one for every 1 of another) as a single bundle, and finds how many bundles cover the fixed cost.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: db01e4df-a8ce-4fa4-9261-a1fd127dc974 -->

You should know contribution, the C/S ratio and the margin of safety from [[engineering-economics/26-break-even-analysis-1|Break-Even Analysis 1]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. find the break-even point for several products sold in a constant mix, in mixes, units and revenue;
2. find it a second way with the average C/S ratio, and work the margin of safety and target profit for a mix;
3. describe multi-product charts, and the limitations and advantages of CVP analysis.

## What multi-product break-even is
<!-- bite: b3841a10-e46f-462c-b4b3-b875ddbaee8f -->

With one product, break-even = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit. With two products there's a problem: whose contribution? Each product has its own.

The trick is the **mix**. If the firm always sells 5 units of M for every 1 unit of N, treat "5 M + 1 N" as one bundle, called a mix. A mix has one contribution, so the old formula works again with "mix" in place of "unit". This is assumption 3 of CVP from lesson 26: the sales mix is constant.

## Example: two products in a mix
<!-- bite: bc15a297-5ef6-4d09-8567-b1b73d760b9d -->

**Lecture 9, example 2:** product M sells at 7 with variable cost 2.94, product N sells at 15 with variable cost 4.50. They sell in the ratio 5 M to 1 N and fixed cost is 36,000.

1. Contribution per unit: M = 7 − 2.94 = 4.06, N = 15 − 4.50 = 10.50.
2. Contribution per mix (5 M + 1 N) = 5 × 4.06 + 10.50 = 30.80.
3. Mixes to break even = 36,000 ÷ 30.80 = about 1,169.
4. Units: 1,169 × 5 = 5,845 of M and 1,169 of N.
5. Revenue = 5,845 × 7 + 1,169 × 15 = 40,915 + 17,535 = **58,450**.

This is the exact question on the 2025/2026 exam paper (with N called A), so make sure you can do it without looking.

## The same example with the C/S ratio
<!-- bite: a24e1a3e-f3a3-434b-b7bf-ba8a44c0e0f6 -->

You can skip units entirely with the average C/S ratio of the mix.

- Revenue per mix = 5 × 7 + 15 = 50.
- Average C/S ratio = 30.80 ÷ 50 = 61.6%.
- Break-even sales = 36,000 ÷ 0.616 = about **58,442**.

That matches, apart from rounding (the first method rounded 1,168.8 mixes up to 1,169).

## Margin of safety for several products
<!-- bite: 97c06eb0-4783-4c0d-8616-d19aa60960a3 -->

**Example 4:** fixed cost 43,890. W: price 8, variable 3.80. R: price 14, variable 4.20. Ratio 5 W to 6 R.

1. Contribution per mix = 5 × 4.20 + 6 × 9.80 = 21 + 58.80 = 79.80.
2. Mixes = 43,890 ÷ 79.80 = 550.
3. Break-even units: 550 × 5 = 2,750 of W and 550 × 6 = 3,300 of R.
4. Break-even revenue = 2,750 × 8 + 3,300 × 14 = 22,000 + 46,200 = 68,200.
5. Budgeted sales are 74,400, so the margin of safety = 6,200, or **8.3%** of budget.

## Target profit for several products
<!-- bite: be3c31a7-13da-4aee-a7c8-84f9803f28b1 -->

**Example 5:** products F, G, H in the ratio 2:1:3. Selling price 22, 15, 19. Variable cost 16, 12, 13. Fixed cost 80,000, target profit 52,000.

- Contribution per unit: F = 6, G = 3, H = 6.
- Contribution per mix = 2 × 6 + 1 × 3 + 3 × 6 = 33.
- Mixes needed = (80,000 + 52,000) ÷ 33 = 4,000.
- Units: F 8,000, G 4,000, H 12,000. Sales revenue = 176,000 + 60,000 + 228,000 = **464,000**.

> **Correction:** the handout's table shows G's variable cost as 19. The solution uses 12 (contribution 3), so 12 is right.

If the mix changes, the contribution per mix and the break-even point change too. A shift toward products with a lower contribution raises the break-even point and cuts profit.

## Multi-product charts
<!-- bite: e6adfa60-c248-4d9b-94df-742cbc6b2028 -->

With a constant mix you can draw one chart for the whole range in three ways: output in sales value, products plotted in sequence, or output as a percentage of forecast sales.

In the lecture's data (X, Y, Z with budgeted revenue 58,000, variable cost 37,000 and fixed cost 10,000), the overall C/S ratio is 21,000 ÷ 58,000 = 36.21%, so break-even sales are 10,000 ÷ 0.3621 = about 27,600 (the handout rounds to 27,500) and the margin of safety is about 30,400.

A **multi-product P/V chart** plots products from left to right in order of C/S ratio (highest first), here X (62.5%), Y (33.3%), Z (16.7%). It shows the overall break-even point, which products to expand or drop, and the effect of price or volume changes.

## Limitations of CVP analysis
<!-- bite: f58a5012-f45b-4b5a-a34c-eaf635d18f18 -->

- It assumes fixed cost stays the same in total and variable cost per unit stays the same. In reality fixed costs come in steps, and unit variable cost falls with economies of scale and later rises.
- It is valid only in the normal (relevant) range of output.
- It assumes the price is constant, but a higher volume may need a lower price.
- It assumes production equals sales, so changes in inventory are ignored.
- It treats estimates of cost as certain.

## Advantages of CVP analysis
<!-- bite: 296f1e6a-b8ad-494f-9790-a6364feb4880 -->

- Charts are easy for non-finance managers to understand.
- It shows profit or loss at any level of activity within the valid range.
- The break-even point and margin of safety give a feel for risk.

That finishes part 7. Notice that the limits keep saying "in reality, price falls when you sell more" and "costs change with scale". Part 8 explains why: demand, production and markets.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: b807c121-1713-4655-a224-e93f4cfe13ec -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: exact id="1b132571" answer="20" -->
   Product P sells for ₦10 with variable cost ₦6. Product Q sells for ₦20 with variable cost ₦12. They sell in the ratio 3 P to 1 Q. What is the contribution per mix, in naira?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Contribution: P = 4, Q = 8. Per mix (3 P + 1 Q) = 3 × 4 + 8 = ₦20.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: gap id="ef1f6728" answers="1200|1,200;3600|3,600;1200|1,200;60000|60,000" -->
   Product P sells for ₦10 with variable cost ₦6. Product Q sells for ₦20 with variable cost ₦12. They sell in the ratio 3 P to 1 Q, and fixed cost is ₦24,000. Fill in the gaps. Break-even is ___ mixes, which is ___ units of P and ___ units of Q, and revenue of ₦___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Contribution per mix = 20, so mixes = 24,000 ÷ 20 = 1,200. Units: P = 3 × 1,200 = 3,600, Q = 1,200. Revenue = 3,600 × 10 + 1,200 × 20 = 36,000 + 24,000 = ₦60,000.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: exact id="5308a1ba" answer="0.4" accept="40%|40" -->
   Product P sells for ₦10 with variable cost ₦6. Product Q sells for ₦20 with variable cost ₦12. They sell in the ratio 3 P to 1 Q. What is the average C/S ratio of the mix, as a decimal?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Contribution per mix = 20. Revenue per mix = 3 × 10 + 20 = 50. C/S = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.4, or 40%.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: exact id="78f8cef9" answer="20" accept="20%" -->
   A two-product firm's break-even revenue is ₦60,000 and its budgeted revenue is ₦75,000. What is its margin of safety as a percentage of budgeted revenue?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   MOS = 75,000 − 60,000 = ₦15,000. MOS % = 15,000 ÷ 75,000 × 100 = 20%.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="8b578308" -->
   A firm sells a high-contribution product and a low-contribution product in a fixed mix. Customers start buying more of the low-contribution product. What happens to the break-even point?

   - [x] It rises
   - [ ] It falls
   - [ ] It stays the same, because fixed cost hasn't changed

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   It rises. The contribution per mix falls, so more sales are needed to cover the same fixed cost, and profit falls.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="a19dc9b6" -->
   Give three limitations of cost-volume-profit analysis.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Any three of: it assumes fixed cost is constant in total and variable cost per unit is constant, though fixed costs come in steps and unit variable cost changes with scale; it is valid only in the relevant range of output; it assumes a constant price, though higher volume may need a lower price; it assumes production equals sales, ignoring inventory changes; it treats cost estimates as certain; for several products it assumes a constant sales mix.

   </details>
