# Capital Budgeting 2: Project Analysis and Its Objective

Project analysis studies a proposed project from five sides (market, technical, financial, economic and ecological) to decide whether it is worth doing.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 8056aa59-a250-44d6-ac3e-0c78b74217c2 -->

You should know the six phases of capital budgeting from [[engineering-economics/35-capital-budgeting-1|Capital Budgeting 1]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. describe the five facets of project analysis and the question each one answers;
2. explain what a feasibility study covers;
3. state the objective of capital budgeting, say why profit maximisation is a weaker goal, list common weaknesses in capital budgeting, and argue whether efficient capital allocation is the most important finance function.

## What project analysis is
<!-- bite: ecf45076-7dd1-4165-bbf2-eccf5f0626cf -->

Remember phase 2 of capital budgeting, analysis? It's the detailed study of whether a project will work. It looks at the project from five sides, called facets, because a project can fail in five different ways. Nobody wants the product (market). The machine doesn't work here (technical). It loses money (financial). It hurts society (economic). It poisons the river (ecological).

## The five facets of project analysis
<!-- bite: 6277f18f-dfad-4264-b99a-88632b3c361c -->

| Facet | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Market | What will be the total demand, and what share will the project get? |
| Technical | Are the technical conditions right and are the choices sensible? |
| Financial | Is it financially viable and will it satisfy investors? |
| Economic | Is it good for society? (social cost-benefit analysis) |
| Ecological | What is the environmental damage and what will restoration cost? |

## What each facet looks at
<!-- bite: e0ed41d6-b4df-486d-9bec-25ea48ad7a40 -->

- **Market:** past and present consumption and supply, competition, cost structure, elasticity of demand (lesson 30), consumer behaviour, distribution channels, legal limits.
- **Technical:** location, size and scale, process, machines, raw material and power supply, layout, effluent treatment, realistic work schedules.
- **Financial:** investment outlay, financing, cost of capital, profitability, break-even point (part 7), cash flows, criteria of merit (part 3), risk.
- **Economic:** benefits and costs in shadow (efficiency) prices, effect on income distribution, savings and investment, social goals such as jobs.
- **Ecological:** needed for power plants, irrigation, chemicals, leather processing and other polluting industries.

A **shadow price** is the true value of something to society, which can differ from its market price (for example labour in a place with many unemployed people).

## The feasibility study
<!-- bite: d6bc4b1d-bdd3-4118-a6bb-e335d1893ad4 -->

A **feasibility study** covers the first four phases (planning, analysis, selection, financing) and the five facets above.

It starts with an idea, screens it, and stops if the idea is not promising. Otherwise, it carries out the market, technical, financial, economic and ecological analyses, then decides if the project is worthwhile and prepares a funding proposal.

## Objective of capital budgeting
<!-- bite: d2577d69-dc6d-4d57-9844-2ad8f5384610 -->

The goal is to **maximise shareholder wealth**, which means the market value of the firm's equity.

Alternatives such as maximising profit, earnings per share or return on equity are weaker. Profit in absolute terms is not a good guide:

1. it ignores **timing** (profit now vs later);
2. it ignores **risk** (a certain 50,000 is not the same as a variable profit with an expected value of 50,000).

Maximising earnings per share and return on equity share those two weaknesses.

This links to part 6: **return and risk go together**. Higher return usually comes with higher risk. Market value rises with return and falls with risk.

## Common weaknesses in capital budgeting
<!-- bite: ce857363-e260-4e95-8636-35c1d003693c -->

1. Strategy and the capital budget are not aligned.
2. The **base case** (what happens without the project) is poorly identified. A gloomy base case makes any project look good.
3. Risk is treated too simply (just low, medium and high scenarios).
4. Future options (expand, switch use, abandon) are not valued.
5. Divisions use different assumptions (growth, inflation, residual value, cost of capital).
6. Side effects on the firm's other products are ignored.
7. Pay is not linked to the financial measures (NPV, IRR) used to approve the project.

## Is efficient capital allocation the most important finance function?
<!-- bite: debff5d0-e7db-471b-aad1-caabec11efdf -->

This was a 7-mark essay in the 2017/2018 paper. The points for "agree":

Capital is scarce, so every naira used on one project cannot be used on another. Capital decisions are large, long term and hard to reverse, so mistakes are costly. They set the firm's future direction, cash flows and risk. The goal is to maximise shareholder wealth, and the firm that allocates capital better earns more from the same money. That is why many argue it is the most important finance function.

A balanced answer can also note that other functions matter too, such as raising funds (financing), managing working capital and paying dividends, and that a good capital decision still needs good financing and management.

Capital budgeting looks at projects from the money side. Lessons 37 and 38 look at them from the management side: what a project is, and how it's run.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="ab5e5850" -->
   Which facet of project analysis asks "what will be the total demand, and what share will the project get?"

   - [x] Market
   - [ ] Technical
   - [ ] Financial
   - [ ] Economic

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The market facet. It looks at consumption, supply, competition, elasticity of demand and distribution.

2. <!-- q: mcq id="35ed42bb" -->
   A government study values a dam's benefits and costs in shadow prices, and asks how it affects jobs and income distribution. Which facet is this?

   - [ ] Financial
   - [x] Economic
   - [ ] Ecological
   - [ ] Technical

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The economic facet: social cost-benefit analysis, asking whether the project is good for society.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="0ea93ea6" -->
   What is the objective of capital budgeting?

   - [ ] To maximise profit
   - [ ] To maximise earnings per share
   - [x] To maximise shareholder wealth (the market value of the firm's equity)
   - [ ] To minimise risk

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   To maximise shareholder wealth. Profit, earnings per share and return on equity ignore timing and risk.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: gap id="ee1fb521" answers="timing;risk" -->
   Fill in the gaps. Profit maximisation is a weaker goal than shareholder wealth because it ignores ___ (profit now versus later) and ___ (a certain profit versus a variable one).

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Timing and risk.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="d1031a07" -->
   A division assumes the economy will collapse if it doesn't build a new plant, so the plant looks very profitable. Which weakness in capital budgeting is this?

   - [ ] Future options are not valued
   - [x] The base case is poorly identified
   - [ ] Pay is not linked to NPV and IRR
   - [ ] Side effects are ignored

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The base case (what happens without the project) is poorly identified. A gloomy base case makes any project look good.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="0864f295" -->
   An efficient allocation of capital is argued to be the most important finance function in modern times. Do you agree? Explain.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

But other finance functions matter too: raising funds, managing working capital and paying dividends. A good capital decision still needs good financing and management to deliver its value.

   </details>
