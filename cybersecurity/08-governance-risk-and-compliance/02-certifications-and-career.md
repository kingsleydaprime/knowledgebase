# Certifications and Career

**A security certification is an exam-based qualification that shows employers you know an area of security, and they matter more in security hiring than in most of tech.**

## Before you start
<!-- bite: c2711c9e-2f45-43aa-b06c-dcc555751bf4 -->

You should have:

- A sense of the course's sections, since the career paths map onto them → [[cybersecurity/index|the course map]].
- Met the GRC and SOC roles this formalises → [[cybersecurity/08-governance-risk-and-compliance/01-risk-and-frameworks|risk and frameworks]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. Name the main security career paths and which section of this course each builds on.
2. Match the well-known certifications to the path and seniority they fit.
3. Explain why hands-on certs (OSCP) are valued over paper ones, and where certs stop helping.
4. Pick a realistic next step for your own direction.

**Study route.** Kid version and §1–3, then the practice task: drafting your own path and next cert.

## What this lesson is
<!-- bite: 83eab7d6-ff9c-4417-8f31-dc2c2ed8604b -->

The rest of this course teaches the *skills*. This one is about the *job market* for them: the roles, the certificates that act as hiring filters, and how they connect. It's a reference lesson, not a lab — the "practice" is planning your own path.

Security hiring leans on certs more than most of tech because much of the field (especially government and defence) *requires* them, and because skills are hard to verify in an interview. By the end you'll know which path builds on skills you already have, which cert opens its door, and — just as important — why a cert without real practice shows through quickly.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: c1748fd4-ce13-4f79-a71a-ac67a3b8c1d7 -->

Think of security jobs like roles on a football team. There are attackers (strikers), defenders, the medics who deal with injuries, and the manager who runs the whole thing and talks to the owners. They're all "football", but each needs different training and proves it differently — a striker proves themselves by scoring, a manager by results and a coaching badge.

Security is the same. There's the attacking side (break in, with permission), the defending side (watch and stop attacks), the medics (investigate after a breach), and the managers (decide strategy, talk to the board). Each has its own certificates that say "this person can do this role".

**Where the analogy stops working.** A coaching badge without ever having played shows on the pitch immediately — and in security it's the same, but sharper: a paper certificate with no hands-on ability falls apart the moment someone hands you a real target. The respected certs (like OSCP) are respected *because* you can't pass them without actually doing the thing. The badge opens the door; the skill keeps you in the room.

## 1. The career paths
<!-- bite: fe671a11-cf71-4109-b7f6-c342306d6b2c -->

Security isn't one job. The main tracks map onto this course's sections:

1. **Offensive** (red team / penetration tester / ethical hacker) — [[cybersecurity/02-ethical-hacking/index|ethical hacking]] and [[cybersecurity/06-attacks-and-threats/index|attacks]].
2. **Defensive / SOC analyst / blue team** — [[cybersecurity/07-security-operations/index|security operations]]: monitoring, detection, incident response. The largest employer of entry-level security talent.
3. **DFIR** (incident responder / forensic analyst) — the deep end of [[cybersecurity/07-security-operations/05-forensics-and-malware-analysis|forensics and IR]].
4. **Application / product security** — [[cybersecurity/04-web-security/index|web and app security]], secure coding, working with dev teams. A natural pivot for a software engineer.
5. **Cloud security / DevSecOps** — [[cybersecurity/09-cloud-security/index|securing cloud and infrastructure]], overlapping DevOps.
6. **GRC / security management** — [[cybersecurity/08-governance-risk-and-compliance/01-risk-and-frameworks|risk, compliance, audit]], leading toward CISO.

**For someone coming from software engineering**, application security and cloud/DevSecOps are the most natural entries — they build directly on skills you already have.

## 2. The certification landscape
<!-- bite: adb9dbff-cb21-4731-938e-113027643035 -->

Grouped by level and focus.

**Foundational / entry:**

1. **CompTIA Security+** — the standard entry-level cert; broad fundamentals, widely required as a baseline. A sensible first cert.
2. **CompTIA Network+ / A+ / Linux+** — the IT foundation beneath security, much of it overlapping [[devops/01-linux/index|Linux]] and networking.

**Offensive:**

3. **CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker)** — broad, well-known, theory-heavy.
4. **OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional)** — the respected, *hands-on* pentest cert, with a gruelling 24-hour practical exam. Hard to fake, highly valued.
5. **PNPT, GPEN, GWAPT** — other respected practical pentest certs.

## 2. The certification landscape: defensive and senior
<!-- bite: f45b8341-cefa-430b-b984-d83cbcaaf38d -->

The rest of the map, from blue team up to leadership.

**Defensive / blue team:**

6. **CompTIA CySA+**, **GCIH** (incident handling), **GCFA** (forensics), **Blue Team Level 1/2** — SOC, IR and forensics focused.

**Management / senior:**

7. **CISSP** — the flagship senior/management cert (broad, requires experience); often a gate for security leadership.
8. **CISM**, **CISA** — management and audit focused (the GRC track).

**Cloud:** vendor security certs (AWS Security Specialty and friends) plus vendor-neutral ones. Which to chase depends entirely on the path you picked above.

## 3. The honest advice
<!-- bite: a39caa15-4aae-4e49-98ad-60baadd0f290 -->

Certs are a tool, not a destination:

1. **Certs open doors but don't replace skill.** OSCP is respected precisely because it's hands-on; a paper cert with no practical ability shows quickly. Pair every cert with real practice — the [[cybersecurity/02-ethical-hacking/12-practice-exercises|labs and platforms]] (HackTheBox, TryHackMe, picoCTF) and a [[cybersecurity/02-ethical-hacking/05-home-lab-setup|home lab]] are where the actual capability comes from.
2. **Match the cert to the path.** Security+ to get in the door, then OSCP (offensive) / CySA+ or GCIH (defensive) / CISSP (management) depending on direction.
3. **For a developer, building secure things often beats a cert first.** Learning app and cloud security on the job, shipping real work, is the same "reps over reading" principle as the rest of this vault — and it's exactly what this whole cybersecurity rebuild has been doing with owned targets.

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: 4bc89433-26c8-4b9a-a850-d1d83d85b028 -->

1. **Certification**: This is an exam-based qualification proving knowledge of a security area, often used as a hiring filter.
2. **Red team / blue team**: The red team is the offensive career track (attack, with permission); the blue team is the defensive one (monitor, detect, respond).
3. **DFIR (Digital Forensics and Incident Response)**: This is the career track that investigates breaches after they happen — the deep end of the blue team.
4. **OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional)**: This is the respected hands-on pentest certification, earned through a 24-hour practical exam rather than a multiple-choice test.
5. **CISSP**: This is the flagship senior/management security certification, broad and experience-gated, often required for leadership roles.
6. **DevSecOps**: This is security folded into the DevOps workflow — the cloud-security career track for people with an operations background.

## Check your understanding

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="1573fe61" -->
   A software engineer wants to move into security using skills they already have. Which two tracks are usually the most natural entry?

   - [ ] Red team pentesting and DFIR forensics
   - [x] Application/product security and cloud/DevSecOps
   - [ ] GRC management and CISO leadership
   - [ ] Malware reverse engineering and threat intelligence

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Application security and cloud/DevSecOps build directly on a developer's existing skills — writing and reviewing code, understanding systems, working with CI/CD and infrastructure. The other tracks are reachable but require more new, specialised knowledge first. Starting where your current skills transfer gets you in the door fastest.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="b7224ffc" -->
   Why is the OSCP more respected than many multiple-choice certifications?

   - [ ] It's the most expensive one.
   - [x] It's earned through a 24-hour hands-on practical exam where you must actually compromise machines, so it's hard to pass without real skill.
   - [ ] It's required by law for all security jobs.
   - [ ] It never expires.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   OSCP's value comes from being *practical*: you have to actually break into target machines and document it, not just answer questions. That makes it hard to fake, so employers trust it as evidence of real ability. It's the clearest example of the lesson's core point — the badge is only worth what the skill behind it is.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: gap id="db98476a" answers="Security+|Security plus" -->
   The standard entry-level, broad-fundamentals certification that's widely required as a baseline and is a sensible *first* security cert is CompTIA ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Security+. It covers broad fundamentals and is a common baseline requirement, which makes it the usual door-opener before specialising (OSCP for offensive, CySA+/GCIH for defensive, CISSP for management).

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="39e0683c" -->
   Which certification is aimed at the senior/management level and typically requires years of experience, often acting as a gate for security leadership roles?

   - [ ] CEH
   - [ ] Security+
   - [x] CISSP
   - [ ] GCFA

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   CISSP is the flagship management-level cert — broad, experience-gated, and frequently required for leadership (it sits on the GRC/management track with CISM and CISA). CEH and GCFA are practitioner certs (offensive and forensics), and Security+ is entry-level.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="dd60683e" -->
   You pass a theory-only certification but have never practised on a real or simulated target. Per the lesson's honest advice, what's the risk?

   - [ ] The certificate expires immediately.
   - [x] The gap shows quickly when you face a real target — the cert opens a door, but without hands-on skill you can't do the job behind it.
   - [ ] You're legally barred from security work.
   - [ ] Theory certs are worthless and should never be taken.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A cert is a hiring filter, not a substitute for ability. Theory certs still have value (Security+ gets you in the door), but paired with no practice, the lack of real skill surfaces fast. The lesson's advice is to pair every cert with reps on labs, platforms and a home lab.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="ecfcd5af" -->
   Lay out a realistic two-step certification route for someone who wants a defensive (blue-team / SOC) career, and say what they should be doing *alongside* the certs.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Step one: CompTIA Security+ to establish broad fundamentals and get past the baseline hiring filter. Step two: a defensive specialisation — CySA+ or GCIH (incident handling), moving toward GCFA if they lean into forensics/DFIR. Alongside the certs, the key is hands-on reps: work through blue-team labs and platforms (TryHackMe/HackTheBox defensive paths, Blue Team Level 1), and ideally build a small home lab where they generate logs, write detection rules, and practise triaging alerts — exactly the skills the security-operations section taught. The certs open doors; the lab work is what makes them able to walk through. A model answer should pair *a cert sequence* with *a practice habit*, not just list certs.

   </details>

## Practice — draft your own path
<!-- bite: c1c7a30d-8a28-4d26-9c30-e80e98a13981 -->

**Task.** This one's about you, not a lab.

1. Pick the career track that best fits where you're coming from and where you want to go (for a developer, likely appsec or cloud/DevSecOps).
2. Write a two- or three-step cert route for it (door-opener → specialisation → optional senior), with rough timing.
3. For each cert, note the *hands-on practice* you'll do alongside it (a platform, a lab, a project), since that's where the real skill comes from.
4. Name one real thing you could build or break (on an owned target) in the next month that would teach more than the cert's theory.

## Practice: when it's done
<!-- bite: 4b26e12f-c14c-43c0-a6c7-2bd5638c1fc7 -->

Here's how you know the task is finished.

**Done when:** you have a written path — track, cert sequence, and a practice habit beside each cert — and at least one concrete owned-target project lined up. It doesn't go in `LIVE-RUNS-TODO.md` (there's nothing to run); it goes in your learning notes. The test is whether a mentor reading it would say "yes, that's a realistic plan", not "that's a list of exams".
