# Phishing and Scams

**Phishing is a fake message made to look like someone you trust, so you click, pay or share a code — and two simple habits stop nearly all of it.**

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 5e9b8118-855a-4369-a428-215359cb8bac -->

You should have:

- Met the "read me the code" scam, which this expands on → [[cybersecurity/10-protecting-yourself/03-two-factor-and-passkeys|two-factor and passkeys]].
- Nothing technical.

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. Name the phishing tells — and the two that AI has killed.
2. Recognise the main scam varieties, including BEC and romance/investment fraud.
3. Apply the two habits that stop most phishing, and the five rules.
4. Know exactly what to do in the first minutes after you click.

**Study route.** Kid version and the tells, then the rules and the practice task: audit a real message and agree a family code word.

## What phishing is
<!-- bite: 42757212-34a4-4524-aa48-0b7dcc21740f -->

This is thing number four, and the most common way people actually lose money. Almost nothing that reaches you is a *technical* attack — it's a message designed to make you act before you think. The whole craft is manufactured urgency: your account will be closed, your parcel is held, there's suspicious activity. Panic is the exploit; everything else is decoration.

Because the target is *you*, not your computer, no software fully fixes it and no technical skill makes you immune. What works is procedural — two habits that require no expertise. This lesson covers the tells (and the two that stopped working), the varieties from bulk email to months-long romance fraud, the rules, and what to do the moment you realise you clicked.

## The kid version
<!-- bite: 80c61e78-bc82-43b6-8d02-a698239586b2 -->

Imagine a stranger phones pretending to be your school, your bank, your mum — anyone you'd trust — and says something alarming: *there's a problem, you must act right now.* The trick isn't clever technology; it's the rush. If they can make you panic, you'll do what they say before you stop to check. The cure is boring and total: when a message makes you feel you must act *immediately* about money or a password, that feeling is the attack, so you slow down and check through a channel you chose yourself.

**Where the analogy stops working.** A stranger on the phone used to give themselves away — bad grammar, a dodgy number, a clumsy story. Not any more. AI writes perfect messages and clones voices, so a scam can now sound exactly like your bank or exactly like your child. So you can't rely on spotting the fake by how polished it is — you have to rely on the *habit* of never acting from the message itself, because the message will look and sound real.

## The tells
<!-- bite: 27cfc2df-bc2e-43d2-89df-4222c3bb388b -->

1. **Urgency and threat.** *"Within 24 hours or your account will be suspended."* Real organisations don't work this way. Manufactured time pressure is the single most reliable indicator.
2. **An unexpected link or attachment**, even from someone you know — their account may be the compromised one.
3. **A request that breaks procedure.** *"I'm in a meeting, can you just pay this invoice quickly?"* — the boss who never texts, texting.
4. **Slightly wrong details.** `paypa1.com`, a display name that doesn't match the address behind it. Hover over links before clicking; on mobile, long-press to preview.
5. **Wrong tone or context.** A bank using your name oddly, or a service you don't use.

**Two old tells that no longer work:** bad spelling and poor design. **AI has removed both.** Modern phishing is well written and pixel-perfect, so *"it looked professional"* is no longer evidence of anything.

## The varieties
<!-- bite: 9fb3bdce-3a11-4a98-8073-9b16b7a10276 -->

1. **Bulk email phishing** — millions of copies; fake login pages for Microsoft 365, banks, delivery firms.
2. **Spear phishing** — targeted, researched, using real details about you. Far more convincing.
3. **Smishing (text) and vishing (voice).** Voice is now the dangerous one: **AI voice cloning** fakes a distressed relative's voice from seconds of social-media audio.

## The varieties: the costly ones
<!-- bite: c3ef59c0-05cf-4ea8-a0ed-9250e65c3bbe -->

The attacks that take the most money are the next few.

4. **Business email compromise (BEC)** — the most expensive category by value. A supplier emails new bank details for a real invoice; the email is real, from a compromised account, the details are the attacker's. **Verify any change of payment details by phone, on a number you already had.**
5. **Tech support scams** — a pop-up or call about a virus, leading to remote-access software. Microsoft and Apple will never call you.
6. **Romance and investment fraud ("pig butchering")** — weeks of a genuine-feeling relationship, then an investment on a fake platform showing fake gains. **The largest category by money lost**, often run from trafficked-labour compounds; the victims were groomed for months, not foolish.
7. **Recovery scams** — someone who "can get your lost money back", targeting people already defrauded. Being scammed once puts you on a list.

> **Agree a family code word** — a word only your family knows, for verifying an urgent call. It costs one conversation and defeats voice cloning entirely.

## The rules
<!-- bite: e1868377-8ec6-408b-b50d-43f2cef12070 -->

1. **Never use the link. Ever.** Not because you can't spot fakes, but because you don't have to. Open your banking app, type the address, use your bookmark. Nothing legitimate is lost by arriving under your own steam.
2. **Verify out of band.** Urgent request about money or credentials → contact them on a number or address **you already had**, never the one in the message. A five-minute delay costs nothing.
3. **Nobody legitimate asks for these:** your password; a code that was sent to you; remote access to your computer; payment in gift cards or crypto; a crypto seed phrase.
4. **Slow down when you feel urgency.** The feeling of needing to act *right now* is the attack working. Treat it as the alarm itself.
5. **Use a password manager as a phishing detector.** It fills credentials based on the domain — so if it doesn't offer to fill, that's not the real site.

## If you clicked
<!-- bite: a64db8cd-1005-4d27-b552-529944d32791 -->

Don't panic, and don't stay quiet — **shame is what turns a small incident into a large one.**

1. **Entered a password?** Change it immediately, everywhere you reused it, and sign out all sessions → [[cybersecurity/10-protecting-yourself/08-when-it-goes-wrong|when it goes wrong]].
2. **Entered card details?** Call your bank, freeze the card.
3. **Installed something or gave remote access?** Disconnect from the network and treat the device as compromised — a wipe and reinstall is the only reliable fix.
4. **Sent money?** Contact your bank **immediately** — same-day recall sometimes works. Report it (Action Fraud UK, IC3 US, your local equivalent).
5. **At work?** Tell IT **now**. They'd far rather hear it early. Concealment is what causes real damage.

## If you're helping someone else
<!-- bite: ef8366c1-e1df-43c5-8953-861fe3fea3d9 -->

Most people reading this will at some point be the person a parent or friend calls:

- **Don't lead with "how did you fall for that".** They'll stop telling you things, which is worse than any single scam.
- **Set the six basics up *for* them** → [[cybersecurity/10-protecting-yourself/01-your-actual-threat-model|the six things]]. Doing beats explaining.
- **Give one rule they'll remember:** *"If it's urgent and about money, hang up and call me."*
- **Agree the family code word.**

## Terms used in this lesson
<!-- bite: d7fec735-41cd-4553-b184-7b78b3018852 -->

1. **Phishing**: This is a fake message impersonating someone you trust, to make you click, pay, or hand over a code or password.
2. **Manufactured urgency**: This is the artificial time pressure ("act within 24 hours") that is the core of nearly every scam — it exists to stop you thinking.
3. **Out-of-band verification**: This is checking an urgent request through a channel *you* chose (a number you already had), not the contact details in the message.
4. **Spear phishing**: This is a targeted phishing attack using real researched details about you, far more convincing than bulk phishing.

## Terms used in this lesson: the costly varieties
<!-- bite: 8e4491d9-bd40-43f2-98c5-46afee7e5f69 -->

5. **Business email compromise (BEC)**: This is a scam that emails new bank details for a real invoice from a hacked account — the most expensive category by value; verify payment changes by phone.
6. **Vishing / smishing**: Vishing is voice-call phishing (now boosted by AI voice cloning); smishing is text-message phishing.
7. **Pig butchering**: This is long-game romance-and-investment fraud — weeks of relationship, then a fake platform showing fake gains; the largest category by money lost.
8. **Family code word**: This is a private word your family agrees for verifying urgent calls, which defeats AI voice-cloning.

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="6ac29c00" -->
   What is the single most reliable indicator that a message is a scam?

   - [ ] A spelling mistake.
   - [x] Manufactured urgency — pressure to act *right now* or face a threat, which exists to stop you thinking.
   - [ ] A professional-looking logo.
   - [ ] It arrived by email rather than post.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Urgency is the core of nearly every scam: "within 24 hours", "suspicious activity", "your parcel is held". The pressure is the exploit — it's designed to make you act before you check. Treat the feeling of needing to act immediately as the alarm itself. Spelling and design used to be tells but AI has removed both, so "it looked professional" proves nothing.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="192b9e10" -->
   Why are "bad spelling" and "poor design" no longer useful ways to spot phishing?

   - [ ] Scammers now hire professional writers.
   - [x] AI writes flawless, well-designed messages, so modern phishing is pixel-perfect — "it looked professional" is no longer evidence it's real.
   - [ ] Email providers fix the spelling automatically.
   - [ ] They were never useful.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   AI has made polished, perfectly-written phishing trivial to produce, so the old "it had typos / looked amateurish" heuristics now give false reassurance. This is exactly why the defence shifted to a *habit* (never act from the message, verify out of band) rather than trying to spot the fake — the fake will look real.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: gap id="420da3ba" answers="out of band|out-of-band" -->
   The second core habit against phishing is to verify any urgent request about money or credentials through a channel you chose yourself — a number you already had, not the one in the message. This is called verifying ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Out of band (through a different, self-chosen channel). The first habit is never acting from a link in a message; the second is, if you must check, to use contact details you already had. If the request is genuine, a five-minute delay to call the number on the back of your card costs nothing.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="6673dcae" -->
   A supplier your company pays emails to say their bank details have changed, please use the new account for the outstanding invoice. The email is genuinely from their address. What should you do?

   - [ ] Pay to the new account — the email is from the real address.
   - [x] Verify the change by phone on a number you already had, because their account may be compromised (this is business email compromise).
   - [ ] Reply to the email asking them to confirm.
   - [ ] Ignore it and pay the old account.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   This is business email compromise, the most expensive scam category by value. The email really is from the supplier — because their account was hacked — so replying just reaches the attacker. Always verify a change of payment details out of band, by phone on a number you already had. Paying to the new account sends the money straight to the attacker.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="53fb33e5" -->
   How can a password manager act as a phishing detector?

   - [ ] It scans emails for scam keywords.
   - [x] It only offers to fill your credentials on the domain they belong to — so if you're on a fake lookalike site, it won't offer to fill, which is a warning.
   - [ ] It blocks all links in messages.
   - [ ] It calls your bank to verify.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A manager ties each saved login to its real domain, so on `g00gle.com` it simply won't offer your Google password. That silence is a useful signal: if the manager doesn't recognise the site, it's probably not the real one. It's a helpful side effect of using a manager, on top of killing reuse.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="7b8d4f0b" -->
   Phishing is called an attack on "the person, not the computer". Explain what that means and why it implies the best defences are habits rather than software.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Phishing doesn't exploit a flaw in your device — it exploits *you*, by manufacturing urgency and impersonating someone you trust so you click, pay, or hand over a code of your own accord. The "vulnerability" is human judgement under pressure, not a bug software can patch. That's why no antivirus or filter fully stops it and why technical skill doesn't make you immune — a security expert in a hurry can be fooled by a convincing fake. The defences that actually work are procedural habits that don't depend on spotting the fake: never act from a link in a message (open the app or type the address yourself), and verify anything urgent about money or credentials through a channel you chose (a number you already had). These require no expertise, work even when the message looks perfect (which, thanks to AI, it now does), and defeat the overwhelming majority of what will ever be aimed at you. Because the attack targets the person, the fix lives in the person's habits, not in a product.

   </details>

## Practice — audit a message and agree a code word
<!-- bite: a4dd422b-0d00-4c42-a526-91817db3eed2 -->

**Task.** Make the habits real.

1. Find a recent suspicious message (everyone has one). Without clicking anything, list its tells — urgency, link mismatch, broken procedure.
2. For a message claiming to be from a service you use, practise the right move: open the app or type the address yourself and check whether the claim is true.
3. **Agree a family code word** with the people you'd help — a word only you know, for verifying urgent calls.
4. Save one rule where you'll see it: *"If it's urgent and about money, stop and verify out of band."*

## Practice: when it's done
<!-- bite: cd60c731-5509-40dc-b5ca-b1d3422a7e1a -->

Here's how you know the task is finished.

**Done when:** you can name the tells in a real message, you've verified a claim by going to the source yourself instead of using its link, and you've agreed a family code word. The deeper done-state is a reflex: the next time a message makes you feel you must act *right now*, that feeling makes you slow down instead of speed up.
