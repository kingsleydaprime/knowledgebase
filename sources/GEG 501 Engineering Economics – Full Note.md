## 1. Introduction to engineering economics

Engineering economics is the use of economic ideas and tools to judge engineering options and help you decide between them. An engineering solution is only good if it also makes financial sense, so this course teaches you how to put numbers on that.

**Definition (your lecture version):** the application of economic concepts, principles and analytical tools to evaluate engineering options and guide decision making. Another wording in your notes: the application of economic techniques to the evaluation of design and engineering alternatives. Its job is to check whether a project is appropriate, estimate its value, and justify it from an engineering point of view.

The starting idea behind all economics is **scarcity**: resources are limited, so you must choose between alternatives. Economics is the study of that decision making, and engineering economics applies it to projects.

### Scope

- Project evaluation: will the project bring benefits that justify the investment?
- Comparing alternatives: choosing the best among competing options.
- Cost estimation.
- Risk assessment.
- Profit (profitability) analysis.
- Resource allocation and optimization.

### Engineering economics vs traditional economics

Engineering economics borrows from general economic theory, but it is used differently.

| Point | Engineering economics | Traditional economics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Project specific, technical and financial decisions | Broad issues (macro) |
| Time horizon | Long term, a project life of about 3 to 30 years | Short-term market behaviour |
| Method | Quantitative: explicit numbers, cost projections | Qualitative reasoning and theoretical models |
| Use | Practical, applied engineering | Theoretical and policy oriented |

### Role of engineers in decision making

Engineers are decision makers, not just designers. They:

1. Carry out feasibility studies (technical and economic) to compare alternative designs and investments.
2. Select materials and processes that cut cost and environmental impact.
3. Design for life cycle cost: initial, maintenance, operating and disposal cost.
4. Advise management on capital investment, for example buying new equipment or upgrading old.
5. Make replacement decisions by analysing maintenance, repair cost and replacement strategies for ageing assets.

### Steps in engineering economic decision making

These steps follow one another, and each one feeds the next.

1. Identify the problem or investment need. Define the alternatives, constraints and objectives clearly.
2. Develop feasible alternatives. Every problem has several possible solutions.
3. Estimate the cash flows: initial cost, operating cost, maintenance cost, salvage value, and so on.
4. Compare the options using economic criteria: present worth, annual worth, internal rate of return, benefit-cost ratio.
5. Consider risk and uncertainty, using sensitivity analysis or probability analysis.
6. Select the best option, the one that meets the technical and economic goals.
7. Implement and monitor performance, so the project delivers the expected result.

### Applications

- Facility planning: plant location, capacity and layout.
- Make-or-buy decisions: build or outsource.
- Equipment replacement: the best time to replace machines.
- Energy efficiency projects: judging solar, wind or other energy-saving technology.
- Infrastructure and production planning: pipelines, bridges, roads, rail.

Engineering economics gives engineers the tools to make decisions that are technically sound and financially justified. It is the backbone of project evaluation, capital budgeting and optimization.

## 2. Cost concepts

Every decision in engineering economics is a comparison of costs and benefits, so you need to know the types of cost. A cost is a resource you give up to achieve something.

### Basic cost concepts (from your lectures)

| Cost | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed cost | Stays the same whatever the output | Rent, salary of permanent staff, insurance |
| Variable cost | Changes with the level of output or activity | Raw materials: build more bridges, buy more material |
| Sunk cost | Already committed and cannot be recovered, so it should not affect future decisions | A government fee paid before you build a refinery |
| Opportunity cost | The value of the next best alternative you give up | Money put in one project cannot be used in another |
| Incremental cost | The extra cost of one specific action | Expanding production, adopting a new technology |

A few connections between these:

- Because resources are scarce, every choice has an opportunity cost. That is why opportunity cost forces engineers to think about trade-offs. It is a concept, so you usually cannot put a figure on it.
- Variable cost can go up or down with output. Incremental cost deals only with the **increase** caused by a decision, and it can include a new fixed cost (for example buying a new machine to expand).
- Variable cost is tied to production only. Maintaining a car is not a variable cost of production.
- Sunk costs are ignored in comparisons because they are the same whichever option you pick. This links to "relevant cash flows only" in the section on comparing alternatives.

A simple cost equation shows fixed and variable together: **Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × output**. For example y = 10 + 5x has a fixed cost of 10 (the intercept) and a variable cost of 5 per unit (the slope).

### Other cost classifications (from the textbook material)

- **Semi-variable (mixed) cost:** has a fixed part and a variable part. A phone bill is the usual example: a fixed line rental plus a charge for use. It moves in the same direction as output but not in direct proportion.
- **Direct and indirect cost:** direct costs can be traced to a product (raw materials, wages of the workers who make it). Indirect costs are shared overheads (factory manager's salary, rent, supervision, security, utilities, depreciation of machines).
- **Product and period cost:** product costs are tied to making the goods (direct materials, direct labour, variable factory overhead). Period costs are not tied to production, such as fixed costs for the period.
- **Relevant and irrelevant cost:** relevant costs change with a decision (incremental costs, opportunity costs, out-of-pocket costs). Irrelevant costs will not change whatever you decide, so you ignore them.
- **Real cost:** the sacrifice society makes to produce something (the worker's effort, pollution, the saver's waiting), beyond the money paid. It is hard to measure, so money cost is used in practice.

### Average and marginal cost

- **Average cost** = total cost ÷ output. It equals average fixed cost plus average variable cost. Example: total cost of 1,000 for 100 units gives an average cost of 10.
- **Marginal cost** = the extra cost of one more unit: MC = TC(n) − TC(n−1). It depends on variable cost only, because producing one more unit adds nothing to fixed cost.

### Elements of cost and the cost sheet

The total cost is built up in layers. Each layer adds to the one before.

| Layer | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Prime cost | Direct material + direct labour (+ direct expenses) |
| Works (factory) cost | Prime cost + works (factory) overhead |
| Cost of production | Works cost + administrative overhead |
| Total cost of sales | Cost of production + selling and distribution overhead |
| Profit or loss | Sales − total cost |

**Worked example (textbook):** direct material 7,000; direct labour 2,800; factory expenses 2,600; administrative expenses 1,000; selling expenses 900; sales 20,000.

- Prime cost = 7,000 + 2,800 = 9,800
- Works cost = 9,800 + 2,600 = 12,400
- Cost of production = 12,400 + 1,000 = 13,400
- Total cost = 13,400 + 900 = 14,300
- Profit = 20,000 − 14,300 = 5,700

The full list of costs laid out like this, step by step, is called a **cost sheet**.

## 3. Time value of money

Costs and benefits in a project happen at different times, so you cannot add them up directly. Time value of money gives you the rules to bring them to the same point in time first. Everything after this section depends on it.

**Core idea:** a naira today is worth more than a naira in the future. It is like "a bird in the hand is worth ten in the bush". Four reasons:

1. **Interest:** money today can be invested to earn a return.
2. **Inflation:** prices rise, so the same naira buys less later.
3. **Opportunity cost:** money locked in one project cannot be used elsewhere.
4. **Risk:** future cash flows are uncertain, while what you hold today is certain.

So the value of money depends on time. Interest is simply the rent paid for using someone's money.

### Key terms

- **Present value (PV or P):** what a future sum is worth today.
- **Future value (FV or F):** what a present sum will grow to when invested.
- **Discounting:** converting future cash flows to present value.
- **Compounding:** projecting present money into the future.
- **Cash flows:** the series of inflows (benefits) and outflows (costs) over time.

### Cash flows and the cash flow diagram

A project's money movements fall into these groups: first cost (to build or buy and install), operations and maintenance (O&M, yearly costs like power, labour, minor repairs), salvage value (what you get when you sell the equipment at the end), revenues (yearly receipts from sales), and overhaul (a big expense during the asset's life).

A **cash flow diagram** draws these on a time line. Rules:

- Draw a horizontal line divided into periods. A downward arrow is a cost, an upward arrow is a benefit, drawn to scale.
- The end of period t is the same point as the start of period t+1.
- Rent, lease and insurance are usually start-of-period flows. O&M, salvage, revenue and overhaul are end-of-period flows.
- Time 0 is your choice (when you analyse, when funding is approved, or when construction starts).
- One person's outflow is another person's inflow.
- Show two cash flows in the same year separately, so each links back to the problem statement.

**Example:** you borrow ₦1,000 at 8%. At the end of year 1 you repay half the principal plus interest, and at the end of year 2 the other half plus interest.

| Year | Cash flow |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | +1,000 |
| 1 | −580 (500 principal + 80 interest) |
| 2 | −540 (500 principal + 40 interest) |

### Simple interest

Interest is worked out on the original principal only.

```latex
I = P \times i \times n \qquad F = P(1 + in)
```

P is the principal, i the rate per period, n the number of periods. **Example:** ₦200,000 at 5% simple interest for 4 years. I = 200,000 × 0.05 × 4 = ₦40,000, so the total is ₦240,000.

Simple interest is rarely used in engineering economics because real investments do not behave this way. For example, bonds pay more than keeping money in the bank, so people do not leave money idle.

### Compound interest

Interest is earned on the principal **and** on interest already earned. This is how money grows in reality, so almost every analysis uses it. It is used for loans, mortgages, equipment leasing, and for how savings and costs grow.

```latex
F = P(1+i)^n \qquad P = \frac{F}{(1+i)^n}
```

**Examples:**

- ₦500,000 at 8% for 5 years: F = 500,000 × (1.08)^5 = ₦734,664.04.
- $100 at 9% for 2 years: F = 100 × (1.09)^2 = $118.81, so the interest earned is $18.81 (simple interest would give only $18).
- A loan of ₦1,000,000 at 10% for 5 years: if nothing is repaid until the end, you owe 1,000,000 × 1.1^5 = ₦1,610,510.

### Economic equivalence

Cash flows at different times are equivalent if they have the same value once converted to a common time. For example, ₦100k today may be equivalent to ₦140k in 4 years, depending on the interest rate. Equivalence depends on the interest rate.

How you use it to compare two plans:

1. Convert plan 1 to one value at one point in time.
2. Convert plan 2 to one value at the same point, with the same interest rate.
3. Compare the two values.

**Example (slides):** Plan 1 pays ₦1,000 a year for 5 years (total 5,000). Plan 2 pays ₦5,000 now (total 5,000). At 10%, the first plan has a present value of 1,000 × 3.791 = 3,791. Plan 2 is worth 5,000 today, so it is better, even though the totals are equal. This links straight to the interest factors in the next section.

## 4. The interest factors

The formulas from the last section get repeated in almost every problem, so engineers turn them into **factors** you can read from a table or compute once. Each factor converts money from one point in time to another. The name reads "find the first, given the second": P/F means present given future.

**Notation:** F = P(F/P, i, n) means F equals P times the F/P factor at interest rate i for n periods. Here P is a present sum, F a future sum, A an equal payment at the end of each period, i the interest rate per period and n the number of periods.

### The six main factors

| Factor | Name | Formula | Use it to find |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P/F | Present worth (single payment) | 1/(1+i)^n | Today's value of one future amount |
| F/P | Future worth (compound amount) | (1+i)^n | Future value of one present amount |
| P/A | Present worth of annuity | \[(1+i)^n − 1\] / \[i(1+i)^n\] | Today's value of equal yearly amounts |
| A/P | Capital recovery | i(1+i)^n / \[(1+i)^n − 1\] | Equal yearly amount from a present sum |
| F/A | Future worth of annuity | \[(1+i)^n − 1\] / i | Future value of equal yearly deposits |
| A/F | Sinking fund | i / \[(1+i)^n − 1\] | Equal yearly amount needed to reach a future sum |

Notice the pairs. P/F and F/P are inverses of each other. P/A and A/P are inverses. F/A and A/F are inverses. Also, A/P = A/F + i, which is a useful check.

### Worked examples (from your lectures)

Always say what the answer means. Your lecturer stressed that interpretation is the most important part.

**1. P/F:** what is ₦500,000 payable in 3 years worth today at 10%? P/F = 1/1.1^3 = 0.7513, so P = 500,000 × 0.7513 = **₦375,650**. Meaning: ₦375,650 invested today grows to ₦500,000 in 3 years.

**2. F/P:** what does ₦500,000 grow to at 8% in 5 years? F/P = 1.08^5 = 1.4693, so F = 500,000 × 1.4693 = **₦734,650** (the exact figure is ₦734,664, because 1.4693 is rounded).

**3. P/A:** a project saves ₦200,000 a year for 4 years at 10%. What are the savings worth today? P/A = 3.1699, so P = 200,000 × 3.1699 = **₦633,980**. Meaning: 4 years of savings are worth ₦633,980 today.

**4. A/P:** a ₦1,000,000 loan over 5 years at 10%. What is the yearly repayment? A/P = 0.2638, so A = 1,000,000 × 0.2638 = **₦263,800 a year** for 5 years (exact: ₦263,797.48). The total repaid is more than ₦1,000,000 because of interest on the balance.

**5. F/A:** you save ₦100,000 a year at 10% for 4 years. What do you have at the end? F/A = 4.641, so F = **₦464,100**.

**6. A/F:** you need ₦1,000,000 in 5 years at 10%. How much do you save each year? A/F = 0.1638, so A = **₦163,800 a year**. This is how a sinking fund or a replacement reserve works.

### When to use each one

- **P/A and A/P** go together when you work with loans, mortgage payments, installments, lease payments, subscriptions or yearly maintenance fees. Engineers use annuities to compare projects that have yearly costs.
- **F/A and A/F** are for saving up to a future cost: replacement funds, reserve funds, sinking funds.
- **P/F and F/P** are for single amounts.

### Gradient series

In a gradient series the cash flow **increases by a constant amount G every period**, starting from the second year (for example maintenance cost rising ₦10k, ₦20k, ₦30k, so G = ₦10k). It suits rising maintenance, labour or utility cost.

```latex
(A/G, i, n) = \frac{1}{i} - \frac{n}{(1+i)^n - 1} \qquad (P/G, i, n) = \frac{(1+i)^n - 1 - ni}{i^2 (1+i)^n}
```

A/G converts the gradient into an equal yearly amount. P/G converts it to a present value. If there is also a base amount, handle it as an ordinary annuity and add the gradient part.

### Using the factors

- You can use an interest table, a calculator or a spreadsheet. In a spreadsheet: P = PV(i, n, A, F), F = FV(i, n, A, P), i = RATE(n, A, P, F).
- Small differences between table and formula answers are normal. Do not worry about exact digits.
- Factors can be combined. For example (F/G) = (P/G)(F/P).

## 5. Nominal and effective interest, inflation and tax

The factors in section 4 assume the interest rate matches the period (a yearly rate for yearly payments) and that prices stay steady. This section fixes both assumptions. It also connects to inflation, which is one of the reasons money loses value over time.

### Nominal and effective interest

When interest is compounded more than once a year, the stated yearly rate (the **nominal rate**, r) is not what you really earn. The **effective rate** is.

```latex
i_{eff} = \left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{m} - 1
```

m is the number of compounding periods per year. The rate per period is r/m.

- **Example (textbook):** 5.25% a year compounded daily (m = 365). i\_eff = (1 + 0.0525/365)^365 − 1 = 0.0539, so about **5.39%**.
- **Example:** 12% a year compounded monthly gives (1.01)^12 − 1 = 12.68%.

If n is counted in the same periods as the rate per period, you can use the normal factors with i = r/m and n = number of periods.

### Continuous compounding

As m becomes very large, compounding is continuous. The formulas become:

```latex
i_{eff} = e^{r} - 1 \qquad F = Pe^{rn} \qquad P = Fe^{-rn}
```

### Capitalized cost (a project with no end)

For something that lasts forever (a bridge, a dam), the P/A factor tends to 1/i as n grows. So the present sum that pays an annual cost A for ever without touching the principal is:

```latex
P = \frac{A}{i}
```

**Example (FE review):** a cemetery plot costs $75 a year to maintain. At 6%, you must set aside 75 ÷ 0.06 = **$1,250**. Capitalized cost = first cost + A/i.

### Inflation

**Inflation** is a **sustained increase** in the general price level of goods and services **over time**, which reduces the purchasing power of money. Both words matter: a one-off jump in one price is not inflation.

- **Price level (P)** is the absolute level of prices in an economy.
- **Inflation rate** is the percentage change in the price level:

```latex
\pi_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \times 100
```

- It is measured with three indices: the **consumer price index (CPI)**, the **GDP deflator** and the **producer price index (PPI)**.

**Types of inflation:**

1. **Demand-pull:** demand is greater than supply.
2. **Cost-push:** rising production costs push prices up.
3. **Built-in:** a wage-price spiral, where higher wages raise prices and higher prices raise wages.
4. **By speed** (slowest to fastest): creeping, walking, galloping, hyperinflation.

### Nominal and real values

- **Nominal value** is measured in current prices and ignores inflation (nominal wage, nominal interest rate).
- **Real value** is measured in constant prices and accounts for inflation.

```latex
\text{Real value} = \frac{\text{Nominal value}}{\text{Price index}}
```

### Real interest rate (Fisher equation)

The nominal rate has two parts: the real return and compensation for inflation.

```latex
1 + i_{nominal} = (1 + i_{real})(1 + f)
```

- **Example (lecture 3):** nominal 12%, inflation 4%. 1.12 = (1 + i\_real)(1.04), so i\_real = 1.12/1.04 − 1 = 0.0769, which is **7.69%**.
- **Quick approximation:** i\_real ≈ i\_nominal − f, and i\_nominal ≈ i\_real + f. With 15% nominal and 10% inflation, i\_real ≈ 5% (the exact answer is 4.55%). The approximation is fine when rates are small.
- If a question asks for the combined rate going the other way (real plus inflation), use d = i + f + if. **Example (FE review):** the uninflated present worth of $2,000 in 2 years at i = 10% and f = 6% uses d = 0.10 + 0.06 + 0.006 = 0.166, so P = 2,000 × (1.166)^−2 = $1,471.

This matters because it lets you compare equipment cost across years, judge salaries, project fuel cost, and assess how maintenance cost grows.

### Inflation and tax

Taxes are charged on **nominal** values, but your welfare depends on **real** values. So inflation can raise your tax without raising your real income.

- If nominal income rises 12% and inflation is 10%, real income rises only about 2%.
- In a progressive tax system (higher income, higher tax), inflation pushes you into a higher bracket, you pay more tax, and your real disposable income falls. This is **bracket creep**.
- **Example:** the tax threshold is ₦1,000,000 and your income is ₦950,000. After 10% inflation your income is 950,000 × 1.1 = ₦1,045,000, now over the threshold, yet your purchasing power has not changed.
- Income rising by the same percentage as prices means no real gain. Mistaking the bigger number for a real gain is **money illusion**.

**Indexation of tax brackets** fixes this by adjusting thresholds each year using a price index, usually the CPI. With 10% inflation, the ₦1,000,000 threshold becomes 1,000,000 × 1.1 = ₦1,100,000, so 10% tax applies up to ₦1,100,000 and 20% above it.

Without indexation: brackets stay in nominal terms, inflation raises nominal wages even if real wages are unchanged, taxpayers cross thresholds because of inflation, government revenue rises automatically, and bracket creep occurs. Indexation is designed to solve exactly these problems.

### Inflation and company tax

1. **Depreciation** is based on historical cost, not replacement cost. During inflation the allowance is understated in real terms.
2. So taxable profit is overstated.
3. So the effective tax rate goes up.

**Inventory valuation (FIFO and LIFO):** FIFO means first in, first out (the oldest stock is sold first). LIFO means last in, first out (the newest stock is sold first).

**Worked example:** a firm buys 100 units at ₦10 (January), 100 at ₦12 (March) and 100 at ₦14 (May). It sells 150 units at ₦20 each, so revenue is ₦3,000.

- FIFO cost of goods sold = (100 × 10) + (50 × 12) = ₦1,600. Profit = 3,000 − 1,600 = ₦1,400.
- Replacing the stock at today's price costs 150 × 14 = ₦2,100. Profit at current cost = 3,000 − 2,100 = ₦900.
- So FIFO overstates profit by ₦500. Why: FIFO uses old, low costs while revenue reflects today's higher prices.

Part of that profit is only due to inflation, not real productive profit. It is called an **inventory holding gain** or paper gain. Because accounting profit is higher, the firm pays more company tax although real profit did not rise. So **inflation + FIFO = a hidden tax increase**, and the reverse happens under LIFO. That is why firms prefer LIFO during inflation.

## 6. Comparing alternatives

Now that every cash flow can be moved to one point in time, you can compare projects. The methods differ in how they put the answer (a naira value today, a naira value per year, or a percentage), but a correct analysis should lead to the same decision.

### Types of alternatives

- **Independent:** each can be accepted or rejected on its own, for example buying a house and buying a car.
- **Mutually exclusive:** choosing one rules out the others, for example solar power or a diesel generator, machine A or machine B.
- **Contingent:** acceptance depends on another project going ahead first, for example battery storage depends on a solar installation.

### Ground rules before any comparison

1. Compare mutually exclusive alternatives with each other.
2. Use the **same planning horizon** (the same useful life, or a common period).
3. Use one consistent interest rate, the MARR.
4. Use **relevant cash flows only**: future costs and benefits. Sunk costs are ignored (see section 2).

### MARR (minimum attractive rate of return)

MARR is the lowest rate of return you will accept on an investment. It is the benchmark every project is measured against. Other names: hurdle rate, cut-off rate, required rate of return. It depends on:

1. **Cost of capital:** what you pay for the money you borrow or use.
2. **Expected inflation:** the loss of purchasing power.
3. **Risk of the project:** higher risk means a higher MARR.
4. **Opportunity cost:** the return from the next best alternative.

Organisations set different MARRs. Government bodies and regulated utilities usually accept lower rates than competitive industrial firms. A cost-reduction proposal may need a lower rate than a research project, because its cash flows are more certain.

### 1. Present worth (PW) method

Convert every cash flow to time 0 and add them up. The difference between the present worth of inflows and outflows is the **net present worth (NPW)**.

```latex
PW = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+i)^t} \qquad PW = -C_0 + A\,(P/A,i,n) + S\,(P/F,i,n)
```

C0 is the first cost, A the yearly net benefit (or cost), S the salvage value and n the life.

- **One project:** PW > 0 accept (it earns more than the MARR), PW < 0 reject, PW = 0 you are indifferent.
- **Several mutually exclusive projects:** if you measure benefits, pick the highest PW. If you measure only costs, pick the least negative PW (the lowest cost).

**Example (lecture 5):** Machine A costs ₦3,000,000 and gives ₦1,200,000 a year for 4 years. Machine B costs ₦4,200,000 and gives ₦1,700,000 a year for 4 years. MARR = 10%, so P/A = 3.1699.

- PW(A) = −3,000,000 + 1,200,000 × 3.1699 = **₦803,880**
- PW(B) = −4,200,000 + 1,700,000 × 3.1699 = **₦1,188,830**

PW(B) is bigger, so Machine B is preferred.

**Example (slides, dump truck):** at 9%, first cost $50,000, operating cost $2,000 a year, income $9,000 a year, salvage $10,000, life 10 years. PW of benefits = 9,000 × 6.418 = $57,762. PW of costs = 50,000 + 2,000 × 6.418 − 10,000 × 0.4224 = $58,612. NPW = −$850, so do **not** invest. The minimum yearly benefit that makes it worthwhile is 58,612 ÷ 6.418 = $9,132.

**Limit of PW:** it only compares fairly when the alternatives have the same life. For different lives, use annual worth.

### 2. Annual worth (AW) or equivalent annual cost (EAC) method

Convert everything to an equal yearly amount over the project life. It is handy when lives differ, because you compare cost per year.

```latex
AW = PW\,(A/P,i,n) \qquad AW = -C_0\,(A/P,i,n) + A + S\,(A/F,i,n)
```

Same example: A/P at 10% for 4 years = 0.3155.

- AW(A) = −3,000,000 × 0.3155 + 1,200,000 = **₦253,500**
- AW(B) = −4,200,000 × 0.3155 + 1,700,000 = **₦374,900**

B is still preferred. This agrees with PW, as it should. AW > 0 means a yearly profit over the life of the machine.

**Example (textbook):** a machine costs 40,000, gives 10,000 a year for 10 years and has a salvage value of 5,000 at 15%. PW = −40,000 + 10,000 × 5.0188 + 5,000 × 0.2472 = 11,424. AW = 11,424 × 0.1993 = 2,277 per year. AW is positive, so accept.

**Lease vs buy (FE review, EAC):** leasing costs $0.15 per km. Buying costs $5,000, 3-year life, $1,200 salvage, $0.04 per km running cost and $500 a year insurance, at 10%. EAC(buy) = 0.04x + 500 + 5,000(0.4021) − 1,200(0.3021) = 0.04x + 2,148. Set 0.15x = 0.04x + 2,148, so x = **19,527 km** a year to break even. Drive more than that and buying is cheaper.

### 3. Future worth (FW) method

Same idea as PW, but every cash flow is moved to the **end** of the project. FW = PW × (F/P, i, n). The decision rule is the same: FW > 0 accept, and the highest FW wins.

### 4. Rate of return methods

Three rates appear in these problems:

- **MARR:** the minimum acceptable rate (above).
- **Internal rate of return (IRR):** the interest rate that makes the present worth of the project equal to zero. It is the return earned on the money still unrecovered in the project.
- **External rate of return (ERR):** the return you could realistically earn on money outside the project. It is usually set equal to the MARR.

Rule: **accept if IRR ≥ MARR**. You normally find IRR by trial and error: try rates until the benefits equal the costs.

**Example (slides):** invest ₦500, receive ₦300 in year 1, ₦300 in year 2 and ₦200 in year 3. The desired return is 15%.

| Trial rate | PW of the returns | Compare with 500 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10% | 273 + 248 + 150 = 671 | too high, try a bigger rate |
| 20% | 250 + 208 + 116 = 574 | too high |
| 30% | 231 + 178 + 91 = 500 | equal, so IRR = 30% |

IRR of 30% is above the 15% target, so accept. (The slide shows 272 + 247 + 156 = 669 for the 10% row, but the third term should be about 150 and the sum is 671. The IRR of 30% does not change. At 30% the sum is 499, which rounds to 500.)

**Cautions on IRR:**

- Ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR alone can disagree with PW, FW and AW. The consistent method is **incremental IRR**: start with the cheaper acceptable project, then check the extra investment (the difference in cash flows) against the MARR. If the extra return is above the MARR, the bigger project is better.
- Cash flows with more than one sign change can give several rates that make PW zero. A simple investment has just one sign change (minus to plus), so it has one true IRR.

### 5. Benefit-cost ratio (BCR)

Common for public projects such as roads, bridges and water supply.

```latex
BCR = \frac{PV(B)}{PV(C)}
```

- BCR > 1: accept. BCR < 1: reject. BCR = 1: break even, neither profit nor loss.
- Equivalent test: B − C ≥ 0.

**Example:** a road project has PV(B) = ₦18bn and PV(C) = ₦12bn. BCR = 1.5, so accept. Meaning: every ₦1 spent returns ₦1.50 in benefits.

**Example (FE review):** first cost $40M, capitalized perpetual annual cost $12M, capitalized benefit $49M, no residual value. B − C = 49 − 52 = −$3M, so do not undertake it.

**Mutually exclusive projects (lecture 5):** A has PV(B) = 15 and PV(C) = 10, so BCR = 1.50. B has PV(B) = 20 and PV(C) = 14, so BCR = 1.43. Both are acceptable. Your lecture picks A because it has the highest BCR.

> Caution (general knowledge, not from your notes): choosing by the highest BCR alone can mislead. The extra step from A to B costs 4 more and brings 5 more benefit, an incremental BCR of 5/4 = 1.25. That is above 1, so B adds value if money is not limited. If your exam asks you to pick "the highest BCR", follow the lecture. If it asks for the best use of money, check the incremental BCR too.

### 6. Payback period

The time needed for the cash inflows to recover the first cost. It is simple and shows how fast you get your money back, but it ignores what happens after payback. The **discounted payback** does the same after discounting each inflow, so it is always longer. Example from the SFPE chapter: undiscounted, sprinklers were said to pay back in 4 to 7 years, but after discounting at 10% the payback was 5 to 11 years.

### Quick guide: which method?

| Method | Answer is | Accept if | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PW or NPW | A naira value today | PW > 0, pick the highest | Same lives |
| AW or EAC | A naira value per year | AW > 0, pick the highest (or the lowest cost) | Different lives |
| FW | A naira value at the end | FW > 0 | You care about value at the end |
| IRR | A percentage | IRR ≥ MARR | You want a rate (use incremental IRR to rank) |
| BCR | A ratio | BCR > 1 | Public projects |
| Payback | Years | Short enough | A quick screen only |

## 7. Depreciation

In the last section you compared projects using cash flows. Depreciation is different: it is not a cash flow, but it changes tax, profit and replacement decisions, so it still affects your cash analysis.

**Definition:** depreciation is the reduction in the economic value of an asset over time because of use, wear and tear, obsolescence or the passage of time. It is an **accounting allocation, not a cash outflow**. Instead of charging the whole price of an asset in the year you buy it, you spread the cost over its life. Land is never depreciated.

From an economics view, depreciation reflects loss of service potential, not only physical decay.

### Causes of depreciation

| Cause | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Physical deterioration | Use, wear and tear, corrosion, exposure to weather |
| Functional obsolescence | The asset no longer meets the need: demand changed, a more efficient unit exists, or the work exceeds its capacity |
| Technological obsolescence | New technology makes the old asset uneconomical |
| Economic obsolescence | Changes in demand, regulation or market conditions |
| Time-related | A lease, patent or copyright expires, and assets age even without use |
| Accident | Sudden failure or damage |
| Depletion | Using up natural resources: oil, timber, minerals |
| Monetary | Price rises make replacement more expensive than the money recovered |
| Deferred maintenance | Value falls quickly when maintenance is delayed |

### Terms you must know

- **First cost (FC or P):** the purchase and installation cost (price plus taxes and transport).
- **Salvage value (SV or S):** the expected value at the end of useful life.
- **Book value (BV):** the value recorded in the accounts at a given time = first cost minus the depreciation charged so far.
- **Depreciable amount:** FC − SV.
- **Useful life (n):** expected service life in years or units.
- **Depreciation charge (D):** the amount allocated each year.

Property is depreciable if it is used in business to produce income, it wears out or loses value, and it has a life longer than one year.

### Why we depreciate

1. Cost-based decisions.
2. Working out taxable income. Depreciation is deductible, so it reduces tax.
3. Estimating true project profit.
4. Replacement analysis.
5. Asset valuation and reporting.

**Replacement analysis (defender and challenger):** the **defender** is the asset you already own and the **challenger** is the new one you could buy. Compare them using relevant future cash flows (not sunk costs) and pick the one with the better net present value.

### The methods

There are three groups: **time-based** (straight line), **usage-based** (units of production) and **accelerated** (declining balance, sum-of-years digits). Every method writes off the same total, FC − SV, but at a different pace.

#### 1. Straight line (SL)

Equal depreciation every year.

```latex
D = \frac{FC - SV}{n}
```

- **Example (lecture 6):** FC = ₦5,000,000, SV = ₦500,000, n = 5. D = 4,500,000 ÷ 5 = **₦900,000 a year**.
- **Example (lecture 6 class work):** installed cost ₦12,000,000, salvage ₦2,000,000, 5 years. D = **₦2,000,000 a year**.

| Year | Depreciation | Accumulated | Book value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 12,000,000 |
| 1 | 2,000,000 | 2,000,000 | 10,000,000 |
| 2 | 2,000,000 | 4,000,000 | 8,000,000 |
| 3 | 2,000,000 | 6,000,000 | 6,000,000 |
| 4 | 2,000,000 | 8,000,000 | 4,000,000 |
| 5 | 2,000,000 | 10,000,000 | 2,000,000 |

Notice the book value ends exactly at the salvage value.

- **Lecture 3 example:** P = ₦2,000,000, S = ₦200,000, n = 5. D = (2,000,000 − 200,000)/5 = ₦360,000 a year.
- **FE review:** cost $9,000, life 10 years, salvage $200. D = 8,800/10 = $880 a year, so book value after 3 years is 9,000 − 3 × 880 = $6,360.

#### 2. Declining balance (DB) and double declining balance (DDB)

Depreciation is highest in the early years and falls over time, because each year you take a fixed percentage of the **book value** at the start of the year.

```latex
D_t = k \times BV_{t-1} \qquad BV_t = BV_{t-1} - D_t
```

- For plain declining balance k is the rate given in the question.
- For **double declining balance**, k = 2/n (double the straight-line rate). If a question says "use the double declining rate", use k = 2/n.
- Salvage value is not used in the formula, but book value must **never fall below salvage value**. In the year it would, only depreciate down to the salvage value.
- It is popular for tax purposes because it gives bigger deductions early.

**Example (lecture 6, DDB):** an asset costs ₦8,500,000, life 5 years, salvage ₦500,000. k = 2/5 = 0.4 (40%).

| Year | Depreciation (0.4 × opening BV) | Closing book value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3,400,000 | 5,100,000 |
| 2 | 2,040,000 | 3,060,000 |
| 3 | 1,224,000 | 1,836,000 |
| 4 | 734,400 | 1,101,600 |
| 5 | 440,640 | 660,960 |

Book value after year 5 (660,960) is still above salvage value (500,000), so no adjustment is needed.

#### 3. Sum-of-the-years digits (SYD)

Another accelerated method. Write the digits of the life, add them, and charge each year a fraction using the remaining life.

```latex
SYD = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} \qquad D_t = \frac{RF}{SYD}\,(FC - SV)
```

RF is the remaining life at the start of the year (n for year 1, n−1 for year 2, and so on).

**Example (lecture 6):** a generator costs ₦6,000,000, salvage ₦600,000, life 4 years. The depreciable base is 5,400,000 and SYD = 4 × 5/2 = 10.

| Year | RF | Fraction | Depreciation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 4 | 4/10 | 2,160,000 |
| 2 | 3 | 3/10 | 1,620,000 |
| 3 | 2 | 2/10 | 1,080,000 |
| 4 | 1 | 1/10 | 540,000 |

The total is 5,400,000, which equals FC − SV. Always use this as your check.

#### 4. Units of production (usage-based)

Depreciation follows how much the asset is used, not time.

```latex
\text{Depreciation per unit} = \frac{FC - SV}{\text{total expected units}} \qquad D_t = \text{per unit} \times \text{units produced in year } t
```

**Class exercise (lecture 6):** a machine costs ₦15,000,000, salvage ₦3,000,000, expected output 120,000 units. Actual output is 20,000 (year 1), 30,000 (year 2) and 25,000 (year 3). Find the depreciation each year and the book value after year 3.

- Per unit = 12,000,000 ÷ 120,000 = ₦100
- Year 1: 20,000 × 100 = ₦2,000,000
- Year 2: 30,000 × 100 = ₦3,000,000
- Year 3: 25,000 × 100 = ₦2,500,000
- Book value after year 3 = 15,000,000 − 7,500,000 = **₦7,500,000**

(The cost was hard to read in your lecture note. A classmate's note shows ₦15,000,000, which is what I used.)

#### Other: ACRS

The Accelerated Cost Recovery System (used in the FE review slides) takes the depreciation each year as cost times a table factor, and ignores salvage value. Example: cost $9,000 with factors 0.10, 0.18, 0.144 gives $900, $1,620 and $1,296, so book value after 3 years is $5,184. Compare with $6,360 under straight line.

### Handy relationships

- Accumulated depreciation (AD) at year t = AD(t−1) + D(t).
- Book value at year t = BV(t−1) − D(t) = FC − AD(t).

### Depreciation and tax

- Expenses and depreciation are tax-deductible, revenues are taxed. The yearly **tax saving from depreciation** = D × tax rate. That is why a fast method such as DDB brings the tax saving earlier, which has a higher present value.
- If you sell an asset for more than its book value, the difference is taxed.
- A tax credit is a one-time benefit that is subtracted from income tax.
- In inflation, depreciation on historical cost is understated, which overstates profit and tax (see section 5).

## 8. Cost estimation techniques

Every method in sections 4 to 7 needs numbers for first cost, yearly cost and salvage value. Cost estimation is how you get those numbers, so a weak estimate makes the whole analysis meaningless.

**Definition:** cost estimation is the systematic process of forecasting the financial resources needed to complete a project, produce a product or deliver a service over its life cycle.

**It is used for:** project feasibility analysis, capital budgeting, comparing alternative projects, pricing and bidding, and budget control and cost management.

**Poor estimates lead to:** cost overruns (actual cost above the estimate), project abandonment and inefficient use of resources.

### Costs to estimate

1. **Initial (capital) cost:** land, equipment, installation, commissioning.
2. **Operating and maintenance (O&M) cost:** labour, energy and fuel, repairs and spare parts, insurance, administration.
3. **Fixed vs variable cost** (see section 2).
4. **Direct vs indirect cost** (see section 2).

Which technique you use depends on the **project stage**, the **data available** and the **accuracy you need**. They range from simple judgement to quantitative methods.

### A. Qualitative (judgement-based) techniques

**1. Expert judgement.** You rely on the experience and intuition of experts: consult experienced engineers or managers, use historical knowledge, and adjust for the situation. Example: an engineer estimates a small water treatment plant at about ₦450M from similar past projects.

- Advantages: fast, cheap, useful when data is scarce.
- Limits: very subjective, prone to bias and optimism.

**2. Analogy (comparative) estimation.** You estimate by comparing with similar completed projects.

```latex
\text{Estimated cost} = \text{Cost of similar project} \times \text{adjustment factor}
```

Example: a 5 km road cost ₦1M, so a 7 km road is estimated at 1M × 7/5 = ₦1.4M.

- Advantages: simple, useful in early planning.
- Limits: accuracy depends on how similar the projects are, and it ignores technology change.

### B. Quantitative techniques

**1. Parametric estimation.** Uses a statistical relationship between cost and a key parameter such as size, capacity or power.

```latex
C = aX^{b}
```

X is the cost-driving parameter. a and b are constants found from historical data. Example: C = 500X^0.6 with X = 16 gives 500 × 16^0.6 = **2,639**.

- Advantages: more objective, good for preliminary design.
- Limits: needs reliable historical data and assumes stable cost relationships.

**2. Capacity factoring (power-sizing model).** Used when cost scales with capacity.

```latex
C_2 = C_1\left(\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}\right)^{n}
```

n is the cost-capacity exponent, usually between 0.5 and 0.8. Example: a 100 MW power plant costs ₦120B. The cost of a 200 MW plant with n = 0.6 is 120 × (200/100)^0.6 = 120 × 1.5157 ≈ **₦182B**. Doubling the size does not double the cost, which is the idea of economies of scale.

- Advantages: widely used, captures economies of scale.
- Limits: sensitive to the exponent you choose.

### C. Detailed (bottom-up) estimation

The most accurate technique. Break the project into components, estimate the cost of each, and add them up.

| Item | Cost (₦ million) |
| --- | --- |
| Equipment | 120 |
| Installation | 40 |
| Labour | 30 |
| Utility | 10 |
| **Total** | **200** |

Advantages: high accuracy and good for final budgeting. Limits: time-consuming and needs a detailed design.

### Adjusting old costs for inflation (cost index method)

When you use historical cost data, prices have changed since then, so you adjust with an index.

```latex
C_{new} = C_{old} \times \frac{I_{new}}{I_{old}}
```

Example: a project cost ₦80M in 2018 when the index was 150. In 2024 the index is 210, so the cost is 80 × 210/150 = **₦112M**. Common indexes: consumer price index (CPI), construction cost index (CCI) and producer price index (PPI). This connects to inflation in section 5.

### Learning curve (experience curve)

Cost per unit falls as production experience grows.

```latex
Y = aX^{b} \quad (b < 0)
```

Y is the cost per unit and X is the cumulative output. If labour hours fall 20% each time output doubles, an **80% learning curve** applies. It is used in manufacturing, defence projects and aircraft production.

### Causes of error in cost estimation

1. Ignoring inflation.
2. Over-optimism bias.
3. Poor data quality.
4. **Scope creep:** the project quietly grows beyond what was planned and costed (your lecturer asked you to learn more about this).
5. Technological uncertainty.

### Best practices

Use several methods, update estimates as the project evolves, document your adjustments clearly, include contingency allowances, and carry out sensitivity analysis.

**Main takeaways:** cost estimation is the foundation of engineering economics. Detailed estimation gives accuracy while parametric methods give speed. Adjusting for inflation and scale is essential. Reliable estimates reduce financial project risk.

## 9. Risk and uncertainty

All the earlier sections used estimated numbers as if they were certain. In reality the future is not certain, so this section shows how to deal with that.

### Risk vs uncertainty

- **Risk:** the outcome is unknown, but the probability of each outcome is **known or can be estimated**, because there is data (historical records, failure records, market data). Example: steel price has a 40% chance of rising and 60% of falling; a generator has a 10% chance of needing extra repairs each year. A fair coin and the stock market are the textbook examples.
- **Uncertainty:** the outcome is unknown and the **probability is also unknown**, because there is no history or the situation is new. Example: a brand-new technology, a sudden government policy, a plant in a region with no weather records.

(In one place the textbook defines uncertainty the opposite way. Use the definitions above, which match your lecture.)

Decisions can be made under **certainty** (you know the alternatives and outcomes, which is rare), **risk** or **uncertainty**. Uncertainty comes in two forms. With **partial ignorance** you can assign subjective probabilities, so it becomes a risk problem. With **complete ignorance** you cannot, so you need the special criteria later in this section.

### The 5 categories of risk in engineering economics

| Risk | What it is | Typical causes | Engineering examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Market | Uncertainty from changes in market prices of inputs, outputs or materials | Global supply and demand changes, exchange rates, commodity cycles, inflation shocks, supply chain disruption | Steel price rise makes construction dearer; diesel price changes affect running cost; an oil price crash cuts a refinery's revenue |
| Technical | Failure of technology, equipment or design | Poor design or testing, manufacturing defects, ageing equipment, poor maintenance, operating conditions different from design | A machine that keeps breaking down; a new solar inverter that underperforms; unexpected corrosion in a water plant |
| Financial | Changes in financial conditions that affect cost or returns | Interest rate changes, exchange rate swings, inflation shocks, loan repayment changes, tighter credit | Borrowing cost jumps from 12% to 18%; a company that borrows in dollars sees the naira fall |
| Regulatory or political | Changes in laws or government rules | New tax laws, sudden bans, licensing rules, import duty changes | Subsidy removal sharply raises fuel cost; new import tariff on foreign machinery; new building codes force redesign |
| Environmental | Natural events or conditions that affect performance or safety | Extreme weather, flooding, drought, erosion, disasters, climate variability | Flooding damages equipment or delays work; high temperature reduces solar panel and turbine efficiency |

**Impact of each on economic decisions:**

- Market: changes cost estimates and operating cost projections, and can turn a good project into a bad one or the reverse.
- Technical: unpredictable repairs and downtime, lower output, shorter equipment life, higher replacement cost.
- Financial: lower profitability, some projects become unaffordable, loan schedules change, long-term projections become less certain.
- Regulatory: higher operating and legal cost, delays or stalls, redesign or retrofit.
- Environmental: higher insurance and repair cost, delays, unexpected replacement, less reliable projections.

Other risk types in the textbook unit: business, inflation, interest rate, credit, liquidity, derivative, cultural, currency and government policy risk.

### Measuring risk

**Expected value (mean):** the weighted average of outcomes, with probabilities as weights.

```latex
E(x) = \mu = \sum x_i p_i
```

Example: a recession has a 40% chance and gives profit of 100; no recession has 60% and gives 1,000. E = 0.4(100) + 0.6(1,000) = **640**.

**Variance and standard deviation:** how far outcomes are spread around the mean. Two bets can have the same expected value but very different risk.

```latex
\sigma^2 = \sum p_i (x_i - \mu)^2 \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}
```

Example: win or lose 10 with equal chance has mean 0 and variance 100 (σ = 10). Win or lose 1,000 has mean 0 and variance 1,000,000 (σ = 1,000). Same mean, but the second is far riskier.

**Coefficient of variation (CV):** compares risk when the expected values differ.

```latex
CV = \frac{\sigma}{\mu}
```

Example: project A has μ = 100,000 and σ = 30,000, so CV = 0.30. Project B has μ = 150,000 and σ = 40,000, so CV = 0.267. B has the bigger σ but is **less risky per unit of return**.

### Attitude to risk

- **Risk lover:** prefers the gamble, accepts a payoff above the expected value.
- **Risk averse:** prefers the certain outcome, the behaviour when the payoff is below the expected value.
- **Risk neutral:** payoff equals expected value, so only the expected value matters.

### Decisions under risk and uncertainty: pricing example

A firm chooses between three pricing strategies. The payoffs are rates of return (%) in three states of the economy.

| Strategy | Expansion | Stability | Contraction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raise price | 25 | 15 | −10 |
| No change | 15 | 20 | −5 |
| Lower price | 15 | 0 | 5 |

**Under risk** (probabilities 35%, 50%, 15%): expected value and standard deviation of each.

| Strategy | Mean | Std dev |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise price | 14.75 | 11.34 |
| No change | 14.5 | 8.5 |
| Lower price | 6.0 | 6.8 |

Raise price has the highest expected return, but it also has the highest spread, and expected value ignores the manager's attitude to risk. (Your handout shows 9.5 and 9.86 for "No change". Those two numbers are wrong. See the corrections section.)

**Under complete ignorance** there are four rules. Use the same payoff table.

1. **Laplace:** assume all states are equally likely and take the average. Raise price = (25 + 15 − 10)/3 = 10. No change = (15 + 20 − 5)/3 = 10. Lower price = 6.67. Raise and No change tie, so choose **No change** because its spread is smaller (σ 10.79 vs 14.71).
2. **Wald (maximin):** for each strategy take the worst payoff, then pick the best of those. Worst payoffs are −10, −5 and 0, so choose **Lower price**. It is very pessimistic and risk averse.
3. **Maximax:** for each strategy take the best payoff, then pick the best of those. Best payoffs are 25, 20 and 15, so choose **Raise price**. It is very optimistic, for risk lovers.
4. **Savage (minimax regret):** regret is the gap between a payoff and the best payoff in that state. Build a regret table, take each strategy's largest regret, and choose the smallest of those.

| Strategy | Expansion | Stability | Contraction | Max regret |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raise price | 0 | 5 | 15 | 15 |
| No change | 10 | 0 | 10 | **10** |
| Lower price | 10 | 20 | 0 | 20 |

Choose **No change**. Savage is neither too optimistic nor too pessimistic, so it suits long-term capital projects where you want a satisfactory return with moderate risk.

**Hurwicz** (general knowledge, the handout only names it): a blend of the two extremes. Pick an optimism index α between 0 and 1 and score each strategy as α × best + (1 − α) × worst. α = 1 is maximax and α = 0 is maximin. The handout notes that maximax and maximin are the two extreme cases of Hurwicz.

No rule suits every situation. Pick the one that fits the circumstances and the organisation's attitude to risk.

### Four ways to manage risk

1. **Insurance:** transfer the risk through a policy.
2. **Hedging:** offset an expected loss with an expected profit from another contract.
3. **Diversification:** spread money over several investments so each carries less risk.
4. **Adjusting for risk:** make a provision (such as a contingency allowance or a higher MARR) to offset the expected loss. This connects back to MARR in section 6 and to contingency in section 8.

## 10. Break-even and cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis

This uses the fixed and variable costs from section 2 to answer a simple question: how much must we sell before we stop losing money? It also shows how risky a project is, which links to the previous section.

CVP analysis looks at how sales volume, selling price, cost structure and profit affect one another. It helps answer: how many units to sell to break even, how many to earn a target profit, whether to invest in automation, and whether more advertising pays.

### Assumptions

- Selling price is constant.
- Costs are linear and can be split into variable and fixed parts.
- In a multi-product firm, the sales mix is constant.
- Inventories do not change (what is produced is sold).

### Key terms and formulas

- **Profit = Sales − variable expenses − fixed expenses.**
- **Contribution per unit (unit CM)** = selling price − variable cost per unit. It is what each unit contributes towards fixed cost and profit.
- **Contribution to sales ratio (C/S ratio, also called the P/V or profit-volume ratio, or CM ratio)** = contribution ÷ sales × 100%. It equals 1 − the variable cost ratio.
- **Break-even point (BEP):** the activity level where there is neither profit nor loss. Sales equal total cost, or total contribution equals fixed cost.

```latex
\text{BEP (units)} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs}}{\text{Contribution per unit}} \qquad \text{BEP (sales value)} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs}}{\text{C/S ratio}}
```

- **Target profit:** break-even is the special case where the target profit is zero.

```latex
\text{Units for target profit} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs} + \text{Target profit}}{\text{Contribution per unit}} \qquad \text{Sales value} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs} + \text{Target profit}}{\text{C/S ratio}}
```

- **Margin of safety:** how far sales can fall before you make a loss.

```latex
\text{Margin of safety} = \text{Budgeted sales} - \text{Break-even sales} \qquad \text{MOS \%} = \frac{\text{Budgeted sales} - \text{Break-even sales}}{\text{Budgeted sales}} \times 100
```

- **Cost structure:** the proportion of fixed to variable cost in a business.
- **Degree of operating leverage (DOL)** = contribution margin ÷ net operating income. It tells you how much profit changes for a 1% change in sales. A 10% rise in sales with a DOL of 4 raises profit by about 40%. DOL is not constant (it is infinite at break-even because profit is zero), so recompute it at each sales level. A firm with a high share of fixed cost has high operating leverage: more profit when sales rise, and more risk when they fall.

Two methods give the same answer: the **equation method** (sales = variable cost + fixed cost + profit) and the **contribution method** (the formulas above).

### Break-even chart (graphical method)

Plot sales value or units on the horizontal axis and money on the vertical axis. Draw the fixed cost line (flat), then add variable cost to get the total cost line, then draw the total revenue line from the origin. They cross at the break-even point. The gap between revenue and total cost is profit (or loss), and the gap from the break-even point to the budgeted sales level is the margin of safety. The **P/V chart** plots profit against sales instead, starting at a loss equal to the fixed cost, and crosses zero at break-even.

### Worked examples

**Single product (lecture 9, exercise 1):** budget 10,000 units. Materials 12, direct labour 35, variable production overhead 5 hours × 8 = 40, distribution 2 a unit. Variable cost per unit = 89. Selling price 129, so unit contribution = **40**. Fixed costs = factory 120,000 + selling and distribution (160,000 − 2 × 10,000 = 140,000) + administration 80,000 = **340,000**.

- (a) Break-even = 340,000 ÷ 40 = **8,500 units**.
- (b) For a profit of 40,000: (340,000 + 40,000) ÷ 40 = 9,500 units, so sales of 9,500 × 129 = **1,225,500**.

**Break-even between two options (FE review):** hand tools cost $1,000 plus $1.50 per unit. An automated process costs $15,000 plus $0.50 per unit. Setting 1,000 + 1.5x = 15,000 + 0.5x gives x = 14,000 units. At 5,000 units a year that is 2.8 years. Beyond that quantity the automated process is cheaper. (This ignores interest. The lease-vs-buy example in section 6 includes it.)

**Several products at a constant mix (lecture 9, example 2):** product M sells at 7 with variable cost 2.94, product N sells at 15 with variable cost 4.50. They sell in the ratio 5 M to 1 N and fixed cost is 36,000.

1. Contribution per unit: M = 4.06, N = 10.50.
2. Contribution per mix (5 M + 1 N) = 5 × 4.06 + 10.50 = 30.80.
3. Mixes to break even = 36,000 ÷ 30.80 = about 1,169.
4. Units: 1,169 × 5 = 5,845 of M and 1,169 of N.
5. Revenue = 5,845 × 7 + 1,169 × 15 = 40,915 + 17,535 = **58,450**.

**Same example with the C/S ratio:** revenue per mix = 5 × 7 + 15 = 50, so the average C/S ratio = 30.80 ÷ 50 = 61.6%. Break-even sales = 36,000 ÷ 0.616 = about **58,443**, which matches (small differences come from rounding).

**Margin of safety for several products (example 4):** fixed cost 43,890. W: price 8, variable 3.80. R: price 14, variable 4.20. Ratio 5 W to 6 R. Contribution per mix = 5 × 4.20 + 6 × 9.80 = 79.80. Mixes = 43,890 ÷ 79.80 = 550. Break-even revenue = 2,750 × 8 + 3,300 × 14 = 68,200. Budgeted sales are 74,400, so the margin of safety = 6,200, or **8.3%** of budget.

**Target profit for several products (example 5):** products F, G, H in the ratio 2:1:3. Selling price 22, 15, 19. Variable cost 16, 12, 13. Fixed cost 80,000, target profit 52,000.

- Contribution per unit: F = 6, G = 3, H = 6.
- Contribution per mix = 2 × 6 + 1 × 3 + 3 × 6 = 33.
- Mixes needed = (80,000 + 52,000) ÷ 33 = 4,000.
- Units: F 8,000, G 4,000, H 12,000. Sales revenue = 176,000 + 60,000 + 228,000 = **464,000**.

If the mix changes, the contribution per mix and the break-even point change too. A shift toward products with a lower contribution raises the break-even point and cuts profit.

### Multi-product charts

With a constant mix you can draw one chart for the whole range in three ways: output in sales value, products plotted in sequence, or output as a percentage of forecast sales. In the lecture's data (X, Y, Z with budgeted revenue 58,000, variable cost 37,000 and fixed cost 10,000), the overall C/S ratio is 21,000 ÷ 58,000 = 36.21%, so break-even sales are 10,000 ÷ 0.3621 = about 27,600 (the handout rounds to 27,500) and the margin of safety is about 30,500.

A **multi-product P/V chart** plots products from left to right in order of C/S ratio (highest first), here X (62.5%), Y (33.3%), Z (16.7%). It shows the overall break-even point, which products to expand or drop, and the effect of price or volume changes.

### Limitations and advantages

**Limitations:**

- It assumes fixed cost stays the same in total and variable cost per unit stays the same. In reality fixed costs come in steps, and unit variable cost falls with economies of scale and later rises.
- It is valid only in the normal (relevant) range of output.
- It assumes the price is constant, but a higher volume may need a lower price.
- It assumes production equals sales, so changes in inventory are ignored.
- It treats estimates of cost as certain.

**Advantages:** charts are easy for non-finance managers to understand. It shows profit or loss at any level of activity within the valid range. The break-even point and margin of safety give a feel for risk.

## 11. Demand, elasticity and production

The earlier sections treat cost and benefit as given. This section and the next explain where they come from: what buyers want (demand), how producers turn inputs into output (production), and how the two meet in a market. It also ties back to cost, because production decides cost.

### Demand

The **demand** for a commodity is the quantity people will buy **at a given price, per unit of time**. Two things follow: demand always refers to a price (saying demand for mangoes is 100 kg means nothing without a price), and it always refers to a time period (a day, a week, a month).

A desire becomes demand only when you have the means and are willing to spend. Wanting a fan is a desire. Having the money and being ready to pay for it is demand.

**Demand schedule:** a table of the quantities demanded at different prices.

- **Individual** demand schedule: one person's demand. Example: 1 kg at ₦5, 2 kg at ₦4, 3 kg at ₦3, 4 kg at ₦2, 5 kg at ₦1.
- **Market** demand schedule: add up all buyers at each price. With three buyers A, B and C the market totals are 6, 9, 12, 15 and 18 kg at ₦5, 4, 3, 2 and 1.

Plotting price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis gives a downward-sloping **demand curve**. The market curve is the sum of individual curves.

**Why the demand schedule matters:** it shows how demand changes when price changes, it is the base for elasticity, it explains the law of demand, and together with the supply schedule it decides the market price. Businesses use it to forecast the rise in sales from a price cut.

### The law of demand

**Other things being equal, when the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded rises, and when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls.** Price and quantity move in opposite directions. There is no fixed proportion: a 10% fall in price does not necessarily give a 10% rise in demand.

**Why it happens (three causes):**

1. **Diminishing marginal utility:** each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so a buyer will only take more at a lower price.
2. **Income effect:** when the price falls, your real income rises, so you can buy more.
3. **Substitution effect:** when the price of a good falls, it becomes cheaper than substitutes, so people switch to it.

**Assumptions (the "other things equal"):** the buyer's income is unchanged, tastes are unchanged, prices of substitutes and complements are unchanged, no new close substitute appears, and the good has no prestige value.

**Exceptions (where the law seems to fail):**

- Changes in habit, custom or income.
- Necessities of life such as flour: demand hardly changes with price.
- Fear of a future shortage or a future price rise makes people buy more now at today's price.
- Articles of distinction (prestige goods): a higher price raises demand.
- **Giffen goods:** very basic inferior goods (named after Sir Robert Giffen, who observed low-paid workers buying more bread after its price rose, because they could no longer afford meat). Less is bought when the price falls and more when it rises.
- Ignorance: a buyer who does not know the old price may buy more after a rise.

### Elasticity of demand

The law of demand only gives the **direction** of change. Elasticity measures the **size** of the response.

**Price elasticity of demand (e\_p)** = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price.

```latex
e_p = \frac{\Delta q / q}{\Delta p / p} = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta p} \times \frac{p}{q}
```

Mathematically e\_p is negative, because quantity and price move in opposite directions. For convenience we use the absolute value, so e\_p = 2 means demand is more responsive than e\_p = 1.

| Type | e\_p | Meaning | Curve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfectly elastic | infinity | Any price rise loses all sales | Horizontal |
| Relatively elastic | greater than 1 | Quantity changes proportionally more than price | Flat |
| Unit elastic | 1 | Quantity changes by the same proportion as price | Rectangular hyperbola |
| Relatively inelastic | less than 1 | Quantity changes proportionally less than price | Steep |
| Perfectly inelastic | 0 | Quantity does not change at all | Vertical |

**Factors that decide elasticity:**

1. **Nature of the product:** necessities (rice, salt) are inelastic, luxuries (TVs) are elastic.
2. **Extent of usage:** goods with many uses (steel, aluminium, wood) are more elastic.
3. **Availability of substitutes:** many substitutes mean elastic demand.
4. **Income level:** high earners are less affected by price changes.
5. **Proportion of income spent:** cheap items such as matches and salt are inelastic.
6. **Urgency of demand:** an urgent need with no alternative is inelastic.
7. **Durability of the product.**

**Other elasticities** (the handout only names them; the formulas below are general knowledge):

- **Income elasticity** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income. Positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods.
- **Cross elasticity** = % change in quantity demanded of one good ÷ % change in the price of a related good. Positive for substitutes, negative for complements.

### Theory of production

Production is turning inputs into output. It includes services such as transport and banking, not only making physical goods. The **production function** is the relationship between inputs and output. We study it by holding some inputs fixed and varying others.

Its importance: it links cost to the amount of output. Costs govern the supply of a product, and supply with demand decides price. That is why this topic connects back to the cost concepts in section 2.

### Isoquants (equal-product curves)

An **isoquant** shows all the combinations of two inputs that produce the **same level of output**. It is like an indifference curve, except that output can be measured exactly. Example for 20 units of output: (1 of X, 12 of Y), (2, 8), (3, 5), (4, 3), (5, 2).

**Marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS):** the rate at which one input can replace another at the margin while output stays the same. From the table above, moving from combination A to B swaps 4 units of Y for 1 unit of X, so MRTS = 4. Then it falls to 3, 2 and 1.

```latex
MRTS_{XY} = -\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X} = \frac{MP_X}{MP_Y}
```

The MRTS equals the slope of the isoquant, and equals the ratio of the marginal products of the two inputs. It **diminishes** as you substitute more X for Y. The slower it falls, the easier it is to substitute the inputs. If it stays constant, the inputs are perfect substitutes.

**Properties of isoquants:**

1. They slope downward from left to right.
2. Two isoquants never cross.
3. They are convex to the origin (because MRTS diminishes).

### Law of variable proportions (diminishing returns)

This law looks at output when **one input varies and the others are fixed**. In simple words: as you keep adding equal amounts of one input to fixed inputs, after some point the extra output from each addition gets smaller.

**Assumptions:** technology is unchanged, at least one input is fixed, and the inputs can be combined in different proportions.

**Three stages:**

| Stage | Name | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Increasing returns | Total product rises at an increasing rate. Average product rises throughout. The fixed input is too large compared to the variable input. |
| 2 | Diminishing returns | Total product still rises but at a decreasing rate. Marginal and average products both fall but stay positive. It ends where marginal product is zero and total product is at its maximum. |
| 3 | Negative returns | Total product falls and marginal product is negative. There is too much of the variable input. |

A rational producer never operates in stage 1 (the fixed input is not being used well) or stage 3 (adding more input reduces output). So production happens in **stage 2**. This links to cost: in stage 2 the marginal cost of each extra unit rises.

### Returns to scale

The law above changes **one** input. **Returns to scale** asks what happens when **all** inputs increase in the same proportion.

- **Constant returns:** output rises in the same proportion. Doubling all inputs doubles output.
- **Increasing returns:** output rises by more than the proportion. Doubling inputs more than doubles output.
- **Decreasing returns:** output rises by less than the proportion. Doubling inputs gives less than double output.

On an isoquant map, constant returns means equal gaps between successive isoquants along a straight line from the origin. (Your handout explains the constant case in detail and states the other two briefly.)

## 12. Supply, market equilibrium and banking

> **Flag:** your files cover demand but have no section on supply, elasticity of supply or equilibrium price, although they are on the syllabus. ### Supply (general knowledge)

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to sell **at a given price per unit of time**. It mirrors demand.

- **Law of supply:** other things equal, when the price rises, the quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes **upward**. Higher prices make production more profitable and let producers cover higher marginal cost (which links back to stage 2 of the law of variable proportions).
- **Determinants of supply** (a change in any of these shifts the whole curve): cost of inputs, technology, number of sellers, prices of related goods, taxes and subsidies, government rules, and expectations.
- **Movement vs shift:** a change in the good's **own price** moves you along the curve (a change in quantity supplied or demanded). A change in any other factor **shifts** the curve (a change in supply or demand).
- **Price elasticity of supply** = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. It is positive. It is higher when producers can raise output easily (spare capacity, storable goods, a long time to adjust).

### Equilibrium price (general knowledge)

The market is in **equilibrium** where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The price at that point is the **equilibrium price** and the quantity is the **equilibrium quantity**.

- If the price is **above** equilibrium, supply exceeds demand (a **surplus**), so sellers cut prices.
- If the price is **below** equilibrium, demand exceeds supply (a **shortage**), so prices rise.

**Example:** demand Qd = 100 − 2P and supply Qs = 20 + 3P. Set Qd = Qs: 100 − 2P = 20 + 3P, so 80 = 5P and P = **16**. Then Q = 100 − 2(16) = **68** (check: 20 + 3 × 16 = 68).

**Shifts:** if demand rises (the curve shifts right), both the equilibrium price and quantity rise. If supply rises (shifts right), the price falls and the quantity rises. This links to inflation in section 5: a demand-pull rise in prices is demand growing faster than supply.

### Banking

A bank is an institution that deals in **money and credit**. It accepts deposits from the public, lends the money to those who need it, and helps move money from place to place. Banks aim for profit, create credit, and create demand deposits that work as money, so they run the country's payment system.

#### Functions of commercial banks

1. **Accepting deposits:** from people who can save but cannot use the savings profitably themselves. Types: fixed (higher interest, locked for a period), current (for traders, unlimited withdrawals, usually no interest), savings (encourages small savings, limits on withdrawals), recurring (regular monthly instalments) and home safe accounts.
2. **Advancing loans:** after keeping cash reserves, banks lend the rest. Forms: money at call (very short, between banks), cash credit (against stocks and shares), overdraft (withdrawing more than your balance and paying interest on it), discounting bills of exchange (self-liquidating) and term loans (more than one year).
3. **Credit creation:** when a bank grants a loan it opens an account in the borrower's name and credits it, so it creates a deposit of the same size. Deposits therefore grow by a multiple of reserves.
4. **Cheque system:** a cheque is the most developed credit instrument for payments.
5. **Agency functions:** remitting funds, collecting and paying cheques and bills, carrying out standing orders, buying and selling securities, collecting dividends, tax advice, acting as trustee or executor, and acting as a representative for customers.
6. **General utility functions:** lockers, traveller's cheques, letters of credit (useful in foreign trade), collecting statistics, underwriting securities, giving references on customers, and foreign exchange business.

#### Role of commercial banks in a developing economy

A good banking system is a pre-condition for development. Banks do more than deal in money: they lead development.

1. **Capital formation:** the key driver of growth, in three stages: generating savings, mobilising savings and channelling them into productive use. Banks help at all three (interest rewards savers, branches collect savings, loans send money to investors).
2. **Encouraging innovation:** entrepreneurs often lack funds, and bank loans let them start new ventures and expand.
3. **Monetisation of the economy:** banks create and distribute money, moving people from barter to money transactions, and extend into rural areas.
4. **Influencing economic activity** through interest rates and the availability of credit. Lower rates make investment more profitable and stimulate activity, higher rates discourage it.

#### Central bank (general knowledge; your handout's section was cut off and what remains describes the Reserve Bank of India)

The **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)** sits at the top of the banking system. Typical central bank functions:

1. **Issuer of currency:** it is the sole issuer of the naira.
2. **Banker and adviser to the government:** it keeps government accounts, makes and collects payments, and manages public debt.
3. **Banker to banks and lender of last resort:** banks keep reserves with it, and it lends to them in a crisis.
4. **Regulator and supervisor** of banks and other financial institutions.
5. **Monetary policy and credit control:** it manages money supply and interest rates using tools such as the monetary policy rate, cash reserve requirement, liquidity ratio and open market operations.
6. **Custodian of foreign reserves** and manager of exchange rate policy.
7. **Developmental role:** promoting financial stability and the growth of financial institutions.

A central bank should not compete with commercial banks and should keep its assets liquid.

#### Money market (general knowledge; your handout's section was cut off)

The money market is where **short-term funds** (usually up to one year) are borrowed and lent. It gives governments, banks and companies a way to manage short-term cash needs. Common instruments: treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, bankers' acceptances, call money and repurchase agreements. Its rates guide the cost of short-term credit, which feeds into the interest rate (MARR and cost of capital) you used in the earlier sections.

## 13. Capital budgeting and project analysis

Everything so far (cost, time value, comparing alternatives, depreciation, risk) comes together when a firm decides whether to spend a large sum on a project. This section is the big picture, from Chandra's chapter in your ECN 471 material.

### Why capital investment decisions matter

- **Long-term effects:** the results last many years and shape the firm's character.
- **Irreversibility:** the market for used or custom equipment is poor, so a wrong decision often cannot be undone without a big loss.
- **Substantial outlays:** an integrated steel plant costs thousands of millions, and costs rise with advanced technology.

**Why they are difficult:** (1) **measurement problems**, because costs and benefits are hard to identify, especially when the project affects other products or has intangible effects such as worker morale; (2) **uncertainty**, because the benefits extend far into the future; (3) **temporal spread**, because costs and benefits spread over 10 to 20 years for industry and 20 to 50 years for infrastructure, which makes discount rates and equivalence harder.

### Types of capital investment

- **By form:** physical (land, plant, machinery), monetary (deposits, bonds, shares) or intangible (R&D, training, market development).
- **By impact:** strategic (changes the firm's direction, such as entering a new product line) or tactical (carries out the current strategy, such as replacing an old machine).
- **By purpose:** mandatory (needed to meet law, such as pollution control), replacement, expansion, diversification, research and development, and miscellaneous.

### Six phases of capital budgeting

The phases generally follow one another, but there are feedback loops, so the process repeats.

1. **Planning:** set the broad investment strategy, generate project ideas and screen them. A quick preliminary analysis asks if the idea is worth a full study.
2. **Analysis:** a detailed study of the market, technical, financial, economic and ecological sides (below).
3. **Selection:** choose projects using criteria such as NPV and IRR (section 6).
4. **Financing:** arrange the money.
5. **Implementation:** project and engineering design, negotiation and contracting, construction, training, and plant commissioning. Delays cause cost overruns (see section 8). Helpful steps: formulate the project well, assign responsibility to project managers, and use network techniques (PERT and CPM) for planning and control.
6. **Review:** compare actual with expected performance. It tests the assumptions, builds a log of experience, suggests corrective action, shows judgement bias and encourages caution.

**Levels of decision:** operating (lower management, routine, small, short term), administrative (middle management, semi-structured) and strategic (top management, unstructured, major, long term). Examples: minor office equipment, balancing equipment, a diversification project.

### Five facets of project analysis

| Facet | Main question | What it looks at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market | What will be the total demand, and what share will the project get? | Past and present consumption and supply, competition, cost structure, elasticity of demand (section 11), consumer behaviour, distribution channels, legal limits |
| Technical | Are the technical conditions right and are the choices sensible? | Location, size and scale, process, machines, raw material and power supply, layout, effluent treatment, realistic work schedules |
| Financial | Is it financially viable and will it satisfy investors? | Investment outlay, financing, cost of capital, profitability, break-even point (section 10), cash flows, criteria of merit (section 6), risk |
| Economic | Is it good for society? (social cost-benefit analysis) | Benefits and costs in shadow (efficiency) prices, effect on income distribution, savings and investment, social goals such as jobs |
| Ecological | What is the environmental damage and what will restoration cost? | Needed for power plants, irrigation, chemicals, leather processing and other polluting industries |

A **feasibility study** covers the first four phases (planning, analysis, selection, financing) and the five facets above. It starts with an idea, screens it, and stops if the idea is not promising. Otherwise, it carries out the market, technical, financial, economic and ecological analyses, then decides if the project is worthwhile and prepares a funding proposal.

### Objective of capital budgeting

The goal is to **maximise shareholder wealth**, which means the market value of the firm's equity. Alternatives such as maximising profit, earnings per share or return on equity are weaker. Profit in absolute terms is not a good guide: it ignores **timing** (profit now vs later), and it ignores **risk** (a certain 50,000 is not the same as a variable profit with an expected value of 50,000). Maximising earnings per share and return on equity share the last two weaknesses.

This links to the risk section: **return and risk go together**. Higher return usually comes with higher risk. Market value rises with return and falls with risk.

### Common weaknesses in capital budgeting

1. Strategy and the capital budget are not aligned.
2. The **base case** (what happens without the project) is poorly identified. A gloomy base case makes any project look good.
3. Risk is treated too simply (just low, medium and high scenarios).
4. Future options (expand, switch use, abandon) are not valued.
5. Divisions use different assumptions (growth, inflation, residual value, cost of capital).
6. Side effects on the firm's other products are ignored.
7. Pay is not linked to the financial measures (NPV, IRR) used to approve the project.

### 14.1 Sensitivity analysis

Every estimate in a project (cost, sales, life, interest rate) can be wrong. **Sensitivity analysis** asks: if one estimate turns out different, does my decision change? It is the simplest way to test risk (see section 9) without needing probabilities.

**How to do it:**

1. Work out the base case, for example the PW using your best estimates.
2. Change **one** variable at a time (say by −20%, −10%, +10%, +20%) and keep the others fixed.
3. Recalculate PW (or IRR, or AW) each time.
4. Find which variable moves the result most. That is the variable you must estimate most carefully.
5. Find the **break-even (switching) value**: how far a variable can move before PW hits zero and the decision flips.

**Example:** invest ₦100 million now, receive ₦30 million a year for 5 years, MARR 10%. The P/A factor is 3.7908.

| Annual benefit | PW (₦ million) | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 24 (−20%) | −9.02 | Reject |
| 30 (base) | +13.72 | Accept |
| 36 (+20%) | +36.47 | Accept |

The break-even annual benefit is 100 ÷ 3.7908 = ₦26.38 million. So the benefit can fall about 12% before the project stops being worth doing. The decision is sensitive to the annual benefit.

**Results are often shown as:** a table like the one above, or a **spider diagram** (a line for each variable, plotting PW against % change; the steepest line is the most sensitive variable). A **scenario analysis** goes a step further and changes several variables together (best case, base case, worst case).

**Advantages:** simple, needs no probabilities, shows which estimates matter, and shows where to collect more data.

**Limits:** it changes one variable at a time, although in real life variables move together; it does not say how likely a change is; and the ranges chosen (±20%) are arbitrary.

### 14.2 Learning curve: managerial implications **(general knowledge)**

The calculation (Y = aX^b, with an 80% curve meaning cost per unit falls to 80% each time cumulative output doubles) is in section 8. A question on this usually also asks what it means for managers:

- **Cost estimating and budgeting:** do not use the cost of the first unit for the whole job. Estimate later units using the curve.
- **Pricing and bidding:** a firm that is further down the curve can bid lower and still earn a profit. Some firms price low early to win volume and move down the curve faster.
- **Labour planning and scheduling:** hours needed per unit fall, so fewer workers or less time are needed as production continues.
- **Competitive strategy:** a larger market share means more cumulative output, so lower unit cost, which is a barrier to new competitors.
- **Make or buy and capacity:** the saving from repetition helps decide whether to produce in-house.
- **Training and motivation:** training, good supervision and stable crews speed up learning.
- **Limits:** the curve flattens after many units; it assumes the same product, stable workers and no major change in process; and the learning rate (80%, 90%) is an estimate.

### 14.3 Projects, project management and the project cycle **(general knowledge)**

Your 2017/2018 paper has 50 multiple-choice questions and a written section on this topic. It links to section 13 (capital budgeting) and section 1 (what engineering economics is). The key ideas are below.

**Basic definitions**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Project | A one-off, temporary effort with a clear start and end, a budget and a specific objective. Projects differ in complexity, duration and scope. |
| Programme | A group of related projects managed together to achieve a bigger objective than any single project could. |
| Project cycle | The stages a project passes through from idea to completion: idea, identification, evaluation (appraisal), selection, execution (implementation), then operation and review. |
| Economic analysis | Studying whether a project is good for the economy or society, using social costs and benefits (shadow prices), not just the firm's profit. |
| Project evaluation | Judging the worth of a project, before it starts (is it worth doing?) and after (did it deliver?), using criteria such as NPV, IRR and BCR from section 6. |
| Financial analysis | Checking if the project is viable and profitable for the investors in money terms. |
| Portfolio analysis | Assessing the attractiveness of the firm's current businesses or products and the market, to guide future investment. |
| Technical and economic feasibility study | The detailed investigation of a project, covering the market, technical, financial, economic and ecological sides (section 13). |

**Three main objectives of a project:** quality (performance), time and cost (budget). A good project manager balances the three.

**Planning**

- Planning means deciding what to do and how. It includes the scope statement, the project plan and the schedule. It reduces risk and increases efficiency.
- Levels of plan: **visionary plan** (long-term picture), **strategic plan** (top management, long term), **tactical plan** (short range, breaks the mission into smaller actionable pieces), **operational plan** (day to day).
- **Vision statement:** where the organisation wants to be in the future (for example in five years). **Mission statement:** a realistic overview of what the organisation does, its aims and ambitions.
- **Succession planning:** identifying and developing internal people to fill key leadership positions.

**Tools for planning, scheduling and control**

- **Work breakdown structure (WBS):** breaks a large, complex project into smaller, more manageable units.
- **Gantt (bar) chart:** shows activities against time; used to plan and report progress.
- **Responsibility (matrix) chart:** shows who is responsible for which task; useful when allocating tasks to different people in a team.
- **PERT and CPM:** network techniques for planning and control (section 13).
- **Cost control:** the process of controlling cost-related items; it follows the cost management plan and the budget.
- **Monitoring:** a deliberate, systematic collection and analysis of information as the project progresses, to check efficiency and effectiveness. **Evaluation** judges the worth of the activity. Performance is compared with the plan to find the **variance**.
- **Post-implementation review:** control begins early in planning and ends late with a thorough review of every step.

**Roles and people**

- The project manager acts as a planner, an organiser, a coach and a leader (not a footballer!). **Leading** is a central role: working with and through others to achieve the project's objectives.
- **Staffing** means filling the project with qualified people who can take responsibility for specific parts.
- **Communication** is the most critical role for project success. **Vertical communication** passes information on policies down and gives staff a way to respond up. The greater the complexity and uncertainty, the greater the need for **information**.
- Managers also act as **motivator** (when the work is hard), negotiator and coordinator. They must practise the art of **integration** to build a good plan.
- **Functional management** controls resources and manages a business unit.

**Project charter and contracts**

- The **project charter** formally authorises the project and gives it existence. It outlines the project manager's authority and refers to the project description. The detailed scope statement is a separate document, not part of the charter.
- Common contract types: **fixed price (lump sum)**, **cost plus**, and **time and materials**. The seller is also called the contractor, service provider or supplier. The buyer is the acquiring organisation.

**Terms from the capital-budgeting side**

- **Capital budgeting (capital expenditure) decision:** the firm's long-term investment decision (section 13).
- **Assets** are grouped as current assets (cash, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses), fixed assets and investment assets. "Recurrent asset" is not a category.
- **Divestment:** cutting back or selling off part of an operation.
- **Total sales:** the full value of goods or services sold to third parties (cash plus credit).
- **Cost:** the outlay incurred to acquire an asset.
- **Time preference for money:** a person offered ₦1,000,000 now or later prefers it now, because money has time value (section 3).
- **Internal rate of return:** the rate that equates the present value of cash inflows with the present value of cash outflows.
- **Two criteria that fully recognise the timing of cash flows:** net present value and internal rate of return. The accounting rate of return and the payback period do not.

**Why efficient allocation of capital is important (for the essay)**

Capital is scarce, so every naira used on one project cannot be used on another. Capital decisions are large, long term and hard to reverse, so mistakes are costly. They set the firm's future direction, cash flows and risk. The goal is to maximise shareholder wealth (section 13), and the firm that allocates capital better earns more from the same money. That is why many argue it is the most important finance function. A balanced answer can also note that other functions matter too, such as raising funds (financing), managing working capital and paying dividends, and that a good capital decision still needs good financing and management.

### 14.4 Economics basics behind demand, supply, cost and production **(general knowledge, plus your practice booklet)**

Your practice booklet (ESCA, GEG 501) tests ideas from sections 2, 11 and 12 in more detail. These are the points it relies on.

**Micro and macro**

- **Microeconomics** studies single decision makers: households and firms, how prices of goods are set, and how resources are shared between different goods. **Macroeconomics** studies the whole economy (aggregate demand, output, inflation): households, firms and government together.
- The central problems of any economy are **what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce**. They exist because wants are unlimited, resources are scarce and scarce resources have alternative uses. Maximising private profit is not one of the central problems.

**Demand**

- Demand has three parts: the **desire** for the good, the **resources** (ability to pay) and the **willingness** to pay. It only makes sense with a **price** and a **time period**.
- **Demand schedule:** individual and market (the market schedule adds up all individuals).
- **Movement along the curve** (a change in quantity demanded) is caused by a change in the good's own price. **A shift of the curve** (a change in demand) is caused by income, taste, price of related goods (substitutes and complements), expected future prices or number of buyers. A change in the *supply* of the good does not shift the demand curve.
- **Why demand falls as price rises:** diminishing marginal utility, the income effect and the substitution effect.
- **Assumptions of the law:** income, tastes and prices of related goods stay the same. **Exceptions:** Giffen goods (demand rises as price rises), some inferior goods, fear of tomorrow (stocking up), ignorance and prestige goods.
- **Marginal utility (MU):** the extra satisfaction from one more unit. If two goods are substitutes and the price of one rises, the consumer buys less of it and more of the other, and the MU of the good now consumed less rises.

**Elasticity**

| Type | Formula or meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Price elasticity of demand | % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. Negative for normal goods. |
| Income elasticity of demand | % change in quantity ÷ % change in income. Example: income ₦25,000 to ₦50,000 is +100%; shirts 50 to 100 is +100%; so the elasticity is 1. |
| Cross elasticity | % change in quantity of A ÷ % change in price of B. Positive for substitutes, negative for complements. |
| Elasticity of substitution | Responsiveness of the ratio of two goods or inputs to a change in their relative price. |
| Price elasticity of supply | % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. Above 1 is elastic. |

- **Perfectly inelastic** (zero): quantity does not respond to price (vertical curve). **Relatively inelastic** (below 1): steep curve, necessities such as salt. **Unitary** (equal to 1): spending stays the same whatever the price. **Relatively elastic** (above 1): flat curve, luxuries and durable goods such as cars. **Perfectly elastic**: infinite.
- If demand is unitary elastic, a 25% rise in price gives a 25% fall in quantity, and total revenue does not change.

**Consumer and producer surplus**

- **Consumer surplus:** the gap between what buyers would be willing to pay and what they actually pay. It is the area below the demand curve and above the price.
- **Producer surplus:** the gap between the price sellers receive and the lowest price they would accept. It is the area above the supply curve and below the price.
- If demand increases (the curve shifts right) the equilibrium price and quantity both rise, so both consumer surplus and producer surplus can rise.

**What happens to price and quantity when curves shift**

| Change | Price | Quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rises (right) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand falls (left) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply rises (right): lower input cost, better technology | Falls | Rises |
| Supply falls (left): higher cost of production | Rises | Falls |
| Supply rises more than demand rises | Falls | Rises |

If price and quantity both rise together, the most likely cause is an increase in demand. If both curves move at once, one of price or quantity is certain and the other depends on which shift is bigger. A tax on a good is meant to discourage its use.

**Cost and production relationships (these appear in the multiple-choice questions)**

- TC = TFC + TVC, so TFC = TC − TVC. Average fixed cost = TFC ÷ Q. Average variable cost = the cost per unit that varies with output.
- When **marginal cost is below average cost**, average cost is falling. **MC cuts AC at the minimum point of AC.**
- **Marginal product (MP) equals zero when total product is at its maximum.** MP is greater than AP while AP is rising, and MP cuts AP at the maximum of AP. MP is highest at the point of inflexion of the TP curve.
- **Short run:** at least one input (such as the building or land) is fixed. **Long run:** all inputs can change.
- **Law of variable proportions:** one input changes (stage 1, 2 and 3 in section 11); stage 2 is where firms work. **Returns to scale:** all inputs change together.
- A curve showing different input mixes giving the same output is an **isoquant** (equal-product curve), the production version of an indifference curve; the **MRTS** shows how easily one input replaces another.
- If supply rises when technology improves, the curve moves right. If production cost rises, the supply curve moves left.

### 14.5 Time value of money and profitability: extra points from your "TVM and other stuff" note

Most of this note repeats sections 3 to 7. These are the extra ideas and formulas it adds.

**Payments at the start or end of a period**

Unless the question says otherwise, payments are made at the **end** of each period (even if the bill arrives on the first of the month). If the first payment is made at the start, the payments are different, so check the question. Interest and payment periods are assumed annual, and the interest rate is assumed constant.

**Annual cash flow**

Gross profit is sales minus expenses. Depreciation reduces tax but is not a cash cost. Therefore:

```latex
GP = S - E, \quad NP = (GP - D)(1 - t), \quad CF = NP + D = GP(1-t) + D\,t
```

Example: sales 500, expenses 300, so GP = 200. Depreciation D = 50, tax rate 30%. Tax = (200 − 50) × 0.3 = 45. NP = 105. CF = 105 + 50 = 155. Check: 200 × 0.7 + 50 × 0.3 = 155. The cash flow is higher when depreciation is claimed, because of the tax it saves (section 7).

**Inflation in project cash flows**

If a project's revenues and expenses both rise with inflation, you discount with the combined rate (1 + i\_comb) = (1 + i)(1 + f) (section 5). Depreciation is **not** inflated, because it is based on the original cost.

**Capitalized cost with replacement (perpetuity)**

For equipment that must be replaced every n years forever, you need the original cost plus enough money to fund every future replacement:

```latex
\text{Capitalized cost} = C_o + \frac{C_R}{(1+i)^n - 1}
```

Example: a pump costs ₦10,000, lasts 5 years, no salvage, i = 10%. Capitalized cost = 10,000 + 10,000 ÷ (1.1^5 − 1) = 10,000 + 16,380 = ₦26,380. If the operating cost is R\_op a year forever, add R\_op ÷ i. If the item has salvage value, the replacement cost C\_R is the original cost minus salvage.

**Sinking-fund depreciation**

The yearly charge is the amount you would deposit each year to build up (original value − salvage value) by the end of the life. It uses the A/F factor from section 4.

**More profitability measures**

- **Return on investment (ROI)** = annual profit ÷ fixed capital × 100. For example, a profit of ₦20 million a year on ₦100 million is 20%. It ignores the time value of money.
- **Payout (payback) time** = (fixed capital + start-up cost) ÷ annual cash flow. It ignores interest and anything after the payback point.
- **Discounted cash flow rate of return (DCFROR)** is the same as IRR: the rate that makes NPV zero. It is not the bank rate. Compare it with a required return that is higher than the bank rate, because a business carries risk and effort.
- **NPV** = present value of the cash flows minus the investment. A positive NPV means the project earns more than leaving the money in the bank at the chosen rate.
- **Discounted break-even period** = time for the cumulative *discounted* cash flow to reach zero.

**Cash flow items to remember:** land and working capital are paid at the start and recovered at the end; salvage value is received at shutdown; start-up cost is added at the beginning.

**MACRS:** a legal depreciation method used in the USA. It starts with double declining balance and switches to straight line, uses the half-year rule, and does not subtract salvage. In your course the main methods are straight line, double declining balance, sum-of-the-years' digits and units of production (section 7).

**How reliable are the inputs?** Forecasts of sales volume, product price, tax rate, inflation and risk are the least reliable and have the biggest effect on profitability. Installed cost, working capital and operating costs are better known but still matter a lot. This is why sensitivity analysis (14.1) is used.
