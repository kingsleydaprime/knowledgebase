# The Triple Constraint

The triple constraint says that scope, time and cost are the three biggest limits on a project, and they are tied together: change one and at least one of the others must move.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 8835a9da-9efb-4073-be9b-136f51ebfa4f -->

You should have read [[planning-and-scheduling/02-the-project-environment|02 — The Project Environment and Key Terms]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. name the three constraints and say what each one is;
2. predict what happens to the other two when one changes;
3. explain why the triple constraint is the reason crashing and resource levelling exist.

## What the triple constraint is
<!-- bite: b2f68525-7d0d-4c6f-8a4a-b755ac4d24a8 -->

Go back to the fence and gate. The owner wants three things: a certain fence (the **scope**), by a certain day (the **time**), for a certain amount of money (the **cost**). The planner can't fix all three freely, because they pull on each other.

1. **Scope**: This is exactly what the project will deliver: how long the fence is, how many gates, whether it gets painted.
2. **Time**: This is how long the project takes, from start to handover.
3. **Cost**: This is how much money the project uses.

People often draw them as the three sides of a triangle. Pull one corner and the shape has to change somewhere else.

## Change one, and another moves
<!-- bite: a2c3e5dd-9a75-4754-81e4-647d3a8562b7 -->

Here is how the pulling works, with the fence.

1. **Scope grows.** The owner adds a second gate. That is more work, so either the fence takes longer, or you pay for extra workers to finish on the same day, or both.
2. **The deadline moves earlier.** The owner wants it done before a party next Saturday. Either you pay more (overtime, extra hands) or you do less (skip the paint for now).
3. **The budget is cut.** You can't afford the extra hands any more. Either the fence takes longer, or something comes out of the scope.

Remember this one: you can usually pick any two, and the third is decided for you. Fixed scope and fixed date? Then the cost is whatever it takes.

Some versions of the idea add a fourth corner, **quality**, sitting in the middle of the triangle. If you squeeze all three sides at once, quality is what quietly gives way: the paint goes on thin and the posts aren't set deep.

## Where this goes in the subject
<!-- bite: 6406137f-39a0-4e8e-8e60-45f42bd12ea6 -->

The triple constraint is not just a definition to memorise. Two big topics later in this subject are the triple constraint made into a method:

1. **Crashing** (lessons 22 to 24) trades **cost for time**: you spend money on an activity to make it shorter, and you work out exactly which activity is cheapest to shorten.
2. **Resource levelling** (lessons 25 to 31) trades **time against what resources you have**: with fewer workers than the plan wants, the project takes longer.

The 2024/25 paper asks about the triple constraint directly. A full answer names the three, explains that they are linked, and gives one "change this, that moves" example.

To manage the three at all, a project needs an agreed way of working. The best-known one is PMBOK, and the next lesson is about how it organises a project.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 4fd7c50b-8d95-42c8-8c50-761bb03abe3a -->

1. **Listing the three with no link between them.** The marks are for "change one and another must move", with an example.
2. **Thinking you can fix all three.** If scope, time and cost are all fixed and something goes wrong, quality is what slips.
3. **Treating time and cost as separate topics.** The whole of crashing is about how they trade against each other.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 024cfb53-3d2d-4c54-bbe0-e2fe1f6df391 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: gap id="3c7e19a2" answers="scope|time|cost;scope|time|cost;scope|time|cost" -->
   The three sides of the triple constraint are ___, ___ and ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Scope, time and cost (in any order).

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="a9d40e5f" -->
   A client moves a website launch two weeks earlier but will not drop any features. What must most likely happen?

   - [ ] The cost falls, because the project is shorter
   - [x] The cost rises, for overtime or extra developers
   - [ ] Nothing, the three constraints are independent
   - [ ] The scope grows

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   With scope fixed and time cut, cost has to rise to buy the extra speed.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="6b2fd813" -->
   A church hall project's budget is cut by a third, and the opening date can't move. What is the most likely change?

   - [x] Part of the scope is dropped, such as the air conditioning
   - [ ] The hall opens earlier
   - [ ] Scope grows to make up for it
   - [ ] Nothing changes

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Cost and time are fixed, so scope has to give. If nobody admits that, quality is what quietly suffers.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: open id="f0e86c4d" -->
   Explain the triple constraint, with one example from a road-building project.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Scope, time and cost are the three main limits on a project, and they are linked, so a change in one forces a change in at least one other. Example: the government adds two extra kilometres to the road (scope grows), so the contractor needs more time, or more money for extra crews to finish on the original date.

   </details>
