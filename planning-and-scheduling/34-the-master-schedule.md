# The Master Schedule

The master schedule is the project's main timetable, and the other schedules are derived from it.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: c441ddc2-94fa-40d7-9ace-64df67260cb9 -->

You should have read [[planning-and-scheduling/33-bar-charts-line-of-balance-and-time-location-charts|33 — Bar Charts, Line of Balance and Time-Location Charts]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. list the steps to prepare a project schedule;
2. name the schedules derived from the master schedule and say what each is for;
3. explain why a schedule must be revised regularly, and what scheduling software does.

## What the master schedule is
<!-- bite: 7bb70bd9-c461-42f9-bfb7-5d5bce938c98 -->

Look at everything you've built for the fence: a WBS, a network, times from CPM or PERT, a crash plan, a smoothed worker profile and a bar chart. Each is a piece. The **master schedule** is where they come together into one agreed timetable for the whole project, the one everyone works from.

On a big job with several sub-projects (the fence, a gatehouse and a driveway, say), each sub-project has its own detailed schedule, and the master schedule sits above them, showing the main activities and milestones of all of them on one page.

## The steps to prepare it
<!-- bite: fafd2739-96dc-44d0-a72a-a6d33e791630 -->

From the slides, in order:

1. **Study the scope.** Know exactly what is to be delivered (lessons 03 to 07).
2. **Decide the method.** How will the work be done: by hand or with a hired digger?
3. **Break the scope into work packages and activities.** The WBS (lesson 06).
4. **Build the CPM or PERT network.** Logic, durations and the critical path (parts 3 to 5).
5. **Derive a week-by-week or month-by-month schedule** from the network, usually as a bar chart.
6. **Add a master schedule** if there are sub-projects, tying them together.
7. **Revise it regularly**, because real progress always differs from the plan.

Notice how the steps line up with this subject. That's not an accident: the subject follows the order a planner actually works in.

## The schedules that come out of it
<!-- bite: ec4bdafd-934a-46bf-a777-99be52c1d5c7 -->

Once the master schedule fixes when each activity happens, other teams can plan from it:

1. **Construction schedule**: This is when each piece of physical work happens, for the site team.
2. **Material schedule**: This is when each material must arrive on site. The wood must be there by day 3, the paint by day 4.
3. **Labour schedule**: This is how many workers of each trade are needed in each period: the loading histogram of lesson 25, made official.
4. **Equipment schedule**: This is when each machine is needed, so it can be hired for exactly those days.
5. **Financial schedule**: This is when money will be spent, so the owner knows how much cash is needed each month.
6. **Invoice schedule**: This is when the contractor will bill the client, usually at milestones.

Each one is the same timetable seen by a different department. Change the master schedule, and all six change with it.

## Why revision never stops
<!-- bite: 8dcb98ec-4656-4347-ab2c-19e1048df63a -->

Step 7 matters more than it looks. The plan is a prediction, and predictions drift. The wood arrives a day late; the painter is faster than expected. If the schedule isn't updated, it stops describing reality, and people stop trusting it. A schedule nobody trusts is worse than none.

This is the Monitoring and Controlling process group from lesson 04 at work. Measuring how far reality has drifted from the plan, in both time and money, is what earned value does, and that's the last lesson.

## Scheduling software
<!-- bite: a420869f-71ea-4479-807e-f14dae5cdff5 -->

The software named in class is **MS Project** and **Primavera**. They:

1. run the CPM forward and backward passes, however big the network;
2. draw Gantt charts and network diagrams from the same data;
3. let you assign resources to activities, see the loading, and level it;
4. compare actual progress against a saved baseline.

They do the arithmetic, not the thinking. The planner still has to get the logic, the durations and the resource needs right, which is everything this subject has been about.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 3e5524f2-2c73-40e4-b2ec-b39c7717bb38 -->

1. **Treating the schedule as fixed once approved.** It must be revised as the project runs.
2. **Thinking the derived schedules are separate plans.** They are views of the master schedule and must stay in step with it.
3. **Expecting software to fix bad logic.** Wrong dependencies give a wrong schedule, however neatly drawn.
