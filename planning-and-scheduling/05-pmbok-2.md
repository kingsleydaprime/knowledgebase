# PMBOK 2: The Ten Knowledge Areas, Methods and Certifications

PMBOK's ten knowledge areas are the ten things a project manager has to manage, from holding the plan together to dealing with the people affected.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 4bb2533d-379a-4635-aefd-1975ac5b8ca8 -->

You should have read [[planning-and-scheduling/04-pmbok-1|04 — PMBOK 1]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. name the ten knowledge areas and outline what each one manages;
2. say which knowledge area each later lesson of this subject belongs to;
3. name the main methodologies and certifications.

## What a knowledge area is
<!-- bite: 93ba90c2-c36a-4459-9a5e-a7fc8cbd2173 -->

The five process groups from the last lesson say *when* management happens: at the start, during, at the end. The knowledge areas say *what* is being managed. Every knowledge area has work in several process groups. Cost, for example, is estimated in planning, spent in executing and checked in monitoring.

The 2019/20 paper asks you to outline all ten for 10 marks, so one plain line each is worth knowing. Your slides give only the names, so the lines below are written from general project-management knowledge; check them against Assignment 1.

## The ten knowledge areas, 1 to 5
<!-- bite: dc5f9415-5233-4970-b44b-4f4b1137b551 -->

Here they are with the fence and gate as the example.

1. **Integration**: This is holding all the other areas together. It owns the overall project plan and change control. When the owner asks for a second gate, integration decides how the change goes through every other area.
2. **Scope**: This is deciding exactly what is and is not in the project. The work breakdown structure lives here. Is painting in? Is the gate lock in?
3. **Time (Schedule)**: This is sequencing activities, estimating their durations and building the schedule. This subject is mostly about this area.
4. **Cost**: This is estimating, budgeting and controlling money: the wood, the paint, the workers' pay.
5. **Quality**: This is making sure the work meets its standards and requirements: posts set deep enough, paint evenly coated.

## The ten knowledge areas, 6 to 10
<!-- bite: c5598397-57a9-4aea-99f8-0511e9581a30 -->

6. **Human Resources**: This is getting, developing and managing the team: hiring two labourers and a painter.
7. **Communications**: This is deciding who gets what information and when: the owner gets a progress call every Friday.
8. **Risk**: This is identifying, analysing and responding to uncertainty: what if it rains in week one?
9. **Procurement**: This is buying goods and services from outside: ordering the wood and the gate from a supplier.
10. **Stakeholder**: This is identifying and engaging everyone affected: the owner, the neighbours, the estate association.

A way to remember them: **I S T C Q H C R P S**, or say it as "I Saw Ten Cats Quietly Hunting; Cats Rarely Purr Softly".

## Methodologies and certifications
<!-- bite: 94acec56-1e9e-4f26-93a8-4e5b3ff28b85 -->

PMBOK is not the only way to run a project. Methodologies named in class:

1. **Predictive (waterfall)**: This is planning everything up front and following the plan in order. PMBOK-based work is usually this.
2. **Agile**: This is delivering in short cycles and adjusting as you learn. Scrum and Kanban are the common forms.
3. **Hybrid**: This mixes the two, such as a predictive plan for the building and agile for its software.
4. **Lean Six Sigma**: This is cutting waste and variation from processes.

Certifications named in class: PMP and CAPM (from PMI), PRINCE2 Foundation and Practitioner (PeopleCert/Axelos), CSM (Scrum Alliance), PSM I (Scrum.org), CompTIA Project+, the Google Project Management Certificate, and IPMA Level D. The exam fees on the slide change often, so check them before quoting.

That finishes the big picture. Part 2 starts the real planning: you can't schedule what you haven't listed, and the work breakdown structure is how you list it.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 61c980e6-9440-4f98-81a1-afedbc0eb4c1 -->

1. **Mixing knowledge areas and process groups.** Groups are stages (when); areas are subjects (what).
2. **Forgetting Stakeholder.** It was added as the tenth area in the 5th edition, and older lists stop at nine.
3. **Outlining with one word.** "Risk: risk" earns nothing. Say what is managed and give an example.

   How many knowledge areas does PMBOK (6th edition) have?

   10.

2. <!-- q: mcq id="2b8f6c14" -->
   A project manager signs a contract with a steel supplier for a bridge. Which knowledge area is this?

   - [ ] Integration
   - [ ] Scope
   - [x] Procurement
   - [ ] Quality

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Procurement: buying goods and services from outside the organisation.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="e09a47d6" -->
   The client asks for an extra floor on a building. Which knowledge area decides how that change flows through cost, schedule and the rest?

   - [x] Integration
   - [ ] Communications
   - [ ] Human Resources
   - [ ] Stakeholder

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Integration owns overall change control and holds the other areas together.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="c6a1b38f" -->
   Which of these are knowledge areas? Choose all that apply.

   - [x] Risk
   - [ ] Executing
   - [x] Communications
   - [ ] Closing
   - [x] Stakeholder

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Risk, Communications and Stakeholder are knowledge areas. Executing and Closing are process groups.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="94e2fd5b" -->
   Outline any four knowledge areas, with one example each from building a school.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Any four with meaning and example, for instance: Scope (exactly what is built: 12 classrooms and a lab, no hall); Cost (estimating and controlling the budget for blocks, roofing and labour); Risk (planning for the rainy season flooding the foundations); Communications (weekly reports to the ministry and the head teacher).

   </details>
