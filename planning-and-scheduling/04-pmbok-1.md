# PMBOK 1: Editions, Process Groups and the Level of Activity

PMBOK is the Project Management Institute's guide to running projects, and up to its 6th edition it sorts the work into five process groups.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: f2a5cff7-b153-446a-a9ed-7e4bdce8e02b -->

You should have read [[planning-and-scheduling/03-the-triple-constraint|03 — The Triple Constraint]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. list the PMBOK editions and say what changed in the 7th;
2. name the five process groups in order and say what each does;
3. sketch the level-of-activity diagram and explain its shape.

## What PMBOK is
<!-- bite: ebae067b-1c91-4cae-8378-1dabff55fe53 -->

Last lesson ended with a question: to balance scope, time and cost, a project needs an agreed way of working. PMBOK is the most widely used one. It doesn't tell you how to build a fence. It tells you which management steps any project needs, from getting approval to handing over, so nothing important is forgotten.

## The PMBOK editions
<!-- bite: a097f243-c3f6-4ba2-ad0c-353184640158 -->

PMBOK has changed over the years, and the biggest change came in the 7th edition.

It has been revised many times:

1. **Editions**: 1996, 2000, 2004 (3rd), 2008 (4th), 2013 (5th), 2017 (6th) and 2021 (7th).
2. **Up to the 6th edition**: 5 process groups, 47 processes and 10 knowledge areas.
3. **The 7th edition**: drops the process structure and uses 8 **performance domains** instead: Stakeholder, Team, Development Approach and Life Cycle, Planning, Project Work, Delivery, Measurement, and Uncertainty.

The exams use the 6th-edition picture: five process groups and ten knowledge areas. This lesson does the process groups; the next does the knowledge areas.

## The five process groups
<!-- bite: d845ce90-8d27-41c2-a913-23b53b044a6c -->

A process group is a set of management steps that belong to the same stage of running a project. Follow the fence through them:

1. **Initiating**: This is defining the project and getting permission to start. The owner agrees to pay for a fence and gate, and a project manager is named.
2. **Planning**: This is working out how the job will be done: the activities, the order, the times, the costs and the workers. Most of this subject sits here.
3. **Executing**: This is doing the work in the plan: digging, building, painting.
4. **Monitoring and Controlling**: This is tracking progress against the plan and correcting it. The wood is late, so the plan is changed.
5. **Closing**: This is finishing formally: the owner accepts the fence, the bills are paid, and the lessons learned are written down.

Remember the order with the first letters: **I P E M C**.

## The level of activity across the groups
<!-- bite: 93ecb765-6c7c-4744-aff8-74a11d289ae3 -->

Asked in 2019/20, 2023/24 and 2024/25, for 7 to 9 marks with a diagram. The groups aren't five separate blocks one after the other. They overlap, and each one rises and falls at its own time.

![Five overlapping humps against project time: Initiating is a small early hump, Planning rises early and peaks before the middle, Executing is the largest hump in the middle, Monitoring and Controlling runs from early to the end rising with executing, and Closing is a small hump at the end](images/ps-level-of-activity.svg)

## Reading the level-of-activity diagram
<!-- bite: 58a71a7b-eb63-48b1-9092-9ea9f2c800b0 -->

The picture above is the one the exam asks you to draw; here is what each hump says.

Read it left to right:

1. **Initiating** is a short burst at the very start.
2. **Planning** rises fast just after, is heavy early, and never quite stops, because plans get revised.
3. **Executing** builds up and is the biggest hump, in the middle. This is where most of the effort and money go.
4. **Monitoring and Controlling** runs alongside from planning to the end, rising and falling with executing, because there is most to check when most work is happening.
5. **Closing** is a short burst at the end.

To draw it in the exam: time on the horizontal axis, level of activity on the vertical, one labelled hump per group, overlapping.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: cc55d7c5-1b93-46b0-96fc-f793082e77b0 -->

1. **Drawing the groups as five boxes in a row.** They overlap; that is the point of the diagram.
2. **Making planning the biggest hump.** Executing is the biggest. Planning is heavy early but smaller.
3. **Forgetting monitoring runs the whole way.** It starts with planning and only stops at the end.
4. **Quoting the 7th edition's domains as process groups.** The domains replaced the process structure; the exam wants the five groups.

The process groups tell you *when* things happen. The ten knowledge areas tell you *what* is being managed, and that's the next lesson.

   - [ ] Closing

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Monitoring and Controlling: tracking progress against the plan and correcting it.

   </details>
