# Rolling Wave Planning and Reserves

Rolling wave planning means planning the near future in detail and the far future roughly, and reserves are extra time or money set aside for the surprises no plan can list.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: b5120fc1-1cf0-4aa6-9448-935aea1826d5 -->

You should have read [[planning-and-scheduling/06-work-breakdown-structure|06 — The Work Breakdown Structure]].

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. explain rolling wave planning and when to use it;
2. tell a contingency reserve from a management reserve on three points;
3. say which reserve covers a given surprise.

## What rolling wave planning is
<!-- bite: c598ad55-65b1-4114-8029-31bdd1aa07d8 -->

The WBS in the last lesson assumed you could see the whole fence at the start. Many projects aren't like that. Think of building a new estate over three years. You know exactly what happens in the next month: clear the site, survey it. You have no idea yet exactly how the houses in year three will be finished, because the buyers haven't chosen.

Rolling wave planning handles this. Work that is a few weeks away is broken down into full activities. Work months away stays as one high-level work package until more is known. As the project moves forward, the next stretch gets planned in detail, so the detailed part of the plan "rolls" forward like a wave.

A one-sentence version for an exam: rolling wave planning is a form of progressive planning in which near-term work is planned in detail and later work is kept at a higher level, then broken down as the project progresses and more is known.

It suits projects where the later stages can't be defined yet. The 2023/24 paper asks for it for 4 marks: explain the idea, say why it is used, give one example.

## Why not plan everything in detail up front?
<!-- bite: 96d46134-dae3-4b8e-adf4-ac804112617f -->

Planning the far future in detail looks careful, but it wastes effort and gives false confidence.

1. **The details will change.** Planning which tiles go in a house that hasn't been sold yet means planning it twice.
2. **The estimates are guesses anyway.** A detailed plan for year three looks precise but rests on nothing.
3. **It hides the real uncertainty.** A rough package honestly says "we don't know yet".

Rolling wave planning accepts that some of the future is unknown. But even the near-term plan will meet surprises. That is what reserves are for.

## What reserves are
<!-- bite: acc8c6bc-b210-4170-8985-50802f376f69 -->

A reserve is extra money or time set aside for things going wrong. Every real plan needs one, because no plan survives contact with the site unchanged. There are two kinds, and they differ in what kind of surprise they cover and who controls them.

1. **Contingency reserve**: This is money or time set aside for risks you have already identified, the "known unknowns". You know rain might stop the fence-digging in week one; you don't know if it will. So you add two days.
2. **Management reserve**: This is money or time set aside for things nobody foresaw, the "unknown unknowns". Nobody listed "the supplier's warehouse burns down", but something like it can happen.

## The three differences
<!-- bite: 8c85bdfa-c606-4771-ae46-15684f167506 -->

The 2023/24 paper asks you to tell them apart for 3 marks. One point each:

1. **What it covers.** Contingency covers identified risks ("known unknowns"). Management reserve covers unforeseen work or events ("unknown unknowns").
2. **Whether it is in the baseline.** Contingency is part of the cost and schedule baseline, the approved plan you measure against. Management reserve is held outside the baseline.
3. **Who releases it.** The project manager can use contingency when a listed risk happens. Management reserve needs senior management's approval, and once used, it is added to the baseline.

Your slides don't define these two, so this is written from general project-management knowledge.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 8ca5172a-f521-417e-90ab-6b21e5d6b408 -->

1. **Thinking rolling wave means "no plan" for later.** Later work is planned, just at a high level.
2. **Swapping the two reserves.** Known risks go to contingency; unforeseen events go to management reserve.
3. **Saying both are in the baseline.** Only contingency is.

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="e8b1d4a2" -->
   Which project suits rolling wave planning best?

   - [ ] Repainting a classroom next week
   - [x] A four-year research programme whose later stages depend on early results
   - [ ] Printing 500 copies of a known book
   - [ ] Replacing one broken window

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The research programme: its later stages can't be defined until the early results are in.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="47c9f0e3" -->
   A builder knows the cement price might rise next quarter and sets aside N2 million in the approved budget for it. What is this?

   - [x] A contingency reserve
   - [ ] A management reserve
   - [ ] A work package
   - [ ] Rolling wave planning

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A contingency reserve: it covers an identified risk and sits inside the baseline.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: gap id="a03f6e7d" answers="contingency;management" -->
   Known unknowns are covered by the ___ reserve; unknown unknowns are covered by the ___ reserve.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Contingency, management.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="5d2e81b6" -->
   Who usually has to approve the use of the management reserve?

   - [ ] Any team member
   - [ ] The project manager alone
   - [x] Senior management
   - [ ] The supplier

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Senior management. The project manager controls contingency, not the management reserve.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="f17c5a09" -->
   Explain rolling wave planning, with an example, and give one reason it is used.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Rolling wave planning plans the near-term work in full detail and keeps later work as high-level packages, breaking each down as it gets closer and more is known. Example: a new estate plans site clearing in detail now but leaves "finish houses in phase 3" as one package until buyers choose their designs. It is used because detailed plans for the far future would be guesses that have to be redone.

   </details>
