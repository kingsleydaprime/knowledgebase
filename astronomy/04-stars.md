# Stars

> **[Intermediate]** · What a star is, why mass decides everything, and how they end.

**A star is a self-gravitating ball of plasma in hydrostatic equilibrium**, fusing light elements into heavier ones and radiating the energy released.

**The equilibrium is the whole story.** Gravity pulls inward; radiation and gas pressure from fusion push outward. **A star is a controlled explosion balanced against its own collapse for billions of years** — and every stage of stellar evolution is that balance being renegotiated as fuel runs out.

## Mass decides everything

**Given a star's mass and composition, essentially everything else follows** — its luminosity, temperature, colour, lifetime and death. That's an unusually strong statement for astrophysics, and it's why the field organises around mass.

The counterintuitive part: **more massive stars live far shorter lives.** A star with 10× the Sun's mass has ~10× the fuel but burns it roughly 10,000× faster.

| Mass | Lifetime | Ends as |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 M☉ (red dwarf) | **> 10 trillion years** | Still burning — none has died yet |
| 1 M☉ (Sun) | ~10 billion years | White dwarf |
| 10 M☉ | ~20 million years | **Supernova → neutron star** |
| 30+ M☉ | ~5 million years | **Supernova → black hole** |

**The universe is 13.8 billion years old, so every red dwarf ever formed still exists.** They're also the most common star by far — roughly three-quarters of all stars — which matters for where to look for planets.

## The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

**The single most important diagram in astronomy.** Plot luminosity against temperature and stars fall into distinct regions rather than scattering:

```
  luminosity
     ▲
 10⁶ │        ★ supergiants
     │   ────────────────────
 10⁴ │      ★ ★  giants
     │        ╲
 10² │          ╲  MAIN
   1 │            ╲ SEQUENCE  ☉
10⁻² │              ╲
     │   · white      ╲
10⁻⁴ │     dwarfs       ╲
     └──────────────────────────▶
      hot / blue        cool / red
```

**~90% of stars sit on the main sequence** — that's simply where a star spends most of its life, fusing hydrogen in its core. Position along it is set by mass.

**Temperature runs backwards** on the conventional axis, another Hipparchus-era legacy.

**Spectral classes** — O B A F G K M, hottest to coolest. The Sun is **G2V**. Modern surveys added L, T, Y for brown dwarfs.

**Why the diagram is powerful: it's an evolutionary map.** A cluster's stars all formed together, so plotting them shows where the massive ones have already left the main sequence — and **the turn-off point dates the cluster.** That's how globular clusters were shown to be ~12–13 billion years old, which put a hard floor under the age of the universe.

## The life of a star

**Formation** — a cold molecular cloud fragments and collapses under gravity. Contraction heats the core; at **~10 million K** hydrogen fusion ignites and the collapse halts. The star joins the main sequence.

**Main sequence** — hydrogen → helium in the core. Stable, and most of the star's life.

**After core hydrogen** — the core contracts and heats; the envelope expands enormously. **Red giant.** The Sun will engulf Mercury and Venus, and probably Earth, in ~5 billion years.

**Then it depends on mass:**

**Low/intermediate (< ~8 M☉):** helium fuses to carbon and oxygen; the star cannot get hot enough to go further. The outer layers drift away as a **planetary nebula** — a misleading name from early low-resolution observations — leaving a **white dwarf**: an Earth-sized object of degenerate matter, supported not by fusion but by **electron degeneracy pressure**, a quantum-mechanical effect. It simply cools for the rest of time.

**High mass (> ~8 M☉):** fusion continues through carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon — each stage faster than the last, building an onion-shell structure. **Silicon burning lasts about a day.**

**It stops at iron**, and this is the pivotal fact in stellar physics: **fusing iron consumes energy rather than releasing it.** The core's support vanishes, it collapses in under a second, and the infalling envelope rebounds in a **core-collapse supernova**.

## Why you are made of stars

**The Big Bang produced hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. Nothing else.**

Everything heavier was made in stars:
- **Carbon, oxygen, nitrogen** — fusion in stellar cores, dispersed by winds and planetary nebulae
- **Elements up to iron** — fusion in massive stars
- **Heavier than iron** — cannot come from ordinary fusion. Made by **neutron capture**: slowly (s-process) in giant stars, and rapidly (r-process) in **neutron-star mergers** — confirmed observationally in 2017 → [[astronomy/01-what-astronomy-is|multi-messenger astronomy]]

**The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were made inside a star that exploded before the Sun formed.** That statement is literal, quantitative and checkable — the isotope ratios in meteorites record it.

## What we can measure, and how

**Distance** — parallax for nearby stars (Gaia measured ~2 billion); **standard candles** further out → [[astronomy/07-cosmology|note 07]].

**Mass** — reliably only for **binary systems**, from orbital mechanics. Roughly half of all stars are in binaries, which is fortunate, because otherwise stellar masses would be almost unconstrained.

**Radius** — from luminosity and temperature via the Stefan–Boltzmann law; directly for eclipsing binaries.

**Composition, temperature, motion** — spectroscopy → [[astronomy/03-light-and-instruments|note 03]].

**Interior structure** — **asteroseismology**, reading the star's pulsation modes like seismic waves. Genuinely remarkable: you can infer the internal structure of a distant star from tiny brightness oscillations.
