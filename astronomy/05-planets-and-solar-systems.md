# Planets and Solar Systems

> **[Intermediate]** · How planetary systems form, our own, and the five thousand others we've found since 1995.

## Formation

**Planets form as a by-product of star formation.** A collapsing cloud has angular momentum, so it flattens into a rotating **protoplanetary disc** — a fact confirmed directly when ALMA imaged discs with gaps carved by forming planets.

Then: **dust grains collide and stick → pebbles → planetesimals (km-scale) → protoplanets.** Close to the star it's too hot for volatiles, so rocky planets form; beyond the **snow line**, water ice is solid and available, so cores grow large enough to capture hydrogen and helium and become gas giants.

**The step that's genuinely hard is metre-scale.** Objects around a metre across should spiral into the star faster than they can grow, and should shatter rather than stick when they collide. **The "metre barrier" is an open problem**, with streaming instabilities and pebble accretion as leading answers — an honest example of a field with a well-tested framework and real gaps in it.

## The Solar System

**Rocky inner planets:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars.
**Gas giants:** Jupiter, Saturn. **Ice giants:** Uranus, Neptune — mostly water, ammonia and methane, not hydrogen.
**Then:** the asteroid belt, the Kuiper belt, and the hypothesised Oort cloud out to ~100,000 AU.

**Things worth knowing:**

**Jupiter dominates everything but the Sun.** It's 2.5× the mass of all other planets combined, and its gravity shaped the whole architecture — including, probably, why Mars is small and why the asteroid belt never formed a planet.

**Venus and Earth are near-twins that diverged catastrophically.** Similar size and composition; Venus has a runaway greenhouse, a 470°C surface, and 92 atmospheres of pressure. **Understanding why is directly relevant to climate science.**

**The moons are as interesting as the planets.** **Europa** and **Enceladus** have subsurface liquid-water oceans under ice, kept liquid by tidal heating. **Titan** has a thick atmosphere and liquid methane lakes. **These are the leading places to look for life in the Solar System**, not Mars.

**Pluto's reclassification** wasn't a demotion so much as a definition problem: once Eris was found at a similar size, either both were planets or neither was, and the IAU chose "clears its orbital neighbourhood" as the criterion. **Pluto is now the type example of a dwarf planet**, and New Horizons showed it to be geologically active — far more interesting than its label.

## Exoplanets

**Before 1995 we knew of zero planets around other stars. There are now over 5,700 confirmed**, and the number of *known* planets outside the Solar System vastly exceeds the number inside it.

**How they're found:**

**Transit** — a planet crossing its star dims it slightly. Kepler and TESS used this and it has found the most. Gives **radius** and **orbital period**. Requires the orbit to be edge-on from our view, so it's geometrically biased.

**Radial velocity** — the star wobbles around the shared centre of mass, shifting its spectrum. Gives **minimum mass** and period. The method of the first detection.

**Together they give density**, which tells you rocky vs gaseous — which is why systems with both measurements are disproportionately valuable.

**Direct imaging** — extremely hard (the star is a billion times brighter) but improving; works best for young, hot, widely separated giants.

**Microlensing** — the planet's gravity briefly magnifies a background star. Finds distant and free-floating planets; one-off events that can't be re-observed.

**What we've learned, and much of it was a surprise:**

- **Planetary systems are common.** Most stars have planets
- **Hot Jupiters** — giants orbiting in days — were the first found and were not predicted. They must have **migrated** inward, which rewrote formation theory
- **Super-Earths and mini-Neptunes** (between Earth and Neptune) are the **most common type known**, and there are none in our Solar System
- **Our architecture is not typical**, as far as we can tell — though detection biases make "typical" hard to establish honestly

**Bias is the recurring caveat:** every method favours large planets close to their stars. **The absence of Earth-analogues in the catalogue is substantially a detection limit, not a fact about the universe.**

## Habitability

**The habitable zone** — where liquid water could exist on a surface. Useful, and much cruder than it sounds: it ignores atmosphere (Venus is arguably in it), tidal heating (Europa is far outside it and has an ocean), and atmospheric loss.

**Red dwarfs** are the interesting case. They're the most common stars and their habitable zones are close in, so planets there are easier to detect. **But** close orbits mean tidal locking, and red dwarfs flare violently, which may strip atmospheres. **Genuinely unresolved.**

**Biosignatures** — the current frontier. JWST can measure some exoplanet atmospheric composition via transmission spectroscopy. **Oxygen plus methane together** is a promising combination, because they react quickly and shouldn't coexist without replenishment.

**The honest state: no biosignature detection has been confirmed.** Claims appear, get scrutinised, and usually weaken. **That cycle is science working correctly**, and it's worth watching a few rounds of it before believing a headline → [[research/README|research]].
