# Concept Generation 3: Ideas From Outside, and Evaluating Concepts

Three more concept generation techniques borrow ideas from outside the problem: from nature, from other fields, and from a crowd.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: d6138e56-f870-4cf0-b138-ffafdfeb34c9 -->

You should know brainstorming, morphological analysis, TRIZ ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/08-concept-generation-1|Concept Generation 1]]) and SCAMPER ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/09-concept-generation-2|Concept Generation 2]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. describe biomimicry, design analogy and collective intelligence techniques, with an example of each;
2. name the five factors for evaluating concepts;
3. give the additional tips for concept generation.

## What these three techniques are
<!-- bite: 15c8859f-80b2-47cb-bd7f-bf71f07f138d -->

So far, every technique worked on the problem itself: brainstorming it, charting its functions, applying TRIZ principles to it, asking SCAMPER questions about it.

The last three look somewhere else for the answer:

- **Biomimicry** looks at nature.
- **Design analogy** looks at other domains.
- **Collective intelligence** looks at a crowd of people.

The idea is the same each time: someone (or something) has probably solved a problem like yours already.

## 5. Biomimicry
<!-- bite: 0556d962-6513-4293-8d86-2d22abbbed6e -->

Biomimicry is seeking inspiration from nature's solutions to similar problems.

Nature has had millions of years to test its designs. Example: Japan's bullet trains used to make a loud boom when they came out of tunnels. Engineers noticed that the kingfisher dives from air into water with hardly a splash, because of the long pointed shape of its beak. They reshaped the train's nose like the beak, and the train became quieter, faster and used less electricity.

## 6. Design analogy
<!-- bite: 36849b10-f72d-4599-8a92-da03823ab4d4 -->

Design analogy is drawing inspiration from existing solutions in other domains and adapting them to your specific problem.

It's like biomimicry, but the source is another field of human work, not nature. Example: a children's hospital wanted to make handing patients from the operating theatre to intensive care safer. They studied how Formula 1 pit crews change tyres in seconds, with every person having one job and a fixed order. They copied that into a handover routine, and mistakes went down.

If you noticed this is close to SCAMPER's "Adapt", you're right. Adapt is the question; design analogy is the technique of going to look in another field for the answer.

## 7. Collective intelligence techniques
<!-- bite: 113b2e5e-30af-4d94-95ce-68f99e9648a0 -->

Collective intelligence techniques leverage the power of crowdsourcing and online platforms to gather diverse ideas from a broader community. Examples are InnoCentive and NineSigma.

A company posts its problem on the platform, often with a cash prize, and thousands of people from different fields send solutions. The person who solves a chemistry problem might be a retired engineer on another continent. It's brainstorming with the whole world in the room.

That's all seven techniques. Now we have many concepts. What do we do with them?

## Concept evaluation and refinement
<!-- bite: 49b02b91-e8f3-449a-828d-b9ad327d3f92 -->

Once concepts are generated, evaluate each one based on the defined criteria. Select the most promising concepts and refine them by identifying potential improvements. This may involve developing sketches, prototypes and so on, to test and validate your concept before finalizing your design.

Factors to consider:

1. **Feasibility**: can it be built with available resources and technology?
2. **Cost**: to design, manufacture and maintain.
3. **Functionality**: does it meet the objectives and requirements?
4. **Safety**: for users and the environment.
5. **Aesthetics**: how it looks.

This is a bridge: the next three lessons are proper methods for doing this evaluation fairly.

## Additional tips for concept generation
<!-- bite: 0348872b-c041-48c9-84d6-c1aa3b5c152a -->

1. Focus on the user.
2. Embrace ambiguity.
3. Challenge assumptions.
4. Build on existing knowledge.
5. Seek feedback and collaborate.

Concept generation is iterative, so be willing to revisit concepts as you get more information and feedback.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 415a256a-b4a0-42f2-aa47-51e186487df8 -->

1. **Copying nature exactly.** Biomimicry borrows the *principle* (a pointed shape that cuts through air), not the animal.
2. **Mixing up biomimicry and design analogy.** Biomimicry takes from nature; design analogy takes from another human field.
3. **Evaluating on one factor.** The cheapest concept may be unsafe. Check all five factors.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: ee217ed0-504f-42db-a515-a0e0cd9d3614 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="3e8a1f62" -->
   Engineers design a building that stays cool without air conditioning by copying how termite mounds move air. Which technique is this?

   - [x] Biomimicry
   - [ ] Design analogy
   - [ ] Collective intelligence

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Biomimicry: the inspiration comes from nature (termites).

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="b75d0c94" -->
   A company posts a design problem online with a prize, and receives 2,000 solutions from people around the world. Which technique is this?

   - [ ] Brainstorming
   - [ ] Design analogy
   - [x] Collective intelligence

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Collective intelligence: crowdsourcing ideas through an online platform such as InnoCentive or NineSigma.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="1fc7e2a8" -->
   A food factory copies the barcode scanning system used in supermarkets to track its ingredients. Which technique is this?

   - [ ] Biomimicry
   - [x] Design analogy
   - [ ] TRIZ

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Design analogy: a solution from another domain (retail) adapted to this problem.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="6d40b9e3" -->
   Which of these are factors to consider when evaluating concepts? Choose all that apply.

   - [x] Feasibility
   - [x] Safety
   - [x] Aesthetics
   - [ ] Number of team members who suggested it

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Feasibility, safety and aesthetics. The other two factors are cost and functionality. Who suggested an idea doesn't matter.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="c09e5a71" -->
   Why is concept generation described as iterative?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Because you keep revisiting concepts as you get more information and feedback. A concept that looked weak may improve after testing, and new information may bring new concepts.

   </details>
