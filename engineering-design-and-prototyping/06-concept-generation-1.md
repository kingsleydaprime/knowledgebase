# Concept Generation 1: Brainstorming, Morphological Analysis and TRIZ

Concept generation is the second phase of engineering design, where we move from the problem definition to potential solutions.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 7a9c643b-0069-4a94-a525-b43dc5105319 -->

You should be able to write a problem definition ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/04-problem-definition|lesson 04]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. explain why diverse concepts matter;
2. describe brainstorming and morphological analysis, and build a small morphological chart;
3. say what TRIZ is and apply a few of its 40 principles.

## What concept generation is
<!-- bite: cd577402-f39e-465f-8662-7b67cc28c6c5 -->

Okay! The problem is defined. Now we need answers.

Here we move from problem definition to potential solutions. Concept generation involves brainstorming and exploring various ideas to address the identified needs and constraints.

A **concept** is one possible answer to the problem, described roughly: "a backpack with wheels", "a backpack that opens from the back". Not the dimensions yet, just the idea.

There are seven techniques for generating concepts. This lesson covers the first three, and the next two lessons cover the rest. But first: why do we want many concepts, not just one?

## Importance of diverse concepts
<!-- bite: 6a98748e-d370-482d-bc35-3b3feee35be4 -->

- Diverse concepts are essential for finding the best possible solution.
- They avoid being limited to conventional solutions or simply improving existing ideas.
- Diversity arises from exploring different functionalities, technologies, materials and design principles.
- Aim for a broad range of concepts, even some that seem unconventional or outlandish at first.

If your only idea is "the old bag, but slightly better", you'll never find the bag that's ten times better. The crazy idea sometimes has one good part you can keep.

## 1. Brainstorming
<!-- bite: d70ba309-6197-4b8a-a17d-efda55b37e3b -->

Gather individuals (ideally with diverse backgrounds) to freely generate ideas without judgement. Encourage quantity over quality at this stage.

Two variations help:

- **Brainwriting**: everyone writes ideas down quietly first, then shares. The quiet person gets heard as much as the loud one.
- **Round-robin brainstorming**: people take turns, one idea each, round the circle.

Both help overcome inhibitions and encourage participation. Judging comes later, in concept selection. If you judge now, people stop talking.

## 2. Morphological analysis
<!-- bite: 0b1d947e-be6d-4e56-ba65-ca76f39ae320 -->

Morphological analysis involves identifying the key functional elements of the design and then exploring all possible combinations of their attributes. It ensures a thorough exploration of the design space and can lead to unexpected solutions.

It sounds big, but it's just a table. Write each function of the design in a row, and the ways to do it across:

| Function | Option 1 | Option 2 | Option 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carry the load | Two shoulder straps | One cross-body strap | Wheels and a handle |
| Close the bag | Zip | Roll-top and clip | Magnetic flap |
| Organise items | Inside pockets | Removable pouches | One open space |

Pick one option from each row and you have a concept. "Wheels, roll-top, removable pouches" is one. With 3 options in 3 rows, there are 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 concepts, many of which nobody would have thought of directly.

## 3. TRIZ
<!-- bite: f6390a47-0844-48c3-bdab-ac5bad586166 -->

TRIZ stands for the Theory of Inventive Problem Solving. The letters come from the Russian name, which is why they don't match the English words.

TRIZ provides a structured framework for identifying and overcoming design challenges. It uses a database of **40 inventive principles** and **contradiction matrices** to guide the search for innovative solutions.

A contradiction is when improving one thing makes another worse: a stronger bag is heavier. The matrix says which of the 40 principles have solved that kind of contradiction before, so you don't start from zero.

The next four sections are the 40 principles, as listed in the lecture. Published TRIZ lists name some principles differently, so for the exam use these names.

## The 40 principles of TRIZ: 1 to 10
<!-- bite: 07814a6c-3d36-4d87-b532-09060e68febf -->

1. Segmentation: divide the object into independent parts
2. Taking out: take out unnecessary parts or features
3. Local quality: change the local quality of an object
4. Asymmetry: make the object asymmetrical
5. Merging: merge two or more objects
6. Universality: make the object multifunctional
7. Nesting: place one object inside another
8. Counterweight: balance the object
9. Preliminary action: pre-arrange the object to perform a desired function
10. Prior action: perform a prior action to prevent a potential problem

## The 40 principles of TRIZ: 11 to 20
<!-- bite: b3902bd3-0190-4eec-952a-8be8df22e323 -->

11. Cushioning: cushion objects to reduce impact
12. Composite materials: combine different materials to achieve desired properties
13. Parameter change: change the parameters of an object
14. Parameter variation: vary the parameters of an object
15. Dynamicity: make the object dynamic
16. Partial or excessive action: use partial or excessive action to achieve the desired result
17. Another dimension: move the object into another dimension
18. Mechanical vibration: use vibration to improve or achieve a function
19. Periodic action: make the object perform periodic actions
20. Continuity of useful action: make the object perform useful actions continuously

## The 40 principles of TRIZ: 21 to 30
<!-- bite: 2b965792-3a09-4c9d-a012-ac67cde36061 -->

21. Skipping: skip steps in a process
22. Blessing in disguise: turn a harmful factor into a beneficial one
23. Feedback: use feedback to control the object or process
24. Intermediary: introduce an intermediary object or process
25. Self-service: make the object serve itself
26. Copying: copy useful features from other objects or systems
27. Cheap substitutes: replace expensive parts with cheaper ones
28. Parameter modification: change the parameters of an object or process
29. Mechanical mixture: mix different objects or substances
30. Composite material property change: change the properties of a composite material

## The 40 principles of TRIZ: 31 to 40
<!-- bite: 81017b84-b2f3-4924-af7c-54f07023c736 -->

31. Porous materials: use porous materials to achieve desired functions
32. Color change: change the color of an object
33. Parameter homogeneity: make the parameters of an object or process uniform
34. Discarding and recovering: discard unnecessary parts and recover them later
35. Parameter change and recovery: change parameters and then recover them
36. Phase change: change the phase of an object or substance
37. Thermal expansion: use thermal expansion to achieve desired functions
38. Strong oxidants: use strong oxidants to achieve desired results
39. Inert atmosphere: use an inert atmosphere to prevent unwanted reactions
40. Composite material substitution: substitute one composite material with another

## Using TRIZ on the backpack
<!-- bite: 43c63761-861d-4a62-a869-db15b62c4968 -->

These principles are only a starting point. The key is to use them creatively and adapt them to your specific problem. Take the backpack:

- **Segmentation (1):** split the bag into modules: a laptop sleeve that zips off, and a main bag.
- **Nesting (7):** a small pencil case that lives inside a pocket inside the bag.
- **Universality (6):** the bag's back panel doubles as a seat cushion.
- **Cushioning (11):** padding on the straps to reduce the impact on the shoulders.

Four principles, four new concepts. TRIZ has one famous cousin that works by asking questions instead: SCAMPER, in the next lesson.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: b6864628-94ca-46df-803c-8f0323864895 -->

1. **Judging ideas during brainstorming.** Quantity first. Judging is for concept selection.
2. **Thinking TRIZ stands for English words.** The letters come from the Russian name.
3. **Using the principles as rules.** They are prompts. Adapt them to your problem.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: e8942dce-20a5-4da5-918a-d8052476c2ab -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: exact id="6b3e1d07" answer="40" accept="forty" -->
   How many inventive principles does TRIZ have?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   40, used together with contradiction matrices.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: exact id="d07a4c59" answer="24" -->
   A morphological chart for a desk lamp has three functions. "Give light" has 4 options, "Hold position" has 3 options and "Switch on" has 2 options. How many possible concepts can you make by picking one option per function?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   4 × 3 × 2 = 24.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="81fc6e2b" -->
   A designer adds a phone holder and a cup holder to a laptop stand so it does more than one job. Which TRIZ principle is this?

   - [ ] Segmentation
   - [x] Universality
   - [ ] Nesting
   - [ ] Taking out

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Universality: make the object multifunctional.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="2c9fa0d4" -->
   Russian dolls sit one inside the other. Which TRIZ principle does that picture show?

   - [ ] Merging
   - [x] Nesting
   - [ ] Counterweight

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Nesting: place one object inside another.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="5fe8b371" -->
   In brainwriting, what happens?

   - [x] Everyone writes ideas down quietly first, then shares them
   - [ ] One person writes down the best ideas the group agrees on
   - [ ] Ideas are scored as they are written

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Everyone writes ideas quietly first. It helps people who wouldn't speak up, and nothing is judged yet.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="a6c0e3f8" -->
   Why should a design team aim for diverse concepts, including some that seem outlandish?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Diverse concepts are essential for finding the best solution. They stop the team from being limited to conventional solutions or small improvements on existing ideas. An outlandish concept may fail as a whole but contain a part worth keeping.

   </details>
