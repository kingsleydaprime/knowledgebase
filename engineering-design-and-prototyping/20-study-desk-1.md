# Study Desk 1: Defining the Problem and Choosing a Concept

This worked example takes one real problem, a student's study desk, through the engineering design process, and this part covers phases 1 to 3.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 237d4415-5194-4c65-bb66-6ee5b65df1f6 -->

You should know phases 1 to 3: problem definition ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/04-problem-definition|lesson 04]]), concept generation ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/06-concept-generation-1|lessons 06]] to [[engineering-design-and-prototyping/08-concept-generation-3|08]]) and the decision matrix ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/11-concept-selection-3|lesson 11]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. write a full problem definition for a design task;
2. generate concepts using brainstorming, TRIZ and SCAMPER together;
3. select a concept with a decision matrix and justify the choice.

## What this worked example is
<!-- bite: 5b171bc2-694a-425f-8e42-dbb4109e8bda -->

This is the term paper. The task was: **"Generate any problem and solve it using the engineering design process."**

The problem chosen: **improving the ergonomics and organisation of a student's study desk.** Anyone who has read with a laptop flat on a table knows this one: your neck bends down, and books, pens and chargers end up everywhere.

Use this as a model whenever an exam question says "illustrate with an engineering example". Every phase from lesson 2 appears here, in order.

## Phase 1: Problem definition
<!-- bite: bb67ca7a-9df0-46ad-b748-5afc36270ac4 -->

- **Objective:** Create a desk accessory that reduces strain while reading and keeps the student's desk organised.
- **Requirements:**
  1. Must hold the laptop and notes at eye level.
  2. Should be able to hold at least 4 textbooks.
  3. Should have a dedicated space for stationery.

## Phase 1: Constraints and criteria for success
<!-- bite: 19b2cff5-d9a6-4848-81c7-54f79b940ca0 -->

The problem definition goes on with what limits the design and how we'll know it worked:

- **Constraints:**
  1. Should cost less than ₦20,000 to manufacture.
  2. Should be lightweight.
  3. Should be eco-friendly.
- **Criteria for success:**
  1. Should measure a 20% reduction in user-reported neck strain.
  2. Should achieve a "high" rating in satisfaction surveys.

See how each part does its own job: the objective is the goal, requirements say what it must do, constraints limit the choices, and the criteria for success are numbers we can test later.

## Phase 2: Concept generation, brainstorming
<!-- bite: af8644fd-77fe-4858-8c74-666fb317e284 -->

We start by brainstorming. Ideas include:

1. A foldable laptop stand with built-in drawers.
2. A rotating stand for books.
3. A standing desk converter.

Three different directions, not three versions of one idea. That's the diversity lesson 6 asked for.

## Phase 2: Applying the principles of TRIZ
<!-- bite: 2645f4b1-de70-468e-8112-c6cc2679a4c2 -->

- **Segmentation:** divide the organiser into modules, one part for the laptop and the other for the stationery, for flexibility.
- **Dynamicity:** make the organiser flexible and adaptable to perform its function. Give the stationery holders magnets or sliding rails so the user can configure the layout depending on whether they are typing, drawing and so on. Make the accessory collapsible so it can fold or retract when not in use.
- **Universality:** make it serve another function as well, for example let it have a cup holder.

## Phase 2: Then SCAMPER
<!-- bite: 8c5a4c83-b744-4448-9d19-37ad538ebd5d -->

- **Combine** a desk lamp with a wireless charging pad.
- **Modify** its base to include a hidden storage compartment.

Notice how the techniques build on each other. Brainstorming gave the directions, TRIZ shaped them, and SCAMPER added features to them.

## Phase 3: Concept selection
<!-- bite: 5e53c764-1614-4256-9d82-97fde90f44e7 -->

The aim is finding the most balanced solution. The best method for this is the decision matrix. Here, each score is multiplied by the criterion's weight (the weighted score is in brackets).

| Criteria | Weight | Concept A | Concept B | Concept C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | 5 | 3 (15) | 4 (20) | 4 (20) |
| Ergonomics | 4 | 2 (8) | 3 (12) | 2 (8) |
| Sustainability | 3 | 2 (6) | 2 (6) | 3 (9) |
| Size | 2 | 3 (6) | 3 (6) | 2 (4) |
| **Total score** |  | **35** | **44** | **41** |

The weights come straight from the problem definition: cost has the biggest weight because of the ₦20,000 constraint, and ergonomics next because reducing strain is the objective.

## Why Concept B
<!-- bite: e7d64638-9b00-46b5-84fb-9d9c456a66c6 -->

Concept B has the highest total (44), so Concept B is chosen, due to scoring best on the two criteria that matter most: cost (weight 5) and ergonomics (weight 4).

Check the working for B: 5(4) + 4(3) + 3(2) + 2(3) = 20 + 12 + 6 + 6 = **44**.

Concept C came close (41) because it's the most sustainable, but sustainability carries less weight than ergonomics, and the whole point of the design is less neck strain.

We have our concept. Next it gets refined, detailed, built and tested, in part 2.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 6ceab808-019f-49e9-ba40-7f5ec29ea157 -->

1. **Weights that don't match the problem.** If the objective is reducing strain, ergonomics can't have the lowest weight. The weights should come from the problem definition.
2. **Concepts that are all the same idea.** Three sizes of laptop stand isn't concept generation. Aim for different directions.
3. **Choosing without saying why.** "B wins" isn't enough in an exam. Say which criteria made it win.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 84903b69-ef0f-4d6f-a7be-c7b829bb9bcf -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="6e3a0f84" -->
   In the study desk problem, "should cost less than ₦20,000 to manufacture" is:

   - [ ] A requirement
   - [x] A constraint
   - [ ] A criterion for success

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A constraint: it limits the design choices.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="b07d2c59" -->
   A designer makes a phone stand whose holder slides along a rail so the user can move it to suit what they're doing. Which TRIZ principle is this?

   - [ ] Segmentation
   - [x] Dynamicity
   - [ ] Universality

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Dynamicity: making the object flexible and adaptable, like the magnets and sliding rails on the study desk organiser.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: exact id="2f9c8e17" answer="47" -->
   A decision matrix for a classroom chair has these weights: Comfort 5, Cost 4, Durability 3. Concept P scores 4 on comfort, 3 on cost and 5 on durability. What is Concept P's total score?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   5(4) + 4(3) + 3(5) = 20 + 12 + 15 = 47.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="c4a81d36" -->
   A decision matrix for a solar lamp gives Concept X = 52, Concept Y = 49, Concept Z = 55. Which concept is chosen?

   - [ ] X
   - [ ] Y
   - [x] Z

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Z, the highest total. In an exam, also say which high-weight criteria made it win.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="8d5f1b02" -->
   Write a problem definition (objective, two requirements, one constraint, one measurable criterion for success) for a shoe rack for a student hostel room.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For example. Objective: keep a student's shoes organised and off the floor in a small room. Requirements: must hold at least 6 pairs of shoes; must fit in a space 60 cm wide. Constraint: must cost under ₦10,000 to make. Criterion for success: in a survey of 20 students, at least 15 say their floor is tidier after two weeks. Any answer works if the criterion is measurable.

   </details>
