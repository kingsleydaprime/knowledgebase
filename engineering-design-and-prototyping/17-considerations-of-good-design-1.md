# Considerations of Good Design 1: Performance and Functional Issues

Design considerations are the things a good design must get right, whatever the product is, and the first group is performance and functional issues.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: b40f36d8-a201-44aa-9a4b-3aff4deec795 -->

You should know the phases of the design process ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/02-the-design-process|lesson 02]]) and what a requirement is ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/06-requirements-and-requirement-writing|lesson 06]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. name the three broad groups of design considerations;
2. list and explain the seven performance requirements;
3. tell primary from complementary functional performance, using a screw jack.

## What design considerations are
<!-- bite: 14a00a87-b3bb-4a66-ae59-3c770abf6239 -->

The earlier lessons were about the process. This one is about what a good design must get right, whatever the product is.

Design considerations differ from problem to problem, but they fall into three broad groups:

1. Performance and functional issues
2. Total life cycle issues
3. Regulatory and social issues

This lesson is group 1. Lessons 18 and 19 are groups 2 and 3.

## A. Performance and functional issues
<!-- bite: 8c8eefe9-143b-4c2a-8398-73459bd7aec8 -->

This group is about the achievement of performance requirements. Performance requirements include seven design considerations:

1. Functional performance
2. Behaviour performance
3. Environmental performance
4. Aesthetic performance
5. Human factors engineering (HFE)
6. Manufacturing technology
7. Cost and economy

We'll carry one example through all seven: a **screw jack**, the tool you put under a car to lift it when changing a tyre.

## 1. Functional performance requirement
<!-- bite: 4f495007-5aa9-498a-905f-a65a8a8d7e6d -->

A major and most important design consideration is its function. The functional requirement asks: "Is the system operational for what it was originated for?" It is divided into:

- **Primary functional performance**: the main job the design must do.
- **Complementary functional performance**: the supporting qualities around that job.

Example: a screw jack designed to lift a car of up to 300 kg.

- The **primary function** is to lift the required 300 kg load up to a height of 18 inches. So the measures are the load and the height.
- The **complementary performance** includes useful life, reliability, robustness in service conditions, less muscle power to lift, and less fatigue for the operator.

## What functional requirements depend on
<!-- bite: a18ef922-880a-4ef5-8e6d-16618f464a73 -->

Functional requirements depend on factors like:

1. Form, shape and size.
2. Surface finish.
3. Material and their properties.
4. Manufacturing processes involved.
5. Features (specific physical attributes).

For the screw jack: the thread's shape and size decide how much force you need; the material decides whether it can hold 300 kg without bending; the surface finish of the thread decides how smoothly it turns.

## 2. Behaviour performance requirements
<!-- bite: a3682f88-3d3a-40dd-91be-7b557ead0405 -->

Function asks *can it do the job?* Behaviour asks *how well does it perform under real conditions?*

For the screw jack:

- Ease of operation.
- Safety while operating.
- Noise level.
- Maintenance.

A jack that lifts 300 kg but slips halfway up meets the functional requirement and fails the behaviour one.

## 3. Environmental performance requirements
<!-- bite: ad7960af-e980-479a-9365-fa0d43fb1a26 -->

These are as important as economic considerations. There are two aspects:

- **Green design**: how the product will behave with respect to maintaining a safe, clean and healthy environment. It started because of government regulations but over time became standard design practice.
- **Service conditions**: the conditions and real situation under which the product must operate, such as temperature limits, humidity, corrosive conditions, dirt, dust, vibration and noise. They must be predicted and considered in the design.

A screw jack lives in a car boot and gets used on dusty, wet roadsides. If the designer forgets that, it rusts and jams the first time you need it.

## 4. Aesthetic performance requirements
<!-- bite: 924be977-7e4a-4ecd-abb3-89f5d9ae993f -->

Aesthetics is the sense of beauty: how the product appears to a customer. It involves shape, colour, surface quality and visual appearance.

It is the responsibility of the **industrial designer** (an applied artist), not the engineering designer, but it should be part of the initial design concept. You can't bolt good looks on at the end.

## 5. Human factors engineering (HFE) requirements
<!-- bite: fb3b71ca-483f-4a35-bb92-28a42649d463 -->

HFE ensures that the design can be operated efficiently by humans. It involves:

1. Ergonomics and biomechanics.
2. Engineering psychology.
3. Physiological and anthropometric data (measurements of the human body: hand size, arm reach, height).
4. Human muscle power and response times.

For the screw jack: the handle must be long enough that a person can turn it without straining, and the grip must fit a hand. That's where "less muscle power" and "less fatigue" from the functional requirement come from.

## 6. Manufacturing technology
<!-- bite: 84eebb68-4957-4cbe-bcdc-745573425217 -->

Manufacturing must be considered at the initial level, and closely integrated with product design. Its limitations include:

1. The manufacturing processes that can be used.
2. Material availability.
3. Equipment availability within the company.

A brilliant jack design that needs a machine the factory doesn't have is not a design that will be made.

## 7. Cost and economy
<!-- bite: 415935d4-41fd-4b27-a50f-2811f1542e7f -->

Cost is a key design requirement: every design must be economic, and projects can be rejected because of unfavourable initial cost estimates.

Total cost may include:

- Development cost.
- Initial product cost.
- Life cycle cost.
- Tooling cost.
- Infrastructure cost.
- Return on investment (ROI).

That's all seven. Notice "life cycle cost" at the end: a product's cost and its effect on the world don't stop when it leaves the factory. That's the next group of considerations.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 709c0782-0807-4568-bec8-1a350152cd12 -->

1. **Mixing up functional and behaviour performance.** Functional: can it do the job (lift 300 kg to 18 inches)? Behaviour: how well under real conditions (easy, safe, quiet, low maintenance)?
2. **Thinking aesthetics is the engineer's job.** It belongs to the industrial designer, but it must be in the initial concept.
3. **Forgetting service conditions.** Dust, rain and heat must be predicted at the design stage, not discovered by the user.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 23c00572-84cb-4c47-8e1c-9b4d5e86d742 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: exact id="8c4d1e2f" answer="7" accept="seven" -->
   How many design considerations do the performance requirements include?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Seven: functional, behaviour, environmental, aesthetic, human factors engineering, manufacturing technology, and cost and economy.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="3b0f9a64" -->
   A ceiling fan must move 200 cubic metres of air per minute. Is that its primary or complementary functional performance?

   - [x] Primary
   - [ ] Complementary

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Primary: moving air is the main job of the fan. Complementary performance would be things like useful life, reliability and quiet running.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="e6a28c07" -->
   A garden tool must work in temperatures from 0 °C to 45 °C and survive rain and mud. Which performance requirement is this?

   - [ ] Behaviour performance
   - [x] Environmental performance (service conditions)
   - [ ] Aesthetic performance

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Environmental performance, specifically service conditions: the real situation the product must operate in.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="5d7b3f91" -->
   Who is responsible for aesthetic performance?

   - [ ] The engineering designer
   - [x] The industrial designer
   - [ ] The manufacturing engineer

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The industrial designer, an applied artist. But it should still be part of the initial design concept.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="a0e4c652" -->
   Which of these does human factors engineering involve? Choose all that apply.

   - [x] Ergonomics and biomechanics
   - [x] Anthropometric data
   - [x] Human muscle power and response times
   - [ ] Material availability

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The first three, plus engineering psychology. Material availability belongs to manufacturing technology.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="f9c17b2d" -->
   For a wheelbarrow designed to carry 100 kg of sand, give its primary functional performance and two complementary performance requirements.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Primary: carry a 100 kg load of sand over the ground. Complementary (any two): long useful life, reliability, robustness in service conditions (wet sand, rough ground), less muscle power to push, less fatigue for the operator.

   </details>
