# Technical Communication

Technical communication is getting a design across to the people who must approve, build, use or pay for it, in reports, drawings and presentations they can act on.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 6a7b6c1d-46fc-4189-b1fa-bc87c134b5fc -->

You should know professional practice ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/32-professional-practice|Professional Practice]]) and engineering drawings ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/21-engineering-drawings-and-dfma|21 — Engineering Drawings and DFMA]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. match the form and detail of a message to its audience;
2. lay out a design report section by section;
3. follow the rules for clear technical writing, figures and presentations.

## What technical communication is
<!-- bite: e6a99987-1782-47a7-bd3e-163da135c7d8 -->

A design that nobody understands doesn't get built. Engineers spend a large share of their time explaining: to managers who approve the money, to technicians who make the parts, to users who need instructions, and to examiners who mark the project.

**Technical communication** is presenting technical information so that a specific reader can understand it and act on it. This lesson uses the study desk project (lessons 34 and 35) as the example of something to report.

## Know your audience
<!-- bite: 7ca8e83a-9d59-4af4-a5f5-c58aba51c380 -->

The same design is explained differently to each reader:

| Audience | Wants to know | Best form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manager or client | Does it work, what does it cost, what do you recommend? | One-page summary, short presentation |
| Engineers | How it works and why, with evidence | Full report, calculations, models |
| Factory | Exactly what to make | Drawings, BOM, specifications |
| User | How to use it safely | Instructions, labels, diagrams |

Ask before writing: **who will read this, what do they already know, and what must they do afterwards?**

## The design report
<!-- bite: d3acf2a3-1c8f-415c-97dc-d7d39e66c526 -->

A design report usually follows the design process itself:

1. **Title page.**
2. **Summary (abstract or executive summary):** the problem, the solution, the key results and the recommendation, in half a page. Many readers read only this, so write it last.
3. **Introduction:** the problem, its background and the objectives.
4. **Requirements:** the specification, with the important requirements and their priorities.
5. **Concepts and selection:** the options considered and how one was chosen (decision matrix, Pugh matrix).
## The design report: from detailed design to appendices
<!-- bite: a7e9be98-8e58-4e26-b5e5-5eb8a5deb49b -->

The second half of the report covers the design itself, the evidence and the conclusions.

6. **Detailed design:** the chosen design, with drawings, calculations and materials.
7. **Prototype and testing:** what was built, how it was tested, and the results against the requirements.
8. **Conclusions and recommendations:** whether the objectives were met, and what to do next.
9. **References.**
10. **Appendices:** full calculations, data and extra drawings that would interrupt the main text.

## Writing clearly
<!-- bite: 5f6ff90b-56f0-41de-be0b-07b98439ec00 -->

1. **Lead with the point.** Start each section with its conclusion, then the support.
2. **One idea per paragraph.**
3. **Numbers with units and sources:** "the frame weighs 1.8 kg (measured)", not "the frame is light".
4. **Plain words and short sentences.** Define each technical term the first time.
5. **Be honest about limits:** say what wasn't tested and what failed.
6. **Cite your sources,** so the reader can check them.

## Figures and tables
<!-- bite: 36106336-780e-441a-b98a-c344a4d0b5b2 -->

1. **Number and caption every figure and table,** and refer to each in the text: "Figure 3 shows...".
2. **Choose the right form:** a table for exact values, a line graph for a trend over time, a bar chart for comparing options, a drawing or sketch for shape, a flowchart for a process.
3. **Label axes with the quantity and the unit.**
4. **One message per figure.** If you need two, draw two.

## Presentations and design reviews
<!-- bite: c38c7a1b-5edd-4a21-be0c-e51483cee028 -->

1. **One main message per slide,** stated in its title: "Concept B scored highest", not "Results".
2. **Show evidence,** such as a chart or photo of the prototype, rather than paragraphs of text.
3. **Tell it in order:** problem, what you did, what you found, what you recommend.
4. **Keep to time,** and rehearse it aloud.
5. **Expect questions,** and answer honestly: "we didn't test that" beats a guess.

In a design review (lesson 32), the presentation is followed by questions. Record the actions and decisions in the minutes.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: adee1966-c11f-4cf0-aac4-b2b2dd4d037b -->

1. **Writing for yourself.** Write for the reader's knowledge and needs.
2. **Burying the conclusion** at the end of a long report.
3. **Numbers without units,** and figures nobody refers to.
4. **Slides full of text** that the presenter reads aloud.

That completes the topics. The last part puts the whole design process together on one real problem: a study desk, built step by step.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: c2601dac-b2e4-4226-bcb4-7e32e69449db -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="00159fba" -->
   A busy managing director needs to decide whether to fund a new product. What should you give them first?

   - [ ] The full 60-page report with all calculations
   - [x] A one-page executive summary with the recommendation, cost and key results
   - [ ] The engineering drawings
   - [ ] The test procedure

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The executive summary. A manager needs the decision, the cost and the evidence in brief; the detail is there if they want it.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: open id="5d759780" -->
   Put these design report sections in order: conclusions and recommendations, requirements, introduction, prototype and testing, concepts and selection, detailed design.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Introduction, requirements, concepts and selection, detailed design, prototype and testing, conclusions and recommendations. The report follows the design process.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="b882a367" -->
   You want to show how three concepts scored in a decision matrix. Which figure suits it best?

   - [ ] A line graph
   - [x] A bar chart
   - [ ] A flowchart
   - [ ] A pie chart of the costs

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A bar chart compares values across options. A line graph suits a trend over time.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: open id="0884d0d0" -->
   Rewrite this sentence from a test report so it is clearer and more useful: "The prototype performed quite well under load and was fairly stiff."

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For example: "Under a 400 N load the prototype deflected 2.1 mm, within the 3 mm limit in requirement R5, so it passed." It gives the load, the measured result with units, the requirement it is checked against, and the verdict.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: mcq id="400cb82b" -->
   Which slide title follows the advice for presentations?

   - [ ] Results
   - [ ] Slide 7
   - [x] The lighter frame cut cost by 18%
   - [ ] Some findings from testing

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A title that states the slide's one message, so the audience knows the point before reading the detail.

   </details>

6. <!-- q: open id="13a405f4" -->
   Why is the summary of a report written last, though it comes first?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   It summarises the whole report, including the results and the recommendation, which aren't known until the work and the rest of the report are done. It comes first because many readers read only the summary.

   </details>
