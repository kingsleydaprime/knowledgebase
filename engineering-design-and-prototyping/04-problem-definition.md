# Problem Definition

Problem definition is the first phase of engineering design: understanding the problem before you try to solve it.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 15ed1d7f-5732-466c-9e72-729a00d3f1ac -->

You should know the seven phases of the design process ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/02-the-design-process|lesson 02]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. name the four things problem definition involves, and tell requirements, constraints and criteria for success apart;
2. explain four methods for problem definition;
3. write a problem definition for a simple product.

## What problem definition is
<!-- bite: 39e8d7a2-a6e6-4f48-bd16-03220b2a345b -->

Problem definition and concept generation are the first two steps of engineering design: first you understand the problem (problem definition), then you come up with possible answers (concept generation).

A solution can only be as good as the problem it was built for, so we start there. If you design the perfect answer to the wrong question, you've still failed.

Problem definition involves four things: objectives, requirements, constraints and criteria for success. Let's take them one at a time.

## The four things problem definition involves
<!-- bite: 6c7764b1-d268-4b07-9100-88ad23ba5e13 -->

1. **Objectives**: What are you trying to achieve? What are the desired outcomes of the design?
2. **Requirements**: What are the essential features and functions? What are the limitations and constraints?
3. **Constraints**: Factors that limit your design choices. They could be technical, economic, environmental or social.
4. **Criteria for success**: The metrics that will be used to measure success.

Requirements say what the design must do; constraints say what limits how you can do it; criteria for success say how you will know it worked.

## Example: a student backpack
<!-- bite: 34f6569f-b2d8-4ab5-b93d-f5d8d5591e5e -->

Let's use one example the whole way. Students complain that their bags hurt their backs and everything inside gets mixed up.

- **Objective:** a backpack that is comfortable to carry all day and keeps things organised.
- **Requirements:** must hold a laptop and at least 5 textbooks; must have a separate pocket for a water bottle.
- **Constraints:** must cost less than ₦25,000 to make (economic); must be made with sewing machines the factory already has (technical).
- **Criteria for success:** in a survey after two weeks, at least 80% of students say it is more comfortable than their old bag.

Notice the criteria for success are numbers. "Students like it" is not something you can measure; "80% say it is more comfortable" is.

## Methods for problem definition
<!-- bite: f4768992-bb8b-4678-b2be-3da4998327e5 -->

How do you find all that out? You don't guess it. You use these four methods:

1. **User research**: Interviewing, observing and surveying potential users to understand their needs, wants and pain points.
2. **Benchmarking**: Studying existing products or solutions that address similar problems, to identify their strengths and weaknesses.
3. **Mind mapping**: A visual brainstorming technique that helps you organise your thoughts and identify key aspects of the problem.
4. **SCAMPER**: A creative problem-solving technique that involves asking questions to stimulate new ideas.

## The four methods on the backpack
<!-- bite: 1e87bdaa-70bb-46e4-be18-cc4c48849877 -->

Here's how each method would feed the backpack problem:

- **User research:** you watch students at the school gate and see them carrying bags on one shoulder. You ask why, and they say the straps dig in. That's a pain point.
- **Benchmarking:** you study hiking backpacks, which carry heavy loads comfortably, and cheap school bags, which are light but tear. Each has strengths and weaknesses to learn from.
- **Mind mapping:** you write "backpack" in the middle of a page and branch out: comfort, storage, price, weather, safety. Each branch shows a key aspect of the problem.
- **SCAMPER:** you ask questions about the existing bag. What can be eliminated? What can be combined? (Lesson 07 covers it fully.)

Once the problem is clear, you can start producing answers. But first, requirements deserve a closer look, because writing them well decides whether the rest of the design goes well. That's the next lesson.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 5116adeb-c3e9-4f87-a7d4-91b64a449451 -->

1. **Mixing up requirements and constraints.** A requirement is what the design must do ("hold 5 textbooks"). A constraint limits your choices ("cost under ₦25,000").
2. **Criteria for success you can't measure.** "It should be good" can't be checked. Give a metric.
3. **Jumping straight to a solution.** "We need a bag with wheels" is already a concept. Problem definition describes the need, not the answer.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 231b36b1-4a63-4070-b829-7c3e38ae3bb7 -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="2be7c40d" -->
   A team designing a water filter writes: "The filter must cost less than ₦5,000 to produce." What is this?

   - [ ] An objective
   - [x] A constraint
   - [ ] A criterion for success

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A constraint, an economic one. It limits the design choices (which materials and processes you can afford).

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="94d0a7e2" -->
   For the same water filter: "In field tests, 95% of samples must meet the drinking water standard." What is this?

   - [ ] A requirement
   - [ ] A constraint
   - [x] A criterion for success

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A criterion for success: a metric used to measure whether the design worked.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: mcq id="5e10fb83" -->
   An engineer studies three phone chargers already on the market to see what each does well and badly. Which method is this?

   - [ ] User research
   - [x] Benchmarking
   - [ ] Mind mapping

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Benchmarking: studying existing products that solve similar problems to identify their strengths and weaknesses.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: gap id="c8f3a619" answers="technical;economic;environmental;social" -->
   Fill in the gaps. Constraints could be ___, ___, ___ or ___.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Technical, economic, environmental or social (any order).

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="7a4e25dc" -->
   Write a problem definition (objective, one requirement, one constraint, one criterion for success) for a lunchbox that keeps food warm for a primary school pupil.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   One possible answer. Objective: keep a pupil's lunch warm until lunchtime. Requirement: must keep food above 50 °C for 4 hours. Constraint: must weigh under 500 g so a small child can carry it. Criterion for success: in testing, at least 9 out of 10 lunches are still above 50 °C after 4 hours. Any answer works if the requirement says what it must do, the constraint limits choices, and the criterion is measurable.

   </details>
