# Considerations of Good Design 3: Regulatory and Social Issues

Regulatory and social issues are the rules and the society side of design: the standards, ethics, safety laws and human behaviour every design has to answer to.

## Before you start
<!-- bite: 6b4b447c-7f78-4daf-82ef-43e04117abb5 -->

You should know the first two groups of design considerations ([[engineering-design-and-prototyping/13-considerations-of-good-design-1|lesson 13]] and [[engineering-design-and-prototyping/14-considerations-of-good-design-2|lesson 14]]).

After this lesson you will be able to:

1. list the five regulatory and social issues;
2. explain what standards are and who sets them;
3. explain why the behaviour of the end-user matters more than the materials.

## What regulatory and social issues are
<!-- bite: 79b9edf9-013e-4d61-b4c7-479e8c1e92cd -->

A design can work perfectly and last for years and still not be allowed on the market, or still do harm because of how people use it. That's this third group.

Regulatory and social issues include:

1. Specifications and standards.
2. Codes of ethics.
3. Public health and safety.
4. Human factor engineering.
5. Behaviour of the end-user.

## 1. Specifications and standards
<!-- bite: d3309d72-0467-4951-b647-c37870e79eb3 -->

A good and successful design must conform to the specifications and standards. Societies like ASME, ASTM and IEEE provide design standards.

Standards are a voluntary agreement among users (consumers) and producers (industrialists), and they often represent only the minimum. A company can set its own standards if it needs more.

A good design must conform to all legal requirements and design codes.

Why does this matter? Because a standard means a bolt from one factory fits a nut from another, and a "10 A" plug means the same thing everywhere. Without standards, every product would be a stranger to every other one.

## 2. Codes of ethics
<!-- bite: 4222418e-b7d9-4ef7-b408-d1f32e407223 -->

Codes of ethics require the engineer to protect public health and safety.

That means the engineer's first duty is to the public, not to the boss or the client. If a design is unsafe, the engineer must say so, even when it's inconvenient.

## 3. Public health and safety
<!-- bite: 3c6c21f9-ea28-4fbb-b99a-2fe963039e5c -->

Agencies put direct constraints on the designer in the interests of protecting security, health and safety. Examples:

- Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA).
- Environmental Protection Agency (EPA).
- Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC).
- Department of Homeland Security (DHS).

These are American agencies, because the lecture material follows American textbooks. In Nigeria, bodies like the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) do similar work.

If these aspects cannot be covered by the functional design, then warnings must always be attached to the product, and user manuals must ensure safe operating procedures and conditions. That's why a hair dryer says "do not use in the bath".

## 4. Human factor engineering
<!-- bite: a595ba2d-e1c5-4b08-ab1d-7e809c6bef79 -->

A good design must ensure it can be operated efficiently and safely by human beings, using the knowledge of ergonomics, biomechanics and engineering psychology.

You met this in lesson 13 as a performance requirement. Here it comes back as a social issue, because a design that hurts people or confuses them into mistakes is a problem for society, not just for the product.

## 5. Behaviour of the end-user
<!-- bite: ad9f7c8c-fa53-44a4-bf93-c471ae76b4e7 -->

This one is important. Focusing only on materials deals with about 5% of the problem; the other 95% depends on the behaviour of the end-user.

Example: for a hair dryer, about 95% of the carbon footprint comes from the electricity the user spends drying hair over the years, and only about 5% from the materials and manufacturing stage.

So a designer who wants a greener hair dryer gets more from making it dry hair faster on less power than from switching to a greener plastic.

That finishes the considerations of good design. With them in mind, the concept can now be detailed: drawn up, made manufacturable, and given its materials. That's the next two lessons.

## Common pitfalls
<!-- bite: 854ac340-831b-4737-942d-de20ad9b54aa -->

1. **Thinking standards are the best you can do.** They often represent only the minimum. A company can set higher ones.
2. **Thinking standards are laws.** Standards are a voluntary agreement. Legal requirements and design codes are separate, and a good design meets both.
3. **Focusing only on materials for sustainability.** About 95% of the impact can come from how the product is used.

## Check your understanding
<!-- bite: 576676f1-c8da-40db-aac3-0188512b713f -->

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. <!-- q: mcq id="1c8a5e7b" -->
   Which of these provide design standards? Choose all that apply.

   - [x] ASME
   - [x] ASTM
   - [x] IEEE
   - [ ] OSHA

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   ASME, ASTM and IEEE are societies that provide design standards. OSHA is a safety agency that puts constraints on designers.

   </details>

2. <!-- q: mcq id="e9d42b06" -->
   Standards are best described as:

   - [ ] Laws passed by the government
   - [x] A voluntary agreement among users and producers, often representing only the minimum
   - [ ] The highest quality a product can reach

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   A voluntary agreement among users (consumers) and producers (industrialists). They often represent only the minimum.

   </details>

3. <!-- q: exact id="6f1b0d83" answer="95" accept="95%" -->
   For a hair dryer, roughly what percentage of its carbon footprint comes from the electricity the user spends using it over the years?

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   About 95%. Only about 5% comes from materials and manufacturing.

   </details>

4. <!-- q: mcq id="a37c9f52" -->
   A power drill can't be made fully safe by its design alone, because the bit spins fast. What must the designer do?

   - [ ] Nothing, since the design is as safe as it can be
   - [x] Attach warnings and give a user manual with safe operating procedures
   - [ ] Stop the product from being sold

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   When safety can't be covered by the functional design, warnings must be attached and the user manual must ensure safe operating procedures and conditions.

   </details>

5. <!-- q: open id="0b5e8c14" -->
   A team wants to cut the environmental impact of an electric kettle. Using the behaviour of the end-user, explain where they should focus.

   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Most of a kettle's impact, like the hair dryer's, comes from the electricity used over its life, not from its materials. So they should focus on use: for example a better-insulated body, a gauge that helps users boil only the water they need, and an automatic switch-off. Changing the plastic only touches the small materials share.

   </details>
