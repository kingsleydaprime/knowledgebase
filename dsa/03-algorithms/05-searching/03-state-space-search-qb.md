# State-Space Search — Question Bank

Micro-questions over [[03-state-space-search|the state-space search module]]. Answer in a full sentence before opening the toggle. Format explained in [[dsa/02-data-structures/01-arrays-qb|the arrays bank]].

---

## A. The collection that does not exist

**1. How does "search" here differ from searching a sorted array?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The array existed — you could print it.** Here there is no list of board positions: only a starting state, a rule for what is one move away, and a test for being finished.

</details>

**2. How large are the 8-puzzle and 15-puzzle state spaces?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

181,440 reachable configurations and about $10^{13}$ respectively. **Nobody is building that list.**

</details>

**3. State the key reframing.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The data structure being searched is generated by the search itself** — an implicit graph with vertices and edges and all the usual properties, existing only as a function.

</details>

**4. What follows once you see it that way?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The algorithms you already know turn out to be the complete answer** — and the only thing separating them is what container holds the states waiting to be looked at.

</details>

## B. The vocabulary

**5. What is a state, and what property must it have?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A complete description of one configuration, containing everything needed to decide what happens next. **It must be hashable** if you want duplicate detection — hence tuples rather than lists.

</details>

**6. What is the successor function?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Also the transition model or move generator — the function returning every state reachable in one step. **It is the edge list of the implicit graph, computed on demand.**

</details>

**7. What is the goal test, and what is the subtlety?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A predicate deciding whether a state is a solution. **It is a *test*, not a known state** — "checkmate" describes a huge family of boards, not one board.

</details>

**8. What is the frontier, and why is it the most useful fact in the lesson?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The states discovered but not yet expanded. **Which container you use for it decides which algorithm you are running.**

</details>

**9. What is the explored set?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Also closed/visited — the states already expanded, kept so you never process one twice.

</details>

**10. Tree search versus graph search?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Tree search has no explored set** — every route to a state is a separate discovery. **Graph search has one**, so each state is expanded at most once.

</details>

**11. What are $b$ and $d$, and what do they give together?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Branching factor (average successors — 2–4 for the 8-puzzle, ~35 for chess) and solution depth. Together they give the size of the search: **roughly $b^d$ nodes**.

</details>

**12. Define complete and optimal.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Complete**: guaranteed to find a solution if one exists. **Optimal**: guaranteed to find the *cheapest* one. DFS on an infinite space is not complete, and is not optimal for anything.

</details>

## C. The one function

**13. What varies between the five algorithms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Only `frontier.pop()`.** The explored set, goal test, successor expansion and reconstruction are identical.

</details>

**14. Give the frontier → algorithm table.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

| Frontier | Pops | Algorithm | Optimal? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| stack (LIFO) | most recent | DFS | no |
| queue (FIFO) | earliest | BFS | yes, uniform costs |
| min-heap on $g$ | cheapest so far | uniform-cost / Dijkstra | yes, any costs |
| min-heap on $h$ | most promising | greedy best-first | **no** |
| min-heap on $g+h$ | best estimated total | A\* | yes, if $h$ admissible |

</details>

**15. DFS returned a 49,285-move solution to a puzzle three moves from solved. Is it broken?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No — it is doing exactly what it promises**, which is to find *a* solution. It found one by wandering most of the state space. **That gap between "a solution" and "the solution" is what the whole table is about.**

</details>

## D. Uninformed versus informed

**16. Which algorithms are uninformed, and what does that mean?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

DFS, BFS and uniform-cost — they use **nothing but the structure of the space**. A\* and greedy are informed: they use a heuristic estimate of distance remaining.

</details>

**17. On the hardest 8-puzzle (31 moves), why did BFS expand 181,439 states?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The entire reachable space.** To prove a 31-move solution is shortest, it had to rule out every shorter one.

</details>

**18. What did A\* achieve on the same instance?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The same optimal answer from **20,291 expansions — a ninth of the work. The heuristic is the entire difference.**

</details>

**19. What did greedy best-first achieve?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

100 expansions and a 47-move path — **200× faster and 52% worse. That is the trade when you stop counting the cost already paid.**

</details>

**20. Compare zero, misplaced-tiles and Manhattan heuristics.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

All optimal (all admissible); expansions 181,439 / 143,840 / 20,291. **Manhattan does 7× less work than misplaced-tiles, which barely beats nothing.**

</details>

**21. Why does Manhattan dominate misplaced-tiles?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

For every state its estimate is at least as large — a tile out of place contributes at least 1 to both, usually more to Manhattan. **A larger admissible heuristic is closer to the truth, prunes more, and never expands more nodes.**

</details>

**22. State the design goal for a heuristic.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Get $h$ as large as you can without ever exceeding the true remaining cost.**

</details>

**23. Where do both heuristics come from?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The standard recipe — solve a relaxed version exactly.** Misplaced-tiles solves "a tile can teleport anywhere in one move"; Manhattan solves "tiles can pass through each other". Both relaxations are easier, so their answers can never exceed the real one.

</details>

## E. Tree versus graph search

**24. The 8-puzzle space is finite, so tree search must terminate. How much extra does dropping the explored set cost?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**1.2× at depth 2, 60× at depth 10, and still climbing.**

</details>

**25. Why, exactly?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The state space is finite, but the search *tree* is not.** Slide a tile left then back: tree search sees a brand-new node two levels deeper. Every round trip spawns a whole new subtree, and round trips grow exponentially with depth.

</details>

**26. State the bounds.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Graph search is bounded by the number of states, 181,440. Tree search is bounded by nothing.** With a stack instead of a queue it does not terminate at all.

</details>

**27. Give the practical rule and its exceptions.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**If a state can be reached by more than one route — almost always — you need an explored set.** Exceptions: genuine trees (backtracking over decision sequences) and cases where memory forbids it.

</details>

## F. Iterative deepening

**28. What problem does it solve, and how?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Storing every state is $O(b^d)$ memory, **and memory usually runs out before time does**. IDS runs depth-limited DFS with the limit rising from 0.

</details>

**29. Why is the re-exploration not as wasteful as it sounds?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

With branching factor $b$, the last level holds a $\frac{b-1}{b}$ fraction of all nodes, so everything above it adds only a constant factor — **about 11% extra for $b = 10$**.

</details>

**30. What is the trade?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Memory drops from $O(b^d)$ to $O(bd)$, **and you still get BFS's optimal answer**.

</details>

**31. What is IDA\*?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The same trick applied to A\* — **bounding on $f$ instead of depth**.

</details>

## G. Tie-breaking

**32. Why do plateaus happen, and what do they cost?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Many states share the same priority, so **the comparison is a coin flip and the search has no reason to prefer any of them**. How you break the tie decides whether the search drives toward the goal or spreads sideways.

</details>

**33. Give the three standard rules.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Prefer larger $g$** among equal $f$ — equivalently smaller $h$.
2. **Prefer the more recently discovered state** — depth-first-ish within a plateau.
3. **Add a tiny multiple of $h$** (scale by $1+\varepsilon$) — biases toward the goal at bounded optimality cost.

</details>

## H. Modelling

**34. What is the real work, and what is an interviewer usually testing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Turning a vague problem into states and successors.** The algorithms are a table lookup.

</details>

**35. Give the five modelling questions.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **What is a state?** The minimum information determining what happens next.
2. **What are the moves?** One deterministic, complete successor function.
3. **What is the goal test?** Often a property rather than a specific state.
4. **What does a move cost?** Equal → BFS; varying → uniform-cost; estimable remaining → A\*.
5. **Is the space finite?** If not, DFS is off the table.

</details>

**36. What goes wrong if the state carries too much? Too little?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Too much:** identical situations look different, defeating duplicate detection. **Too little:** the successor function cannot do its job.

</details>

**37. Model the word ladder and regex matching.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Word ladder:** state = current word; successors = words one letter away; goal = equals target.
**Regex matching:** state = (pattern index, text index); successors = consume a character or not; goal = both indices at the end.

</details>

**38. Why are dependency resolution and regex matching the ones worth noticing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Neither looks like a search problem until you name the state — and then the whole catalogue becomes available.**

</details>

## I. Traps

**39. What happens if you use an unhashable state?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A Python list cannot go in a set, so **duplicate detection silently becomes impossible**. Use tuples, frozensets, or a canonical string.

</details>

**40. Why must the state not carry a step counter or timestamp?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Two identical boards compare unequal and the explored set stops working.** The state is the minimum that determines the future.

</details>

**41. When is goal-testing on push safe, and when is it a bug?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Safe** for BFS with uniform costs. **A bug** for uniform-cost and A\*: the first *discovery* may be via an expensive route, while the first *expansion* is guaranteed cheapest.

</details>

**42. What is the solvability trap?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Half of all 8-puzzle configurations are unreachable from the goal** — the permutation parity is invariant under legal moves. A search for one explores all 181,440 states and correctly reports failure, after doing the maximum possible work. **Check solvability first when the problem has such an invariant.**

</details>

**43. Summarise the lesson in one sentence.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Name the state and the successor function and the problem becomes a graph you never build — after which the only decisions left are what the frontier is and how good your heuristic is.

</details>
